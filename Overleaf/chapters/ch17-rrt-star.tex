\section{Why this matters for a drone swarm}
\label{sec:ch17-motivation}

The rapidly-exploring random tree of \cref{ch:ch16} solves a problem that
grid search cannot touch: it finds a collision-free route through a
continuous 3D volume with a few thousand samples, whatever the resolution a
grid would have needed. But look at the route it returns. It is a chain of
short, randomly oriented segments, it wanders, and on the map of
\cref{ch:ch16} it comes out somewhere between $20$ and $27$ units long when
the shortest route has length about $16.9$ (\cref{ch:ch16}). Smoothing helps, but it can only pull
the path taut inside the corridor the tree happened to find. Worse, more
samples do not help at all: the parent of every vertex is fixed at the moment
the vertex is created, so the first path that reaches the goal is, up to the
random order of insertion, the path you keep. A drone that flies a route
thirty percent longer than necessary spends thirty percent more battery on
every mission, and a swarm of them spends it thirty times over.

\rrtstar, introduced by Karaman and Frazzoli in 2011
\cite{karaman2011sampling}, repairs this with two extra steps per
iteration: the new vertex takes as its parent the neighbour that gives it
the cheapest route from the start (\textsc{ChooseParent}), and it then
becomes the parent of any neighbour whose route it shortens
(\textsc{Rewire}). The tree keeps re-optimising itself, its best path
converges to the optimum as samples accumulate---\rrtstar is
\emph{asymptotically optimal}---and the price is a logarithmic number of
extra collision checks per iteration. \irrtstar
\cite{gammell2014informed} then adds the observation that, once a path of
cost $c_{\mathrm{best}}$ is known, every point that could improve it lies
inside an ellipsoid with the start and the goal as foci, and that sampling
this ellipsoid directly makes the convergence much faster in large free
spaces.

In the hybrid architecture of \cref{ch:ch24} these planners live in two
places. The global layer plans the swarm on a grid with conflict-based search
(\cbs) or its bounded-suboptimal variant \ecbs (\cref{ch:ch09,ch:ch10}); when a drone must leave that grid, because it
flies through a 3D volume too large or too finely structured to discretise,
\rrtstar plans the continuous segment, and it does so in \emph{anytime}
fashion: the first path is available after a few hundred samples, and the
tree keeps improving it while the drone is already flying. The replanning
layer can seed \irrtstar with the grid path it already has, so that the
sampler concentrates on the ellipsoid around that path from the first
iteration. This is the Week~8 topic of the study plan (\cref{ch:appA}):
its milestone is that you can choose between graph search and
sampling-based planning, and its coding exercise is \cref{exr:ch17-coding}.

The chapter proceeds as follows. \Cref{sec:ch17-intuition,sec:ch17-problem}
give the idea and the definitions; \cref{sec:ch17-algorithm} the three new
primitives, the pseudocode, the connection radius, its $k$-nearest
alternative and one iteration by hand; \cref{sec:ch17-example} the worked
example against \rrt and grid \astar; \cref{sec:ch17-properties} the
asymptotic-optimality theorem and the cost per iteration;
\cref{sec:ch17-informed} \irrtstar and \cref{sec:ch17-variants} the family
up to BIT\textsuperscript{*}; and
\cref{sec:ch17-implementation,sec:ch17-drone} the implementation, its
pitfalls and the place of both planners in the drone system.

\section{The idea in plain words}
\label{sec:ch17-intuition}

Think of the \rrt tree as a road network that is built one road at a time.
\rrt builds each new road from the nearest existing junction, whichever
junction that is. If the nearest junction happens to lie at the end of a
long detour, the new road inherits the detour, and so does every road built
from it later. Nothing in \rrt ever revisits this decision.

\rrtstar makes two changes, both local to the new vertex $x_{\mathrm{new}}$
and both restricted to a ball of radius $r_n$ around it
(\cref{fig:ch17-primitives}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Choose the parent.}\index{RRT*!ChooseParent} Connect
  $x_{\mathrm{new}}$ to the neighbour that gives it the cheapest
  collision-free route from the start, not merely to the nearest vertex.
  \item \textbf{Rewire the neighbours.}\index{RRT*!Rewire} Make
  $x_{\mathrm{new}}$ the parent of every neighbour whose route it shortens
  by a collision-free segment, and pass the saving on to that neighbour's
  descendants.
\end{itemize}
Because a vertex's parent can change long after the vertex was created,
the tree is no longer a record of the order in which space was explored; it
is an ever-improving \emph{shortest-path tree} over the sampled points, in
the same sense that Dijkstra's algorithm (\cref{ch:ch03}) maintains a
shortest-path tree over a graph. As samples accumulate, the sampled points
become dense, the shortest paths through them approach the shortest paths
through free space, and the cost of the best tree path to the goal
converges to the optimum.

The radius $r_n$ is the one delicate quantity: constant, it makes every
iteration do $\bigO{n}$ collision checks; shrinking too fast, it leaves the
balls empty and the tree stops improving. The schedule
$r_n\propto(\log n/n)^{1/d}$ shrinks as slowly as it can while keeping the
expected number of neighbours logarithmic (\cref{sec:ch17-radius}).

\begin{keyidea}
\rrtstar is \rrt with a shortest-path-tree discipline: every new vertex takes
the cheapest parent within radius $r_n$, and it becomes the parent of every
neighbour it can serve more cheaply. With $r_n\propto(\log n/n)^{1/d}$ and a
large enough constant, the best path converges to the optimum almost surely.
\irrtstar keeps the same tree but, once a path of cost $c_{\mathrm{best}}$ exists,
samples only the ellipsoid $\norm{x-x_{\mathrm{init}}}+\norm{x-x_{\mathrm{goal}}}\le c_{\mathrm{best}}$, the only region in which a better path can pass.
\end{keyidea}

\section{Problem statement and notation}
\label{sec:ch17-problem}

We keep the setting of \cref{ch:ch16}. The planning space is a box
$\mathcal{X}\subset\R^d$ with $d\in\{2,3\}$ (a robot position, or a
configuration after the obstacles have been inflated by the drone radius as
in \cref{ch:ch02}), $\Xobs$ is its obstacle region and
$\Xfree=\mathcal{X}\setminus\Xobs$ the free space. The start is
$x_{\mathrm{init}}\in\Xfree$ and the goal $x_{\mathrm{goal}}\in\Xfree$.
A path is a continuous map $\sigma:[0,1]\to\mathcal{X}$; it is
\textbf{collision-free}\index{path!collision-free} if
$\sigma(s)\in\Xfree$ for all $s$, and its cost $c(\sigma)$ is its Euclidean
length. Throughout, $\mu(\cdot)$ denotes volume (Lebesgue measure),
$\disc{x}{r}$ the closed ball of radius $r$ around $x$, and
$\zeta_d=\mu(\disc{0}{1})$ the volume of the unit ball: $\zeta_2=\pi$,
$\zeta_3=4\pi/3$.

\begin{definition}[Optimal planning problem]
\label{def:ch17-problem}
Given $(\Xfree,x_{\mathrm{init}},x_{\mathrm{goal}})$, find a collision-free path
$\sigma^*$ from $x_{\mathrm{init}}$ to $x_{\mathrm{goal}}$ with
$c(\sigma^*)=c^*:=\inf\set{c(\sigma):\sigma\text{ collision-free from }x_{\mathrm{init}}\text{ to }x_{\mathrm{goal}}}$.
We write $c_{\min}=\norm{x_{\mathrm{goal}}-x_{\mathrm{init}}}$ for the
straight-line distance; every path has $c(\sigma)\ge c_{\min}$.
\end{definition}

Sampling-based planners can only find paths that have some room around
them, so the theory needs a notion of clearance.

\begin{definition}[Clearance and robust optimum]
\label{def:ch17-clearance}
A path $\sigma$ has \textbf{strong $\delta$-clearance}\index{clearance!strong}
if $\disc{\sigma(s)}{\delta}\subseteq\Xfree$ for all $s\in[0,1]$. An optimal
path $\sigma^*$ is a \textbf{robustly optimal solution}\index{robust optimality}
if there are collision-free paths $\sigma_n$ with strong
$\delta_n$-clearance, $\delta_n>0$, such that $c(\sigma_n)\to c^*$.
\end{definition}

The optimum of the map in \cref{ch:ch16} hugs the corners of the two walls,
so it has no clearance itself; but pushing it away from the corners by any
$\delta>0$ costs only $\bigO{\delta}$, so it is robustly optimal. A route
through a gap of width exactly zero is the kind of optimum that is
\emph{not} robust: no path with positive clearance approximates it, and no
sampling-based planner will find it.

\begin{definition}[Tree, cost-to-come, best cost]
\label{def:ch17-tree}
The planner maintains a tree $T=(V,E)$ rooted at $x_{\mathrm{init}}$ with
$n=\abs{V}$ vertices; $\mathrm{parent}(v)$ is the parent of $v\ne
x_{\mathrm{init}}$. The \textbf{cost-to-come}\index{cost-to-come}
$\mathrm{Cost}(v)$ is the length of the tree path from $x_{\mathrm{init}}$ to
$v$, so $\mathrm{Cost}(v)=\mathrm{Cost}(\mathrm{parent}(v))+\norm{v-\mathrm{parent}(v)}$
and $\mathrm{Cost}(x_{\mathrm{init}})=0$. The symbol $n$ is reserved for
$\abs{V}$ and iterations are counted by $i$; the two differ, because a
blocked sample adds no vertex. The \textbf{best cost} after $i$
iterations is $c_{\mathrm{best}}=\mathrm{Cost}(x_{\mathrm{goal}})$ if
$x_{\mathrm{goal}}\in V$ and $c_{\mathrm{best}}=\infty$ otherwise.
\end{definition}

The primitives $\Nearest(V,x)$, $\Steer(x,y)$ (move from $x$ towards $y$ by
at most the step size $\eta$) and $\CollisionFree(x,y)$ (the straight
segment from $x$ to $y$ lies in $\Xfree$) are those of \cref{ch:ch16}.
\rrtstar adds one more.

\begin{definition}[Near and the connection radius]
\label{def:ch17-near}
$\Near(V,x,r)=\set{v\in V:\norm{v-x}\le r}$\index{RRT*!Near} is the set of
vertices within distance $r$ of $x$. \rrtstar calls it with the
\textbf{connection radius}\index{connection radius}
\begin{equation}
  r_n=\min\Bigl\{\gamma_{\rrtstar}\Bigl(\frac{\log n}{n}\Bigr)^{1/d},\ \eta\Bigr\},
  \qquad
  \gamma_{\rrtstar}>\gamma^*_{\rrtstar}:=2\Bigl(1+\frac1d\Bigr)^{1/d}\Bigl(\frac{\mu(\Xfree)}{\zeta_d}\Bigr)^{1/d},
  \label{eq:ch17-radius}
\end{equation}
where $n=\abs{V}$ is the current number of vertices, $d$ the dimension,
$\eta$ the step size of $\Steer$ and $\log$ the natural logarithm.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Asymptotic optimality]
\label{def:ch17-ao}
Let $Y_i$ be the cost of the best solution in the tree after $i$
iterations ($Y_i=\infty$ if there is none). A planner is
\textbf{asymptotically optimal}\index{asymptotic optimality} if, for every
problem with a robustly optimal solution of cost $c^*$,
$\Prob\bigl(\lim_{i\to\infty}Y_i=c^*\bigr)=1$.
\end{definition}

Finally, the set that \irrtstar samples.

\begin{definition}[Informed set]
\label{def:ch17-informed}
For a current best cost $c_{\mathrm{best}}\ge c_{\min}$, the
\textbf{informed set}\index{informed set} is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{X}_{\hat f}(c_{\mathrm{best}})=\set{x\in\mathcal{X}:\ \norm{x-x_{\mathrm{init}}}+\norm{x-x_{\mathrm{goal}}}\le c_{\mathrm{best}}}.
  \label{eq:ch17-informed-set}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\section{The algorithm}
\label{sec:ch17-algorithm}

\subsection{The three new primitives}
\label{sec:ch17-primitives}

An \rrtstar iteration begins exactly like an \rrt iteration: draw
$x_{\mathrm{rand}}$, find the nearest vertex
$x_{\mathrm{nearest}}$, steer to $x_{\mathrm{new}}$, and give up on the sample if the segment from
$x_{\mathrm{nearest}}$ to $x_{\mathrm{new}}$ is blocked. If it is free,
\rrt would insert the edge $(x_{\mathrm{nearest}},x_{\mathrm{new}})$ and
stop. \rrtstar instead runs three steps, illustrated in
\cref{fig:ch17-primitives} on the small tree that \cref{ex:ch17-hand} works
through in numbers.

\paragraph{Near.} $X_{\mathrm{near}}=\Near(V,x_{\mathrm{new}},r_n)$ collects
every vertex within the connection radius of the new point. It is the
candidate set for both of the following steps; $x_{\mathrm{nearest}}$ is
added to it if the radius happens to exclude it, so that a parent always
exists.

\paragraph{ChooseParent.} For every candidate $v\in X_{\mathrm{near}}$
compute the cost-to-come that $x_{\mathrm{new}}$ would have through $v$,
namely $\mathrm{Cost}(v)+\norm{x_{\mathrm{new}}-v}$, and connect
$x_{\mathrm{new}}$ to the candidate with the smallest value whose segment
$\CollisionFree(v,x_{\mathrm{new}})$ holds. 

\paragraph{Rewire.} For every candidate $v\in X_{\mathrm{near}}$ other than
the chosen parent, compute the cost $v$ would have through the new vertex,
$\mathrm{Cost}(x_{\mathrm{new}})+\norm{v-x_{\mathrm{new}}}$. If it is smaller
than $\mathrm{Cost}(v)$ and $\CollisionFree(x_{\mathrm{new}},v)$ holds,
replace the edge from $\mathrm{parent}(v)$ to $v$ by the edge from
$x_{\mathrm{new}}$ to $v$. The cost of $v$ drops, and so does the cost of
every descendant of $v$ by the same amount; \textsc{UpdateSubtree}
propagates the change through the subtree so that the invariant
$\mathrm{Cost}(u)=\mathrm{Cost}(\mathrm{parent}(u))+\norm{u-\mathrm{parent}(u)}$
holds again for every vertex.

\subsection{Pseudocode}
\label{sec:ch17-pseudocode}

\Cref{alg:ch17-rrtstar} is the main loop and \cref{alg:ch17-helpers} its
three helpers. The goal is handled as in \cref{ch:ch16} by goal
bias:\index{goal bias} with probability $p_{\mathrm{goal}}$ the sample is
$x_{\mathrm{goal}}$ itself, and since $\Steer$ returns the target when it is
within $\eta$, the goal point eventually becomes a vertex. From then on the
tree path to that vertex \emph{is} the current best path, its cost is
$c_{\mathrm{best}}$, and rewiring keeps shortening it; the bias is switched
off because the goal needs no second copy.

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
\caption{\rrtstar (Karaman and Frazzoli)}
\label{alg:ch17-rrtstar}
\KwIn{free space $\Xfree$ (via $\CollisionFree$), start $x_{\mathrm{init}}$, goal $x_{\mathrm{goal}}$, step $\eta$, constant $\gamma_{\rrtstar}$, goal bias $p_{\mathrm{goal}}$, budget $N$}
\KwOut{a tree $T$; the tree path to $x_{\mathrm{goal}}$ of cost $c_{\mathrm{best}}$, or \emph{failure}}
\Fn{\RrtStarPlan{$x_{\mathrm{init}}$, $x_{\mathrm{goal}}$, $N$}}{
  $V\gets\set{x_{\mathrm{init}}}$; $E\gets\emptyset$; $\mathrm{Cost}(x_{\mathrm{init}})\gets0$; $c_{\mathrm{best}}\gets\infty$\label{alg:ch17-rrtstar:init}\;
  \For{$i\gets1$ \KwTo $N$}{
    $x_{\mathrm{rand}}\gets x_{\mathrm{goal}}$ with probability $p_{\mathrm{goal}}$ if $x_{\mathrm{goal}}\notin V$, else a uniform sample of $\mathcal{X}$\label{alg:ch17-rrtstar:sample}\;
    $x_{\mathrm{nearest}}\gets\Nearest(V,x_{\mathrm{rand}})$; $x_{\mathrm{new}}\gets\Steer(x_{\mathrm{nearest}},x_{\mathrm{rand}})$\label{alg:ch17-rrtstar:steer}\;
    \If{$\CollisionFree(x_{\mathrm{nearest}},x_{\mathrm{new}})$\label{alg:ch17-rrtstar:free}}{
      $n\gets\abs{V}$; $r_n\gets\min\{\gamma_{\rrtstar}(\log n/n)^{1/d},\eta\}$\label{alg:ch17-rrtstar:radius}\;
      $X_{\mathrm{near}}\gets\Near(V,x_{\mathrm{new}},r_n)\cup\set{x_{\mathrm{nearest}}}$\label{alg:ch17-rrtstar:near}\;
      $x_{\mathrm{parent}}\gets$ \RrtStarChooseParent{$X_{\mathrm{near}}$, $x_{\mathrm{nearest}}$, $x_{\mathrm{new}}$}\label{alg:ch17-rrtstar:parent}\;
      $V\gets V\cup\set{x_{\mathrm{new}}}$; $E\gets E\cup\set{(x_{\mathrm{parent}},x_{\mathrm{new}})}$; $\mathrm{Cost}(x_{\mathrm{new}})\gets\mathrm{Cost}(x_{\mathrm{parent}})+\norm{x_{\mathrm{new}}-x_{\mathrm{parent}}}$\label{alg:ch17-rrtstar:add}\;
      \RrtStarRewire{$X_{\mathrm{near}}$, $x_{\mathrm{new}}$}\label{alg:ch17-rrtstar:rewire}\;
      \lIf{$x_{\mathrm{goal}}\in V$}{$c_{\mathrm{best}}\gets\mathrm{Cost}(x_{\mathrm{goal}})$\label{alg:ch17-rrtstar:best}}
    }
  }
  \lIf{$c_{\mathrm{best}}<\infty$}{\KwRet{the tree path from $x_{\mathrm{init}}$ to $x_{\mathrm{goal}}$}}
  \KwRet{failure}\;
}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
\caption{The helpers of \rrtstar}
\label{alg:ch17-helpers}
\Fn{\RrtStarChooseParent{$X_{\mathrm{near}}$, $x_{\mathrm{nearest}}$, $x_{\mathrm{new}}$}}{
  $x_{\mathrm{par}}\gets x_{\mathrm{nearest}}$; $c_{\mathrm{par}}\gets\mathrm{Cost}(x_{\mathrm{nearest}})+\norm{x_{\mathrm{new}}-x_{\mathrm{nearest}}}$\label{alg:ch17-helpers:default}\;
  \ForEach{$v\in X_{\mathrm{near}}$ in increasing order of $\mathrm{Cost}(v)+\norm{x_{\mathrm{new}}-v}$\label{alg:ch17-helpers:sort}}{
    \lIf{$\mathrm{Cost}(v)+\norm{x_{\mathrm{new}}-v}\ge c_{\mathrm{par}}$}{\KwBreak}
    \If{$\CollisionFree(v,x_{\mathrm{new}})$\label{alg:ch17-helpers:cpfree}}{
      $x_{\mathrm{par}}\gets v$; $c_{\mathrm{par}}\gets\mathrm{Cost}(v)+\norm{x_{\mathrm{new}}-v}$; \KwBreak\;
    }
  }
  \KwRet{$x_{\mathrm{par}}$}\;
}
\Fn{\RrtStarRewire{$X_{\mathrm{near}}$, $x_{\mathrm{new}}$}}{
  \ForEach{$v\in X_{\mathrm{near}}\setminus\set{\mathrm{parent}(x_{\mathrm{new}})}$}{
    $c\gets\mathrm{Cost}(x_{\mathrm{new}})+\norm{v-x_{\mathrm{new}}}$\label{alg:ch17-helpers:via}\;
    \If{$c<\mathrm{Cost}(v)$ \KwAnd $\CollisionFree(x_{\mathrm{new}},v)$\label{alg:ch17-helpers:rwfree}}{
      $E\gets E\setminus\set{(\mathrm{parent}(v),v)}\cup\set{(x_{\mathrm{new}},v)}$; $\mathrm{parent}(v)\gets x_{\mathrm{new}}$\label{alg:ch17-helpers:cut}\;
      \RrtStarUpdate{$v$}\label{alg:ch17-helpers:update}\;
    }
  }
}
\Fn{\RrtStarUpdate{$v$}}{
  $\mathrm{Cost}(v)\gets\mathrm{Cost}(\mathrm{parent}(v))+\norm{v-\mathrm{parent}(v)}$\;
  \lForEach{child $u$ of $v$}{\RrtStarUpdate{$u$}\tcp*[f]{depth-first over the subtree}}
}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Walkthrough.} Lines~\ref{alg:ch17-rrtstar:sample}--\ref{alg:ch17-rrtstar:free}
are \rrt. Line~\ref{alg:ch17-rrtstar:radius} computes the radius from the
\emph{current} number of vertices, which is why the radius is recomputed in
every iteration, and line~\ref{alg:ch17-rrtstar:near} collects the
candidates; $x_{\mathrm{nearest}}$ is added so that \textsc{ChooseParent}
always has one whose segment is known to be free, and adding it for
\textsc{ChooseParent} only, as \cref{lst:ch17-core} does, costs at most the
rare rewire of $x_{\mathrm{nearest}}$ itself. In \cref{alg:ch17-helpers}, \textsc{ChooseParent} starts from
the nearest vertex as the default ($x_{\mathrm{par}}$, $c_{\mathrm{par}}$ in
line~\ref{alg:ch17-helpers:default}),
whose segment is already known to be free, and improves on it only with a
candidate whose segment passes the check in
line~\ref{alg:ch17-helpers:cpfree}; the early \KwBreak after the sorted loop
means that no candidate is tested once it cannot beat the best value found.
The new vertex is inserted with its cost (line~\ref{alg:ch17-rrtstar:add} of
\cref{alg:ch17-rrtstar}) \emph{before} rewiring, because \textsc{Rewire}
reads $\mathrm{Cost}(x_{\mathrm{new}})$ in line~\ref{alg:ch17-helpers:via}
of \cref{alg:ch17-helpers}. Its line~\ref{alg:ch17-helpers:rwfree} tests the
improvement first and the collision second, so segments are checked only
when they would be used; line~\ref{alg:ch17-helpers:cut} swaps the parent
and line~\ref{alg:ch17-helpers:update} pushes the new cost down the subtree.
Line~\ref{alg:ch17-rrtstar:best} of \cref{alg:ch17-rrtstar} reads
$c_{\mathrm{best}}$ from the tree; it can only decrease, since
\textsc{Rewire} never increases any cost (\cref{prop:ch17-invariant}).

\subsection{The connection radius}
\label{sec:ch17-radius}

Every symbol in \cref{eq:ch17-radius} has a job.
\begin{itemize}
  \item $n=\abs{V}$ is the number of vertices \emph{in the tree}, not the
  number of iterations; blocked samples add no vertex and do not shrink the
  radius.
  \item $d$ is the dimension. Volume scales like $r^d$, so a ball must have
  radius $\propto(\cdot)^{1/d}$ to contain a prescribed fraction of the space.
  \item $(\log n/n)^{1/d}$ is the schedule. A ball of radius $r_n$ has volume
  $\zeta_d r_n^d=\zeta_d\gamma^d\log n/n$, so with $n$ vertices spread over
  $\mu(\Xfree)$ the expected number of vertices in it is
  $n\cdot\zeta_d\gamma^d\log n/(n\,\mu(\Xfree))=\zeta_d\gamma^d\log n/\mu(\Xfree)$,
  which grows only logarithmically.
  \item $\gamma_{\rrtstar}$ is the constant that decides \emph{how many}
  neighbours that logarithm holds. The threshold $\gamma^*_{\rrtstar}$ makes
  the expected number of neighbours at least $2^d(1+1/d)\log n$; with this
  many, the ball-covering argument of \cref{sec:ch17-properties} goes through.
  \item $\mu(\Xfree)$ is the volume of the free space; the larger it is, the
  sparser the vertices, and the larger the radius must be to find neighbours.
  Any upper bound, such as $\mu(\mathcal{X})$, is a safe substitute because a
  larger $\gamma$ still satisfies the condition.
  \item $\zeta_d$ converts the radius into a volume.
  \item $\eta$ caps the radius at the step size, so that connections are
  never longer than the segments $\Steer$ produces. For small $n$ the
  logarithmic term is far larger than $\eta$ and the cap is active; the
  shrinking phase begins only when $\gamma(\log n/n)^{1/d}$ falls below
  $\eta$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{example}[The constants of the two maps]
\label{ex:ch17-gamma}
The map of \cref{ch:ch16} is the square $[0,10]^2$ with the walls
$[3,5]\times[0,6]$ and $[6,8]\times[4,10]$, so $\mu(\Xfree)=100-12-12=76$
and, with $d=2$ and $\zeta_2=\pi$,
$\gamma^*_{\rrtstar}=2\sqrt{1.5}\,\sqrt{76/\pi}=12.05$. We use
$\gamma_{\rrtstar}=13.3$, ten percent above the threshold. With $\eta=1$ the
cap is active until $13.3\sqrt{\log n/n}<1$, that is until $n=1\,264$
vertices (between iterations $1\,500$ and $2\,000$ of the run in
\cref{tab:ch17-convergence}); at $n=2\,193$ the radius is $0.79$. For the 3D
box field of \cref{ch:ch16}, $\mu(\mathcal{X})=1\,000$ is a safe upper bound
for $\mu(\Xfree)$ and gives $\gamma^*_{\rrtstar}=2(4/3)^{1/3}(1000/\zeta_3)^{1/3}=13.66$;
the code uses $15.0$.
\end{example}

\paragraph{Why the radius must shrink, and shrink slowly.}\index{connection radius!shrinking}
The optimality proof needs two things at once. The tree must keep finding
neighbours everywhere along a near-optimal path, which pushes the radius up;
and the cost per iteration must stay bounded by a slowly growing function of
$n$, which pushes it down. If $r_n$ shrank like $n^{-1/d}$ or faster, the count of the third bullet
above would lose its $\log n$ and stay bounded, so a ball of radius $r_n$
would be empty with a probability bounded away from zero, iteration after
iteration. Somewhere along the optimal path
a ball would then stay empty for long stretches and the tree path would keep
its detour: this is the sense in which \emph{a radius that is too small
kills optimality} (\cref{sec:ch17-implementation}). The factor $\log n$ is
exactly what makes the expected count grow without bound, and the constant
in front decides how fast: with $\gamma$ above the threshold, the
probability that a given ball is empty decays like $n^{-c}$ with $c$ large
enough that, summed over all $n$ and over the $\bigO{n^{1/d}}$ balls needed
to cover the path, the sum is finite. The schedule $(\log n/n)^{1/d}$ is the slowest shrinking radius that keeps
the work per iteration at $\bigO{\log n}$; the same $\log n/n$ is the
threshold at which a random geometric graph on $n$ uniform points becomes
connected \cite{penrose2003random}, and \rrtstar succeeds because the graph
of all pairs within $r_n$ is, with high probability, connected along every
path with clearance.

\subsection{The $k$-nearest variant}
\label{sec:ch17-knn}

Instead of a ball, \textsc{Near} may return the $k_n$ nearest
vertices,\index{RRT*!k-nearest@$k$-nearest} with
\begin{equation}
  k_n=\bigl\lceil k_{\mathrm{RRG}}\log n\bigr\rceil,\qquad k_{\mathrm{RRG}}>e\Bigl(1+\frac1d\Bigr),
  \label{eq:ch17-knn}
\end{equation}
where $e$ is Euler's number \cite{karaman2011sampling}. The threshold has
the practical merit of not depending on $\mu(\Xfree)$, which is often
unknown; $k_{\mathrm{RRG}}=2e\approx5.44$ is valid in every dimension, and
the code uses $1.1\,e(1+1/d)$. The trade is a different behaviour in
non-uniform regions: in a dense cluster the $k$ nearest vertices lie inside
a tiny ball and rewiring becomes very local, while in a sparse region the
$k$-th neighbour may be far away and its segment is likely to be blocked.
On the map of \cref{ex:ch17-gamma}, $k_n=33$ at $n=1\,438$ vertices, and
after $2\,000$ iterations the $k$-nearest version reaches a best cost of
$17.44$ against $17.45$ for the ball version with the same seed; on this map
the two are interchangeable.

\section{Properties: correctness, optimality, complexity}
\label{sec:ch17-properties}

\subsection{What the tree always satisfies}
\label{sec:ch17-invariants}

\begin{proposition}[Invariants of \rrtstar]
\label{prop:ch17-invariant}
After every iteration of \cref{alg:ch17-rrtstar}:
(i)~$T$ is a tree rooted at $x_{\mathrm{init}}$ and every edge is
collision-free;
(ii)~$\mathrm{Cost}(v)$ is the length of the tree path from
$x_{\mathrm{init}}$ to $v$ for every $v\in V$;
(iii)~no cost increases during an iteration, so $c_{\mathrm{best}}$ is
non-increasing in the number of iterations;
(iv)~the vertex set is the one that \rrt would have built from the same
sequence of samples.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(i) Every edge is inserted only after $\CollisionFree$ has accepted it
(line~\ref{alg:ch17-rrtstar:free} of \cref{alg:ch17-rrtstar}, lines~\ref{alg:ch17-helpers:cpfree}
and \ref{alg:ch17-helpers:rwfree} of \cref{alg:ch17-helpers}). A rewire could create a cycle only if
$x_{\mathrm{new}}$ were a descendant of the rewired vertex $v$; but then the
tree path to $x_{\mathrm{new}}$ passes through $v$, so
$\mathrm{Cost}(x_{\mathrm{new}})\ge\mathrm{Cost}(v)+\norm{x_{\mathrm{new}}-v}$
by the triangle inequality, and the test $c<\mathrm{Cost}(v)$ in
line~\ref{alg:ch17-helpers:rwfree} of \cref{alg:ch17-helpers} fails. (ii) The new vertex receives
$\mathrm{Cost}(x_{\mathrm{parent}})+\norm{x_{\mathrm{new}}-x_{\mathrm{parent}}}$,
and after a rewire \textsc{UpdateSubtree} recomputes exactly the vertices
whose tree path changed, namely $v$ and its descendants. (iii)
\textsc{ChooseParent} touches no existing cost, and \textsc{Rewire} lowers
$\mathrm{Cost}(v)$ and, by the same amount, the cost of every descendant.
(iv) Whether a sample becomes a vertex, and where, depends only on
$\Nearest$, $\Steer$ and the check of line~\ref{alg:ch17-rrtstar:free} of
\cref{alg:ch17-rrtstar}; these are the steps of \rrt, and the extra steps
only change edges.
\end{proof}

Part~(iv) has a pleasant consequence: \rrtstar inherits the probabilistic
completeness\index{RRT*!probabilistic completeness} of \rrt
(\cref{ch:ch16}), because it contains the same vertices, and the goal vertex
appears with probability tending to one.

\subsection{Asymptotic optimality}
\label{sec:ch17-ao}

\begin{theorem}[Asymptotic optimality of \rrtstar; Karaman and Frazzoli 2011]
\label{thm:ch17-ao}
Let $d\ge2$, let $\mathcal{X}$ be bounded with $\mu(\Xfree)>0$, let the cost
be Euclidean length, let the samples be uniform on $\mathcal{X}$, let
$x_{\mathrm{goal}}$ enter the tree through the goal bias of
\cref{alg:ch17-rrtstar}, and suppose the problem has a robustly optimal solution
(\cref{def:ch17-clearance}) of cost $c^*<\infty$. If
$\gamma_{\rrtstar}>\gamma^*_{\rrtstar}$ in \cref{eq:ch17-radius}, then the
best cost $Y_i$ in the tree satisfies
$\Prob\bigl(\lim_{i\to\infty}Y_i=c^*\bigr)=1$.\index{RRT*!asymptotic optimality}\index{asymptotic optimality!theorem}
\end{theorem}

Read the statement carefully; it is easy to hear more in it than it says.
\begin{itemize}
  \item It promises convergence of the \emph{cost}, with probability one,
  as $n\to\infty$. It gives no rate: after any fixed budget $N$ the gap
  $Y_N-c^*$ can be large, and it typically shrinks slowly, because the last
  few percent are corner cuts that each need a sample in a small region
  (\cref{tab:ch17-convergence}).
  \item It does not promise convergence of the \emph{path}: the tree path
  may keep switching between routes of almost equal cost.
  \item It needs a robustly optimal solution. An optimum that squeezes
  through a gap of zero width has no clearance and is never found; the same
  holds for any planner that checks collisions at points.
  \item It needs $\gamma_{\rrtstar}$ above a threshold that depends on
  $\mu(\Xfree)$, which is usually unknown. Any upper bound, such as
  $\mu(\mathcal{X})$, is safe.
  \item Nothing is said about \rrt: Karaman and Frazzoli also prove that,
  under the same assumptions, the best cost of \rrt converges with
  probability one to a limit \emph{strictly larger} than $c^*$
  \cite{karaman2011sampling}, because the root of an \rrt tree acquires only
  finitely many children (once vertices surround it, new samples are steered
  from them instead), so every tree path leaves $x_{\mathrm{init}}$ along one
  of finitely many random segments, none of which points along the optimal
  path.\index{RRT!not asymptotically optimal}
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \inputfigure{ch17/ball-covering}
  \caption{The ball-covering argument. A near-optimal path $\sigma_n$
  with clearance $\delta_n$ (green, inside its blue tube) is covered by
  balls of radius $q_n$ (orange) whose centres are spaced so that any two
  points in consecutive balls are at most $r_n$ apart. If every ball
  contains a tree vertex, the vertices form a chain (blue) whose consecutive
  members were in each other's \textsc{Near} sets, so the tree connects
  them; the chain stays inside the tube and is therefore collision-free.}
  \label{fig:ch17-ball-covering}
\end{figure}

\begin{proof}[Proof idea]
The argument is illustrated in \cref{fig:ch17-ball-covering}; we sketch it
in five steps and say where the full proof works hardest.

\emph{Step 1 (a path with room).} Fix $\eps>0$. By robust optimality there
is a collision-free path $\sigma$ with strong $\delta$-clearance for some
$\delta>0$ and $c(\sigma)\le c^*+\eps$. (The full proof uses a sequence
$\sigma_n$ with $\delta_n\to0$ slowly; one path is enough for the idea.)

\emph{Step 2 (cover it with balls).} Take $n$ so large that $r_n<\delta$.
Choose a small $\theta>0$ and cover $\sigma$ with $M_n$ balls of radius
$q_n=r_n/(2+\theta)$ whose centres lie on $\sigma$ at spacing
$\theta q_n$. Two points in consecutive balls are then at most
$\theta q_n+2q_n=r_n$ apart, and the segment between them stays inside the
$\delta$-tube, so it is collision-free. The number of balls is
$M_n\approx c(\sigma)/(\theta q_n)$, which grows like $(n/\log n)^{1/d}$.

\emph{Step 3 (a ball is rarely empty).} A ball of radius $q_n$ has volume
$\zeta_dq_n^d=\zeta_d\gamma^d\log n/((2+\theta)^dn)$. If the $n$ vertices
were independent uniform points in $\Xfree$, the ball would be empty with
probability $(1-\zeta_dq_n^d/\mu(\Xfree))^n\le e^{-n\zeta_dq_n^d/\mu(\Xfree)}=n^{-a}$
with
\[
  a=\frac{\zeta_d\,\gamma^d}{(2+\theta)^d\,\mu(\Xfree)}.
\]
The vertices of \rrtstar are not uniform, since $\Steer$ produces them; the
full proof spends most of its length showing that, once the tree has a
vertex within $\eta$ of every point of the tube, a sample falling into a
ball becomes a vertex in that ball, so the estimate holds up to constants.

\emph{Step 4 (all balls, for all large $n$).} By the union bound the
probability that \emph{some} ball is empty at time $n$ is at most
$M_nn^{-a}\propto n^{1/d-a}(\log n)^{-1/d}$. Summed over $n$ this is finite
exactly when $a>1+1/d$, that is when
$\gamma>(2+\theta)\,(1+1/d)^{1/d}(\mu(\Xfree)/\zeta_d)^{1/d}$; because
$\gamma>\gamma^*_{\rrtstar}$ strictly, such a $\theta>0$ exists. This is
the origin of the constant $2(1+1/d)^{1/d}(\mu(\Xfree)/\zeta_d)^{1/d}$: the
factor $2$ is the two ball radii that must fit inside one connection
radius, and $(1+1/d)^{1/d}$ is the exponent that beats the $n^{1/d}$
balls. The Borel--Cantelli lemma\index{Borel--Cantelli lemma} (if
$\sum_n\Prob(A_n)<\infty$, then with probability one only finitely many
$A_n$ occur) now says that, from some random $n_0$ on, \emph{every} ball
contains a vertex.

\emph{Step 5 (the chain is cheap).} Pick one vertex in each ball. Consecutive vertices are within $r_n$, so the
later one found the earlier in its \textsc{Near} set when it was inserted
(the radius was then even larger), and \textsc{ChooseParent} and
\textsc{Rewire} connect them at least as cheaply as the direct segment. The
chain zigzags inside the tube, so this crude bound only gives a constant
factor above $c(\sigma)$; the full proof removes the factor using the
$\Theta(\log n)$ vertices that every ball eventually contains, most of which
lie very close to the centre line, so the best chain straightens as $n$
grows and its cost tends to $c(\sigma)\le c^*+\eps$. Since $\eps$ was
arbitrary and $Y_n\ge c^*$ always, $Y_n\to c^*$ with probability one.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The fine print of the radius]
\label{rem:ch17-fineprint}
\Cref{eq:ch17-radius} is the constant Karaman and Frazzoli prove, for the
rapidly-exploring random \emph{graph} (RRG),\index{RRG} which keeps every
edge between vertices within $r_n$, and for \rrtstar alike.
Implementations use a smaller one: \textcite{gammell2014informed} and the
OMPL library take
$\gamma_{\rrtstar}=\bigl(2(1+1/d)\,\mu(\Xfree)/\zeta_d\bigr)^{1/d}$, which
in 2D is $1.73\sqrt{\mu/\pi}$ against the proved $2.45\sqrt{\mu/\pi}$, about
$30\,\%$ below the threshold. It works well in practice but is not covered
by the theorem, so \cref{eq:ch17-radius} is the safe choice. Step~5 is where the tree makes life difficult, because the
cost of a vertex depends on the order in which its neighbours arrived:
\textcite{solovey2020revisiting} found a gap there in the original argument
and closed it with a radius that shrinks slightly more slowly,
$\gamma'(\log n/n)^{1/(d+1)}$. In practice everyone uses
\cref{eq:ch17-radius} with $\gamma$ above the threshold, and it converges as
\cref{fig:ch17-convergence} shows.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Cost per iteration}
\label{sec:ch17-cost}

An \rrt iteration does one nearest-neighbour query and one collision
check. An \rrtstar iteration adds a range query and up to
$1+2\abs{X_{\mathrm{near}}}$ collision checks (one per candidate in
\textsc{ChooseParent}, one per candidate in \textsc{Rewire}).\index{RRT*!complexity}
By the computation in \cref{sec:ch17-radius} the expected size of
$X_{\mathrm{near}}$ is $\zeta_d\gamma^d\log n/\mu(\Xfree)$, so the number of
candidates is $\bigO{\log n}$ in expectation, and with a kd-tree
(\cref{ch:ch16}) the nearest-neighbour query costs $\bigO{\log n}$ and the
range query $\bigO{\log n+\abs{X_{\mathrm{near}}}}$, which is $\bigO{\log n}$
again because the reported set is itself logarithmic. A run of $n$ iterations therefore costs
$\bigO{n\log n}$, the same order as \rrt with a kd-tree; Karaman and
Frazzoli show that the ratio of the expected running times of the two
planners is bounded by a constant \cite{karaman2011sampling}. The constant comes almost entirely from collision checks: in
\cref{ex:ch17-map} the \textsc{Near} set had $23.6$ members on average, but
the ordering trick of \cref{alg:ch17-helpers} kept the count at $2.7$
checks per iteration instead of the worst case of $48$.

\section{\irrtstar: sampling where a better path can pass}
\label{sec:ch17-informed}

\subsection{The informed set}
\label{sec:ch17-informed-set}

Once the tree contains a path of cost $c_{\mathrm{best}}$, most of the
free space can no longer matter. Take any point $x$ and any path
$\sigma$ from $x_{\mathrm{init}}$ to $x_{\mathrm{goal}}$ through $x$. The
cheapest way into $x$ is the straight segment from the start and the
cheapest way out is the straight segment to the goal, so
\begin{equation}
  c(\sigma)\ \ge\ \hat f(x):=\norm{x-x_{\mathrm{init}}}+\norm{x_{\mathrm{goal}}-x}.
  \label{eq:ch17-fhat}
\end{equation}
A path that improves on the current one has $c(\sigma)<c_{\mathrm{best}}$,
hence every point on it satisfies $\hat f(x)<c_{\mathrm{best}}$: it lies in
the informed set $\mathcal{X}_{\hat f}(c_{\mathrm{best}})$ of
\cref{def:ch17-informed}. Samples outside that set cannot improve the
solution, and a planner that keeps drawing them wastes its iterations. You
have met $\hat f$ before: it is $\gcost+\hcost$ with the straight-line
heuristic, and the informed set is the region $\fcost\le C^*$ that \astar
with a consistent heuristic never leaves (\cref{ch:ch04}).

The set $\set{x:\norm{x-x_{\mathrm{init}}}+\norm{x-x_{\mathrm{goal}}}\le c_{\mathrm{best}}}$
is, by the string-and-two-pins definition, an ellipse in 2D and an
ellipsoid of revolution in 3D; the general name is \textbf{prolate
hyperspheroid}.\index{prolate hyperspheroid}\index{informed set!geometry}
Its foci are $x_{\mathrm{init}}$ and $x_{\mathrm{goal}}$, its centre is
$\vect{x}_{\mathrm{centre}}=(x_{\mathrm{init}}+x_{\mathrm{goal}})/2$, and its
\textbf{transverse diameter} (along the focal axis) is $c_{\mathrm{best}}$.
Its \textbf{conjugate diameter} follows from the point $x$ on the conjugate
axis at distance $b$ from the centre: both foci are at distance
$\sqrt{b^2+c_{\min}^2/4}$ from it, so $\hat f(x)=2\sqrt{b^2+c_{\min}^2/4}=c_{\mathrm{best}}$
gives $2b=\sqrt{c_{\mathrm{best}}^2-c_{\min}^2}$. In summary,
\begin{equation}
  \text{semi-axes}\quad r_1=\frac{c_{\mathrm{best}}}{2},\qquad
  r_2=\dots=r_d=\frac{\sqrt{c_{\mathrm{best}}^2-c_{\min}^2}}{2},\qquad
  \mu\bigl(\mathcal{X}_{\hat f}\bigr)=\zeta_d\,r_1r_2^{d-1}.
  \label{eq:ch17-axes}
\end{equation}
The volume tells you when informed sampling pays. On the $10\times10$ map
of \cref{ex:ch17-map}, $c_{\min}=11.31$ and the first solution has
$c_{\mathrm{best}}=21.0$, so the ellipse has area $292$ and contains the
whole map. It keeps containing it until $c_{\mathrm{best}}=18.11$
(\cref{exr:ch17-fraction}) and then loses only two thin corners: $99.5\,\%$
of the map is still inside at $c_{\mathrm{best}}=17.32$ (the code rejected
$8$ of $2\,657$ informed draws) and $98.7\,\%$ at the optimum, where the
ellipse has area $167$. Uniform sampling of the map is therefore
essentially informed sampling there, which is why the two planners
coincide in \cref{fig:ch17-convergence}(a). In the field $[-10,20]^2$ with
the same two walls, the same start and the same goal
(\code{wide\_example\_world()}, \cref{fig:ch17-ellipses}) the free space is
nine times larger, and the ellipse of the first solution covers $46\,\%$ of
the field and that of the final one $10\,\%$, so nine of ten uniform samples
are wasted. In 3D the effect is stronger, because the conjugate axes enter
the volume with the power $d-1$: in the box field of \cref{ch:ch16}
($c_{\min}=15.59$) a solution of cost $17.0$ has an ellipsoid of volume
$41\,\%$ of the box, one of cost $16.0$ only $11\,\%$.

\subsection{Sampling the ellipsoid uniformly}
\label{sec:ch17-sampling}

Rejection sampling (draw uniformly in $\mathcal{X}$, keep the sample if
$\hat f(x)\le c_{\mathrm{best}}$) is correct but wasteful in exactly the
cases where the informed set helps, because its acceptance rate is the
volume ratio just computed. \textcite{gammell2014informed} sample the
hyperspheroid directly by mapping the unit ball onto it
(\cref{fig:ch17-ellipse-transform}):
\begin{equation}
  x=\mat{C}\,\mat{L}\,\vect{x}_{\mathrm{ball}}+\vect{x}_{\mathrm{centre}},\qquad
  \mat{L}=\diag(r_1,r_2,\dots,r_d),\qquad
  \mat{C}\vect{e}_1=\vect{a}_1:=\frac{x_{\mathrm{goal}}-x_{\mathrm{init}}}{c_{\min}}.
  \label{eq:ch17-transform}
\end{equation}
Here $\vect{x}_{\mathrm{ball}}$ is uniform in the unit ball, $\mat{L}$
stretches the ball into the axis-aligned spheroid with the semi-axes of
\cref{eq:ch17-axes}, $\mat{C}$ is a rotation matrix that turns the first
coordinate axis into the start-to-goal direction, and the translation puts
the centre between the foci. The map is affine with a constant Jacobian
determinant $\det\mat{L}=r_1r_2^{d-1}>0$, so a uniform density on the ball
becomes a uniform density on its image (\cref{exr:ch17-ellipsoid} asks you
to verify this and the axes). Two ingredients remain.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{A uniform point of the unit ball.} Draw
  $\vect{u}\sim\Normal(\vect{0},\mat{I})$ and $w\sim U(0,1)$ and return
  $w^{1/d}\vect{u}/\norm{\vect{u}}$: the normalised Gaussian is uniform on
  the sphere (\cref{ch:ch02}), and the radius $w^{1/d}$ has the density
  $\propto\rho^{d-1}$ that the volume of a shell demands.
  \item \emph{The rotation to the world frame.}\index{rotation to world frame}
  In 2D, $\mat{C}$ is the rotation by the angle of $\vect{a}_1$,
  $\mat{C}=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}\cos\phi&-\sin\phi\\\sin\phi&\cos\phi\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$
  with $(\cos\phi,\sin\phi)=\vect{a}_1$. In any dimension, Gammell et al.\
  compute it from the singular value decomposition
  $\mat{U}\mat{\Sigma}\mat{V}\T$ of the rank-one matrix
  $\mat{M}=\vect{a}_1\vect{e}_1\T$ as
  \begin{equation}
    \mat{C}=\mat{U}\,\diag\bigl(1,\dots,1,\det\mat{U}\det\mat{V}\bigr)\,\mat{V}\T,
    \label{eq:ch17-rotation}
  \end{equation}
  which is orthogonal with determinant $+1$ and satisfies
  $\mat{C}\vect{e}_1=\vect{a}_1$. In 3D any rotation with first column $\vect{a}_1$ will do, such as
  $[\vect{a}_1\ \ \vect{b}\ \ \vect{a}_1\times\vect{b}]$ with
  $\vect{b}\perp\vect{a}_1$, because the spheroid is symmetric about its
  transverse axis.
\end{itemize}

\Cref{alg:ch17-informed} is the complete planner. Before a solution exists,
$c_{\mathrm{best}}=\infty$ and the sampler falls back to the goal-biased
uniform sampling of \cref{alg:ch17-rrtstar}; from the first solution on,
the tree grows only inside the current ellipsoid, which shrinks whenever
\textsc{Rewire} improves the goal's branch. \textsc{Near},
\textsc{ChooseParent} and \textsc{Rewire} are unchanged, and so is the
theory: the samples remain uniform on a set that contains every point that
can still improve the solution, so \irrtstar keeps the probabilistic
completeness and the asymptotic optimality of \rrtstar
\cite{gammell2014informed}. Samples of the hyperspheroid outside $\mathcal{X}$ are rejected
(line~\ref{alg:ch17-informed:map}); when the hyperspheroid is larger than
$\mathcal{X}$, the code does the opposite and samples $\mathcal{X}$,
rejecting by $\hat f$.

\caption{\irrtstar (Gammell, Srinivasa and Barfoot)}
\label{alg:ch17-informed}
\KwIn{as in \cref{alg:ch17-rrtstar}}
\KwOut{the tree path to $x_{\mathrm{goal}}$ of cost $c_{\mathrm{best}}$, or \emph{failure}}
\Fn{\RrtStarInformed{$x_{\mathrm{init}}$, $x_{\mathrm{goal}}$, $N$}}{

  }
  \lIf{$c_{\mathrm{best}}<\infty$}{\KwRet{the tree path from $x_{\mathrm{init}}$ to $x_{\mathrm{goal}}$}}
  \KwRet{failure}\;

  \lIf{$c_{\mathrm{best}}=\infty$}{\KwRet{$x_{\mathrm{goal}}$ with probability $p_{\mathrm{goal}}$, else a uniform sample of $\mathcal{X}$}}

\Fn{\RrtStarRotation{$\vect{a}_1$}}{
  $\mat{U}\mat{\Sigma}\mat{V}\T\gets$ singular value decomposition of $\vect{a}_1\vect{e}_1\T$\label{alg:ch17-informed:svd}\;
  \KwRet{$\mat{U}\,\diag(1,\dots,1,\det\mat{U}\det\mat{V})\,\mat{V}\T$}\;

$\mu(\Xfree)$, as the code does, is always admissible, because a larger
$\gamma$ still satisfies the condition, and those extra checks are what it
costs.

\section{Variants and extensions}
\label{sec:ch17-variants}

\paragraph{The graph and the batch.} The rapidly-exploring random graph
(RRG)\index{RRG} keeps \emph{all} edges between vertices within $r_n$;
\rrtstar is its tree, and PRM\textsuperscript{*}\index{PRM*} is the
probabilistic roadmap of \cref{ch:ch16} built with the same radius on a
batch of $n$ samples; both are asymptotically optimal. Fast marching trees (FMT\textsuperscript{*})\index{FMT*}
\cite{janson2015fmt} process one batch of samples in a single Dijkstra-like
sweep with lazy collision checks, often faster than \rrtstar for a fixed
budget, but not anytime: the batch size is chosen before the run.

\paragraph{Anytime \rrtstar.}\index{anytime planning!RRT*}
\textcite{karaman2011anytime} run \rrtstar on a moving robot: the tree keeps
growing while the robot follows the current best path, the robot commits to
the first segment of that path and the tree is re-rooted at its end, and
\textbf{branch-and-bound pruning}\index{branch-and-bound pruning} deletes
every vertex $v$ with $\mathrm{Cost}(v)+\norm{x_{\mathrm{goal}}-v}\ge c_{\mathrm{best}}$,
which can never lie on a better path. Pruning is the tree-side twin of the
informed set, and the two are usually combined
(\cref{sec:ch17-implementation}).

\paragraph{BIT\textsuperscript{*} and the batch-informed family.}\index{BIT*}
Batch informed trees (BIT\textsuperscript{*}) of
\textcite{gammell2015bit} take the informed set one step further. Samples
are drawn in \emph{batches} inside the current ellipsoid, the batch together
with the tree is viewed as an implicit random geometric graph, and that
graph is searched with an edge queue ordered by
$\hat f=\hat g+\hat h$, exactly as \astar orders its nodes; collision
checks are made only when an edge is popped, and the search is reused across
batches in the manner of \lpastar (\cref{ch:ch05}). Each batch shrinks the
ellipsoid, and the planner is anytime and asymptotically optimal: it is the
point where sampling-based planning and heuristic graph search, the two
halves of this book, meet. Later refinements by the same group
(ABIT\textsuperscript{*}, AIT\textsuperscript{*}) improve the ordering and
the heuristic, and \textcite{gammell2018informed} generalise informed sets
to other cost functions.

\section{Implementation notes}
\label{sec:ch17-implementation}

The file \code{code/ch17\_rrt\_star.py} implements everything in this
chapter for $d=2$ and $d=3$ with NumPy only: the world and the collision
checker are copied from \cref{ch:ch16}, so the two chapters plan on the
same maps. \Cref{lst:ch17-core} is the body of one iteration.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={One iteration of \rrtstar (from \code{code/ch17\_rrt\_star.py}): \textsc{Nearest}, \textsc{Steer} and the collision check as in \rrt, then \textsc{Near}, \textsc{ChooseParent} with the candidates sorted by cost, and \textsc{Rewire} with the improvement test before the segment check. \code{tree.set\_parent} propagates the new cost to the subtree; \code{radius} is $r_n$ or $k_n$.},label={lst:ch17-core}]

                  else connection_radius(tree.n, d, gamma, eta))
        if d_nearest > 1e-12 and world.segment_free(tree.V[i_nearest], x_new):

            else:

            #     start-goal distance of the informed set) ------------------
            i_par, c_par = i_nearest, tree.cost[i_nearest] + d_nearest

                    if cand[k] >= c_par:

\paragraph{Choosing $\gamma_{\rrtstar}$.} $\mu(\Xfree)$ is rarely known; the
code estimates it by Monte Carlo for the text but plans with
$\gamma=1.1\,\gamma^*(\mu(\mathcal{X}))$, the safe upper bound of
\cref{sec:ch17-radius}: on the 3D scene of \cref{ch:ch16} that is
$\gamma=15.0$ against a threshold of $12.2$ for the true free volume of
about $716$. Overshooting costs work, not optimality
(\cref{fig:ch17-radius}): doubling $\gamma$ multiplies the expected
\textsc{Near} set by $2^d$.

\paragraph{The cost of rewiring.} Collision checks dominate. Three habits
keep them rare: sort the candidates of \textsc{ChooseParent} by cost and
stop at the first free segment; in \textsc{Rewire} test the improvement
before the segment; and reuse the check of the parent segment for the
edge to the nearest vertex, which is already known to be free.

\paragraph{Propagating costs to a subtree.} The cost-to-come of every
candidate must be available in $\bigO{1}$, so the implementation stores
$\mathrm{Cost}(v)$ and keeps it consistent: \cref{lst:ch17-subtree} keeps,
for every vertex, its parent, the length of its parent edge and a list of
its children; \code{set\_parent} moves the vertex, then walks its subtree
with an explicit stack and recomputes $\mathrm{Cost}$ from the parent,
exactly \textsc{UpdateSubtree}. The lazy alternative, summing edge lengths
on demand, costs $\bigO{\text{depth}}$ per candidate and is slower in every
experiment we ran. The self-test asserts the invariant after every run.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={\textsc{UpdateSubtree} (from \code{code/ch17\_rrt\_star.py}): rewiring vertex \code{i} under \code{p} and recomputing the cost of every descendant with an explicit stack.},label={lst:ch17-subtree}]
    def set_parent(self, i, p, edge_len):
        """Rewire vertex i under p and update the costs of its subtree."""
        self.children[self.parent[i]].remove(i)
        self.parent[i], self.edge[i] = p, edge_len
        self.children[p].append(i)
        stack = [i]
        while stack:
            v = stack.pop()
            self.cost[v] = self.cost[self.parent[v]] + self.edge[v]
            stack.extend(self.children[v])
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Nearest and near.} The implementation uses vectorised linear
scans, because its trees have a few thousand vertices and a run takes about
a second. Beyond $10^4$ vertices use a kd-tree (\cref{ch:ch16}):
\textsc{Near} becomes a range query, and since points only ever get
inserted, a tree rebuilt whenever the vertex count doubles keeps the
amortised cost logarithmic. The class \code{InformedSampler} of the file implements
\cref{alg:ch17-informed} line by line, with the rotation computed once per
problem from the SVD of \cref{eq:ch17-rotation}.


\paragraph{Anytime use.}\index{anytime planning!while flying} A drone
does not wait for $N$ iterations. The pattern of
\textcite{karaman2011anytime} is: plan until the first solution, start
flying its first segment, keep iterating at a fixed rate, and hand over the
new path whenever $c_{\mathrm{best}}$ improves by more than a tolerance.
At each waypoint re-root the tree there: the waypoint's subtree keeps its
structure, its costs drop by the cost of the waypoint, and the other
branches are discarded. With branch-and-bound pruning the tree stays small
and focused; \cref{exr:ch17-anytime} builds it.

\begin{pitfall}[A radius that is too small kills optimality]
\rrtstar with a constant small radius, or with $\gamma$ below the
threshold, still finds paths and still rewires, so nothing looks broken;
it just stops improving at a cost above the optimum, because balls along
the optimal route stay empty (\cref{sec:ch17-radius}). Two ways to get
there by accident: forgetting the root, $r_n=\gamma\log n/n$ instead of
$\gamma(\log n/n)^{1/d}$, and reusing a $\gamma$ tuned in 2D for a 3D
problem, where $\zeta_3$ and the exponent $1/3$ change the threshold. 
\end{pitfall}

\begin{pitfall}[Rewiring without propagating]
If \textsc{Rewire} updates $\mathrm{Cost}(v)$ but not the costs of $v$'s
descendants, the tree still returns valid paths, but $\mathrm{Cost}$ of
those descendants is now too high: \textsc{ChooseParent} undervalues them
as parents and \textsc{Rewire} rewires them when it should not.
Convergence slows down and $c_{\mathrm{best}}$, read from
$\mathrm{Cost}(x_{\mathrm{goal}})$, may be reported wrongly. Keep children
lists and propagate (\cref{lst:ch17-subtree}), and assert
\cref{prop:ch17-invariant}(ii) in your tests; the bug shows up only there.
\end{pitfall}

\begin{pitfall}[Sampling the ellipse before a solution exists]
With $c_{\mathrm{best}}=\infty$ the transformation produces \code{inf} and
\code{nan}; with $c_{\mathrm{best}}<c_{\min}$, which happens when the goal
is a region and $c_{\min}$ is measured to its centre, the square root is
imaginary. Sample uniformly until the goal is in the tree, measure
$c_{\min}$ to the point actually reached, and clamp
$c_{\mathrm{best}}^2-c_{\min}^2$ at zero.
\end{pitfall}

\begin{pitfall}[Rewiring through obstacles]
The temptation is to skip the collision check in \textsc{Rewire} because
``$x_{\mathrm{new}}$ was checked when it was inserted''. Only the segment to
its parent was. Every rewired edge is a new segment and needs its own check
(line~\ref{alg:ch17-helpers:rwfree} of \cref{alg:ch17-helpers}); the same
holds for the candidate segments of \textsc{ChooseParent}, and a checker that is not symmetric (a drone with a heading) must run in
the direction the edge will be flown.
\end{pitfall}

\section{Where this fits in the drone system}
\label{sec:ch17-drone}

\begin{dronebox}
\rrtstar and \irrtstar are the continuous planners of the global and
replanning layers of \cref{ch:ch24}. The swarm as a whole is planned on a
grid by \cbs or \ecbs (\cref{ch:ch09,ch:ch10}), because conflicts between
drones are easiest to resolve in discrete space-time. A
single drone calls \rrtstar instead of \astar when (i) it must cross a 3D
volume whose grid would be far larger than the tree that solves it,
(ii) its constraints are not grid-shaped, such as a turning radius handled
by kinodynamic steering, or (iii) an unexpected obstacle has invalidated a
long stretch of its nominal route and the replanning layer wants a short,
smooth detour rather than a staircase. It
keeps \astar or \dstarlite (\cref{ch:ch04,ch:ch05}) when the map is small,
when the plan must be time-indexed for the conflict re-check, or when the
map keeps changing and incremental repair is cheaper than a new tree.
Three habits make the planner fit the architecture. \emph{Anytime
operation}: return the first path after a few hundred iterations, hand it
to the local layer (\orca or the dynamic window approach, DWA,
\cref{ch:ch13,ch:ch14}), and keep
improving it while the drone flies, re-rooting at every waypoint.
\emph{Seeding}: string-pull the grid path from \cbs (\cref{ch:ch16}),
insert its waypoints as the initial tree and set $c_{\mathrm{best}}$ to its
length, so that \irrtstar samples its ellipsoid from the first iteration
(on the map of \cref{ex:ch17-map} that path costs $17.23$, the first random
solution $21.0$). \emph{Re-check}: a continuous path
is still a single-agent path; after any replan, run the conflict check of
\cref{ch:ch24} against the other drones' reservations before flying it.
This is Week~8 of the study plan (\cref{ch:appA}); its milestone is being
able to say, for a given mission, which planner of
\cref{tab:ch17-comparison} or which grid search to call.
\end{dronebox}

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:ch17-summary}
\begin{summary}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \rrtstar adds \textsc{Near}, \textsc{ChooseParent} and
  \textsc{Rewire} to \rrt: the new vertex takes the cheapest collision-free
  parent within $r_n$, then becomes the parent of every neighbour it can
  serve more cheaply, and the saving is propagated to all descendants.
  \item The connection radius
  $r_n=\min\{\gamma_{\rrtstar}(\log n/n)^{1/d},\eta\}$ with
  $\gamma_{\rrtstar}>2(1+1/d)^{1/d}(\mu(\Xfree)/\zeta_d)^{1/d}$ shrinks as
  slowly as it can while keeping $\bigO{\log n}$ neighbours; the $k$-nearest
  variant uses $k_n=\lceil k_{\mathrm{RRG}}\log n\rceil$ and needs no
  volume.
  \item With that radius the best cost converges to the optimum almost
  surely (ball covering plus Borel--Cantelli); the theorem gives no rate,
  needs a robustly optimal solution, and says nothing about the path
  itself. \rrt converges to a suboptimal limit almost surely.
  \item An iteration costs $\bigO{\log n}$ expected neighbours and a few
  collision checks; a run costs $\bigO{n\log n}$, a constant factor above
  \rrt.
    \item Once a solution of cost $c_{\mathrm{best}}$ exists, only the
  prolate hyperspheroid with foci $x_{\mathrm{init}},x_{\mathrm{goal}}$,
  transverse diameter $c_{\mathrm{best}}$ and conjugate diameter
  $\sqrt{c_{\mathrm{best}}^2-c_{\min}^2}$ can contain a better path;
  \irrtstar samples it uniformly (scale the unit ball, rotate, translate)
  and converges much faster in large spaces.
  \item BIT\textsuperscript{*} searches batches of informed samples with
  an \astar-ordered edge queue; anytime \rrtstar re-roots the tree while
  the drone flies and prunes vertices that cannot improve the solution.
\end{itemize}
\end{summary}

\paragraph{Further reading.}
The original paper is \textcite{karaman2011sampling}, which introduces
RRG, \rrtstar and PRM\textsuperscript{*}, proves that \rrt is not
asymptotically optimal and contains the full proof; anytime use is described
by \textcite{karaman2011anytime}, and the proof is revisited by
\textcite{solovey2020revisiting}. \irrtstar is due to
\textcite{gammell2014informed} and was generalised by
\textcite{gammell2018informed}; BIT\textsuperscript{*} is due to
\textcite{gammell2015bit} and FMT\textsuperscript{*} to
\textcite{janson2015fmt}. The textbook treatment of
sampling-based planning is \textcite{lavalle2006planning}, and the tree
itself goes back to \textcite{lavalle1998rrt}. The connectivity threshold
of random geometric graphs, the reason the radius has the form it has, is
the subject of \textcite{penrose2003random}.

\begin{exercise}[\difficulty{1}]
\label{exr:ch17-radius}

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[\difficulty{2}]
\label{exr:ch17-ellipsoid}
Derive the sampling transformation of \cref{eq:ch17-transform}. (a) Show
that the set $\hat f(x)\le c_{\mathrm{best}}$ is the image of the unit ball
under $x=\mat{C}\mat{L}\vect{x}_{\mathrm{ball}}+\vect{x}_{\mathrm{centre}}$
with the semi-axes of \cref{eq:ch17-axes}; start from the frame in which
the foci are $(\pm c_{\min}/2,0,\dots,0)$. (b) Show that an affine map with
invertible matrix $\mat{A}$ sends a uniform density to a uniform density
divided by $\abs{\det\mat{A}}$. (c) Show that
$w^{1/d}\vect{u}/\norm{\vect{u}}$ is uniform in the unit ball. (d) Verify
that \cref{eq:ch17-rotation} is orthogonal, has determinant $+1$ and maps
$\vect{e}_1$ to $\vect{a}_1$; in 2D reduce it to a rotation by the angle of
$\vect{a}_1$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[\difficulty{2}]
\label{exr:ch17-invariant}
Prove \cref{prop:ch17-invariant}(i) in detail: show that a rewire can never
create a cycle, and construct a small tree, sample and near set in which
\textsc{Rewire} considers a candidate that would create one and rejects it.
Then show that the \emph{order} in which \textsc{Rewire} processes the
candidates does not change the final tree of the iteration (except for
exact ties), even when one candidate is a descendant of another, but can
change the propagation work; which order minimises it?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[\difficulty{2}]
\label{exr:ch17-rrt-limit}

to a random limit above $c^*$. Then test it: run \code{rrt\_plain} on
\cref{ex:ch17-map} for $20$ seeds and $10\,000$ iterations, keeping the goal
bias on and recording the best of \emph{all} goal-reaching paths, and
compare the final costs with \cref{fig:ch17-convergence}(a).
\end{exercise}
