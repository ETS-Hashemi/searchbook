\section{Why this matters for a drone swarm}
\label{sec:ch18-motivation}

The planners of Parts~II and~III know where every drone of the swarm is,
because the swarm reports its own positions. An intruder reports nothing. A
foreign drone that crosses the swarm's airspace is seen only through a sensor
--- a radar, a camera with a depth estimate, a receiver of broadcast position
messages --- that delivers a noisy position a few times per second. Suppose
the sensor reports the intruder's position every $\dt=0.1$~s with an error of
about $1$~m in each coordinate. The local avoidance layer of \cref{ch:ch13}
needs the intruder's \emph{velocity} to build a velocity obstacle, and the
obvious way to obtain it is to difference two consecutive reports. That
divides the difference of two $1$-metre errors by $0.1$~s: the velocity
estimate carries a noise of about $\sqrt2\cdot1/0.1\approx14$~m/s, several
times the speed of the intruder itself. \Cref{fig:ch18-raw} shows what this
looks like. The raw positions jitter by metres from one report to the next,
and the differenced velocity is useless. No avoidance rule can work with that
input: \orca would see a different collision cone every tenth of a second, and
a formation would scatter in response to noise.

The Kalman filter turns this stream of jittery reports into a stable estimate
of the intruder's state --- position \emph{and} velocity --- together with a
covariance matrix that says how far the estimate can be trusted. It does so by
carrying a model of how the intruder moves, predicting where it will be at the
next report, and correcting the prediction by the new measurement, weighting
the two by their uncertainties. In the experiment of \cref{fig:ch18-raw},
produced by the code of this chapter, the filter reduces the position error
from $1.18$~m to $0.52$~m (root mean square) and the velocity error from
$16.8$~m/s to $0.79$~m/s, at the cost of a few small matrix products per
report.

In the hybrid architecture of \cref{ch:ch24} the filter is the core of the
\emph{prediction layer}: it tracks the intruder, hands its state and
covariance to the local safety layer (\orca or DWA, \cref{ch:ch13,ch:ch14})
and to the trajectory predictors of \cref{ch:ch20}, and its covariance,
propagated a few seconds ahead, gives the growing uncertainty ellipses with
which the safety layer inflates the intruder's radius. It is the Week~9 topic
of the study plan (\cref{ch:appA}), whose milestone is precisely that your
planner receives a stable estimated state instead of raw observations.

\section{The idea in plain words}
\label{sec:ch18-intuition}

You have two sources of information about the intruder, and both are
imperfect. The first is a \emph{motion model}: a drone that was at some
position with some velocity a tenth of a second ago is now, very probably, a
tenth of a second further along that velocity. The model is not exact, because
the intruder may accelerate, so its prediction becomes less certain the longer
you rely on it. The second is the \emph{sensor}, which reports the position
with an error that does not accumulate but is large at every single report.
Neither source alone is good enough; combined, they are.

The Kalman filter\index{Kalman filter} is the combination. It keeps a Gaussian
belief about the state, a mean $\hat{\state}$ and a covariance $\mat{P}$, and
alternates two steps (\cref{fig:ch18-idea}). In the \textbf{predict
step}\index{Kalman filter!predict step} it pushes the belief through the
motion model: the mean moves along the velocity and the covariance grows,
because the model's own noise is added. In the \textbf{update
step}\index{Kalman filter!update step} it compares the new measurement with
the predicted measurement; the difference is the \emph{innovation}, the news
that the measurement brings. The state is corrected by a fraction of the
innovation, and that fraction, the \emph{Kalman gain}, is large when the
prediction is uncertain and the sensor accurate, small in the opposite case.
The covariance shrinks, because two independent pieces of information are
worth more than one. Then the cycle repeats.

Behind the fraction is the arithmetic of a weighted average: two readings of
the same quantity are best combined with weights proportional to their
precisions (inverse variances), and the precision of the result is the sum of
the two. The rest of the chapter is the bookkeeping that keeps this rule exact
when the state has several components, the sensor sees only some of them, and
time passes between readings.

\begin{keyidea}
Keep a Gaussian belief $\Normal(\hat{\state},\mat{P})$ over the state. Predict
by pushing it through the motion model: the mean moves, the covariance grows
by $\mat{Q}$. Update by moving the mean towards the measurement with a gain
that weights prediction and measurement by their precisions: the covariance
shrinks. For a linear model with Gaussian noise this recursion is exact and
optimal --- no estimator, linear or not, has a smaller mean-square error.
\end{keyidea}

\section{Problem statement and notation}
\label{sec:ch18-problem}

\subsection{The linear-Gaussian state-space model}

\begin{definition}[Linear-Gaussian state-space model]
\label{def:ch18-model}
A \textbf{linear-Gaussian state-space model}\index{state-space model!linear-Gaussian}
for a state $\state_k\in\R^n$ observed through measurements $\meas_k\in\R^m$ at
steps $k=1,2,\dots$ consists of a \textbf{motion model}\index{motion model}
and a \textbf{measurement model},
\begin{align}
  \state_k &= \mat{F}\state_{k-1}+\mat{B}\ctrl_k+\vect{w}_k, & \vect{w}_k&\sim\Normal(\vect{0},\mat{Q}),\label{eq:ch18-motion}\\
  \meas_k &= \mat{H}\state_k+\vect{v}_k, & \vect{v}_k&\sim\Normal(\vect{0},\mat{R}),\label{eq:ch18-measurement}
\end{align}
with an initial belief $\state_0\sim\Normal(\hat{\state}_0,\mat{P}_0)$.
$\mat{F}\in\R^{n\times n}$ is the \textbf{state transition matrix},
$\mat{B}\in\R^{n\times l}$ the control matrix acting on a known input
$\ctrl_k\in\R^l$ (absent for an intruder, $\mat{B}\ctrl_k=\vect{0}$),
$\mat{H}\in\R^{m\times n}$ the \textbf{measurement matrix}, $\mat{Q}$ the
\textbf{process noise covariance}\index{process noise} and $\mat{R}$ the
\textbf{measurement noise covariance}\index{measurement noise}. The noise
vectors $\vect{w}_1,\vect{w}_2,\dots$, $\vect{v}_1,\vect{v}_2,\dots$ and
$\state_0$ are mutually independent. The matrices may depend on $k$; the index
is dropped when they do not.
\end{definition}

$\mat{F}$ carries the deterministic part of the motion (a drone keeps its
velocity), $\vect{w}_k$ collects what the model cannot know (the intruder's
accelerations) and $\mat{Q}$ says how large these unknowns are per step;
$\mat{H}$ selects the linear combinations of the state that the sensor sees
and $\mat{R}$ says how noisy it is. The independence assumption is what makes
a recursion possible: once the current state is known, the past measurements
carry no further information about the future.

\begin{notebox}{Notation: $\mat{F}$ and $\mat{H}$, or $\mat{A}$ and $\mat{C}$?}
This book writes the transition matrix as $\mat{F}$ and the measurement
matrix as $\mat{H}$, the convention of the tracking literature
\cite{barshalom2001estimation}. Control texts, and the model predictive
controller of \cref{ch:ch21}, write the same matrices as $\mat{A}$ and
$\mat{C}$; the double-integrator model of \cref{ch:ch02} uses $\mat{A}$ and
$\mat{B}$; \textcite{thrun2005probabilistic} use $\mat{A}$, $\mat{B}$,
$\mat{C}$, and \textcite{welch1995introduction} use $\mat{A}$ and $\mat{H}$.
Beware also that $\mat{Q}$ and $\mat{R}$ are noise covariances here but cost
weights in \cref{ch:ch21}; the notation table in the front matter lists both
meanings.
\end{notebox}

\subsection{What the filter computes}

\begin{definition}[Filtering, prediction, estimate]
\label{def:ch18-filtering}
Write $\meas_{1:k}=(\meas_1,\dots,\meas_k)$ for the measurements received up
to step $k$. The \textbf{filtering problem}\index{filtering problem} is to
compute, at every step, the posterior density $p(\state_k\mid\meas_{1:k})$.
Under \cref{def:ch18-model} this density is Gaussian, and the filter
represents it by its mean and covariance, the \textbf{estimate}
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\state}_k=\E[\state_k\mid\meas_{1:k}],\qquad
  \mat{P}_k=\Cov(\state_k\mid\meas_{1:k}).
  \label{eq:ch18-estimate}
\end{equation}
The density $p(\state_k\mid\meas_{1:k-1})$, before $\meas_k$ has been used,
is the \textbf{prediction}\index{Kalman filter!prediction}; it is Gaussian
too, with mean $\hat{\state}^-_k$ and covariance $\mat{P}^-_k$. As in the
notation table, the superscript minus marks quantities computed before the
current measurement is used.
\end{definition}

For general densities the recursive Bayes filter
\cite[ch.~2]{thrun2005probabilistic} propagates the posterior through the
motion model and multiplies it by the likelihood $p(\meas_k\mid\state_k)$,
two integrals without closed form; for the linear-Gaussian model both reduce
to matrix arithmetic, because of two facts about Gaussians.

\subsection{Two facts about Gaussians}

\begin{lemma}[Affine transformation and conditioning of Gaussians]
\label{thm:ch18-gaussian-facts}
Let $\state\sim\Normal(\vect{\mu},\mat{\Sigma})$ in $\R^n$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item If $\vect{y}=\mat{A}\state+\vect{b}+\vect{w}$ with
  $\vect{w}\sim\Normal(\vect{0},\mat{Q})$ independent of $\state$, then
  $\vect{y}\sim\Normal(\mat{A}\vect{\mu}+\vect{b},\;\mat{A}\mat{\Sigma}\mat{A}\T+\mat{Q})$.
  \item If $\vect{a}$ and $\vect{b}$ are jointly Gaussian,
  \[
    \begin{pmatrix}\vect{a}\\ \vect{b}\end{pmatrix}\sim
    \Normal\!\left(\begin{pmatrix}\vect{\mu}_a\\ \vect{\mu}_b\end{pmatrix},
    \begin{pmatrix}\mat{\Sigma}_{aa}&\mat{\Sigma}_{ab}\\ \mat{\Sigma}_{ba}&\mat{\Sigma}_{bb}\end{pmatrix}\right)
  \]
  with $\mat{\Sigma}_{bb}$ invertible, then $\vect{a}$ given $\vect{b}$ is
  Gaussian with
  \begin{equation}
    \E[\vect{a}\mid\vect{b}]=\vect{\mu}_a+\mat{\Sigma}_{ab}\mat{\Sigma}_{bb}\inv(\vect{b}-\vect{\mu}_b),\qquad
    \Cov(\vect{a}\mid\vect{b})=\mat{\Sigma}_{aa}-\mat{\Sigma}_{ab}\mat{\Sigma}_{bb}\inv\mat{\Sigma}_{ba}.
    \label{eq:ch18-conditioning}
  \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
Both facts are proved in \cref{ch:appB} --- (ii) is the conditioning theorem
there, whose covariance is the Schur complement of $\mat{\Sigma}_{bb}$ --- and
in \textcite[ch.~3]{thrun2005probabilistic}.

Fact (i) is the predict step in disguise: the motion model
\cref{eq:ch18-motion} is an affine map of $\state_{k-1}$ plus independent
noise. Fact (ii) is the update step: the state and the measurement are
jointly Gaussian, and conditioning on the observed $\meas_k$ gives the
posterior. Note that the conditional covariance in
\cref{eq:ch18-conditioning} does not depend on the value of $\vect{b}$: the
covariances of the filter can be computed before any measurement arrives,
which \cref{sec:ch18-prediction} exploits.

\subsection{Motion models for a drone}
\label{sec:ch18-models}

\begin{definition}[Constant-velocity model]
\label{def:ch18-cv}
The \textbf{constant-velocity (CV) model}\index{constant-velocity model} of a
drone in $d$ dimensions ($d=2$ or $3$) has the state $\state=(\pos,\vel)\in\R^{2d}$,
position and velocity, and, for a sampling interval $\dt$,
\begin{equation}
  \mat{F}=\begin{pmatrix}\mat{I}_d&\dt\,\mat{I}_d\\ \mat{0}&\mat{I}_d\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  \mat{Q}=q\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac{\dt^3}{3}\mat{I}_d&\tfrac{\dt^2}{2}\mat{I}_d\\[3pt] \tfrac{\dt^2}{2}\mat{I}_d&\dt\,\mat{I}_d\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  \mat{H}=\begin{pmatrix}\mat{I}_d&\mat{0}\end{pmatrix}\ \text{ or }\ \mat{H}=\mat{I}_{2d},
  \label{eq:ch18-cv}
\end{equation}
where $q>0$ is the \textbf{white-noise-acceleration intensity}\index{white-noise acceleration}
in $\mathrm{m^2/s^3}$. The first $\mat{H}$ describes a sensor that measures
positions only, the second one that measures positions and velocities;
$\mat{R}$ is then $\sigma_p^2\mat{I}_d$, or
$\diag(\sigma_p^2\mat{I}_d,\sigma_v^2\mat{I}_d)$ for a sensor with independent
errors of standard deviation $\sigma_p$ in position and $\sigma_v$ in velocity.
\end{definition}

$\mat{F}$ is the double-integrator matrix of \cref{ch:ch02} with the
acceleration removed: $\pos_k=\pos_{k-1}+\dt\,\vel_{k-1}$ and
$\vel_k=\vel_{k-1}$. The intruder does accelerate, of course; the model treats
its acceleration as noise, and the shape of $\mat{Q}$ is what makes that
treatment consistent.

\begin{proposition}[Process noise of the constant-velocity model]
\label{thm:ch18-cv-q}
Suppose that between two samples the acceleration of the drone along one axis
is a continuous-time white noise $a(t)$ with $\E[a(t)]=0$ and
$\E[a(t)a(s)]=q\,\delta(t-s)$. Then the disturbance of position and velocity
along that axis over a step of length $\dt$ has covariance
$q\begin{psmallmatrix}\dt^3/3&\dt^2/2\\ \dt^2/2&\dt\end{psmallmatrix}$, and
the disturbances of different axes are independent, which gives the $\mat{Q}$
of \cref{eq:ch18-cv}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Integrate the continuous-time model $\dot p=v$, $\dot v=a(t)$ over $[0,\dt]$
along one axis. A unit impulse of acceleration at time $\tau\in[0,\dt]$ changes
the velocity at time $\dt$ by $1$ and the position by the remaining time
$\dt-\tau$, because it acts on the velocity, which then integrates into the
position. Superposing all instants,
\[
  \vect{w}=\begin{pmatrix}w_p\\ w_v\end{pmatrix}
  =\int_0^{\dt}\begin{pmatrix}\dt-\tau\\ 1\end{pmatrix}a(\tau)\,d\tau .
\]
Its covariance is
$\E[\vect{w}\vect{w}\T]=\int_0^{\dt}\!\!\int_0^{\dt}
\begin{psmallmatrix}\dt-\tau\\1\end{psmallmatrix}
\begin{psmallmatrix}\dt-\tau'&1\end{psmallmatrix}\E[a(\tau)a(\tau')]\,d\tau\,d\tau'$,
and the delta function collapses the double integral to a single one. With
$s=\dt-\tau$,
\[
  \E[\vect{w}\vect{w}\T]=q\int_0^{\dt}\begin{pmatrix}s^2&s\\ s&1\end{pmatrix}ds
  =q\begin{pmatrix}\dt^3/3&\dt^2/2\\ \dt^2/2&\dt\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Independence across axes follows from independent accelerations along the
axes. In matrix form, $\vect{w}=\int_0^{\dt}e^{\mat{A}(\dt-\tau)}\mat{G}\,a(\tau)\,d\tau$
with $\mat{A}=\begin{psmallmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{psmallmatrix}$ and
$\mat{G}=(0,1)\T$, and $e^{\mat{A}s}=\mat{I}+s\mat{A}$ because
$\mat{A}^2=\mat{0}$; the self-test of \code{ch18\_kalman.py} checks the closed
form against this integral numerically.
\end{proof}

Two features of $\mat{Q}$ matter in practice. First, $q$ is a \emph{rate}: it
does not change when the sampling interval changes, because $\mat{Q}$ carries
all the dependence on $\dt$; a sensor that suddenly reports at $20$~Hz
instead of $10$~Hz needs a new $\mat{Q}$ but the same $q$. Second, the
off-diagonal term couples position and velocity, because an unknown
acceleration moves both in the same direction; a diagonal $\mat{Q}$ is a
common shortcut and a mild error, $\mat{Q}=q\,\mat{I}$ regardless of $\dt$ a
common shortcut and a serious one (\cref{sec:ch18-implementation}). As a
starting value, if the intruder's accelerations have magnitude about
$a_{\max}$ and persist for about $T_a$ seconds, take $q\approx a_{\max}^2T_a$,
so that the model's velocity uncertainty after $T_a$, $\sqrt{qT_a}$, matches
the velocity change $a_{\max}T_a$ of a real manoeuvre; \cref{sec:ch18-tuning}
refines the value from data.

\paragraph{The constant-acceleration model.}
When the intruder manoeuvres persistently --- long turns, sustained climbs
--- a model that carries the acceleration as a state reacts faster. The
\textbf{constant-acceleration (CA) model}\index{constant-acceleration model}
has the state $(\pos,\vel,\acc)\in\R^{3d}$, the transition
$\pos_k=\pos_{k-1}+\dt\,\vel_{k-1}+\tfrac12\dt^2\acc_{k-1}$,
$\vel_k=\vel_{k-1}+\dt\,\acc_{k-1}$, $\acc_k=\acc_{k-1}$, a
position-selecting $\mat{H}$, and a white-noise \emph{jerk} of intensity $q$
(in $\mathrm{m^2/s^5}$) as its process noise; the derivation of
\cref{thm:ch18-cv-q} with the vector $\bigl((\dt-\tau)^2/2,\ \dt-\tau,\ 1\bigr)\T$
gives a per-axis $\mat{Q}$ with entries $q\dt^5/20$, $q\dt^4/8$, $q\dt^3/6$
in the first row, $q\dt^3/3$, $q\dt^2/2$ in the second and $q\dt$ in the
corner (\cref{exr:ch18-q}). The price is a larger state whose acceleration
the sensor sees only through two integrations, so the estimate is noisier
during straight flight. \textcite{barshalom2001estimation} treat both models
in detail, together with turning models, and \cref{ch:ch19} compares filters
on a turning target. This chapter uses the CV model throughout;
\code{ch18\_kalman.py} builds both.

\section{The algorithm}
\label{sec:ch18-algorithm}

The filter is the two facts of \cref{thm:ch18-gaussian-facts} applied in
turn, once per measurement. This section derives the two steps, explains the
gain, and writes the result as pseudocode.

\subsection{The predict step}
\label{sec:ch18-predict}

Suppose that after step $k-1$ the belief is
$\state_{k-1}\mid\meas_{1:k-1}\sim\Normal(\hat{\state}_{k-1},\mat{P}_{k-1})$.
The motion model \cref{eq:ch18-motion} is an affine map of $\state_{k-1}$,
with $\mat{A}=\mat{F}$ and $\vect{b}=\mat{B}\ctrl_k$, plus the independent
noise $\vect{w}_k$. Fact (i) gives the prediction directly:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\state}^-_k=\mat{F}\hat{\state}_{k-1}+\mat{B}\ctrl_k,\qquad
  \mat{P}^-_k=\mat{F}\mat{P}_{k-1}\mat{F}\T+\mat{Q}.
  \label{eq:ch18-predict}
\end{equation}
The mean moves as the noise-free model says. The covariance is transformed by
$\mat{F}$ and then inflated by $\mat{Q}$, and the transformation is not a mere
rescaling: for the CV model along one axis, with
$\mat{P}_{k-1}=\begin{psmallmatrix}a&b\\ b&c\end{psmallmatrix}$ (position
variance $a$, velocity variance $c$, covariance $b$),
\begin{equation}
  \mat{P}^-_k=\begin{pmatrix}
    a+2\dt\,b+\dt^2c+q\dt^3/3 & b+\dt\,c+q\dt^2/2\\
    b+\dt\,c+q\dt^2/2 & c+q\dt
  \end{pmatrix},
  \label{eq:ch18-predict-cv}
\end{equation}
so the position variance absorbs the velocity variance ($\dt^2c$): a drone
whose velocity is uncertain has an uncertain position a moment later, even
if its position was known exactly. This coupling is what makes a position
sensor informative about the velocity in the update step.

\subsection{The update step}
\label{sec:ch18-update}

Now the measurement $\meas_k$ arrives. Before it is read, the state and the
measurement are jointly Gaussian given $\meas_{1:k-1}$: the state has mean
$\hat{\state}^-_k$ and covariance $\mat{P}^-_k$, and
$\meas_k=\mat{H}\state_k+\vect{v}_k$ is an affine function of it plus
independent noise. Fact (i) applied to the stacked vector
$(\state_k,\meas_k)$ gives the joint distribution
\begin{equation}
  \begin{pmatrix}\state_k\\ \meas_k\end{pmatrix}\Bigm|\meas_{1:k-1}
  \sim\Normal\!\left(
  \begin{pmatrix}\hat{\state}^-_k\\ \mat{H}\hat{\state}^-_k\end{pmatrix},
  \begin{pmatrix}\mat{P}^-_k & \mat{P}^-_k\mat{H}\T\\
                 \mat{H}\mat{P}^-_k & \mat{H}\mat{P}^-_k\mat{H}\T+\mat{R}\end{pmatrix}\right),
  \label{eq:ch18-joint}
\end{equation}
where the cross-covariance is
$\Cov(\state_k,\mat{H}\state_k+\vect{v}_k)=\mat{P}^-_k\mat{H}\T$ because
$\vect{v}_k$ is independent of $\state_k$. Conditioning on the observed value
of $\meas_k$ with fact (ii), $\vect{a}=\state_k$ and $\vect{b}=\meas_k$, is
the whole update step. Written out with the standard names, it reads
\begin{align}
  \vect{y}_k &= \meas_k-\mat{H}\hat{\state}^-_k, & &\text{innovation}\index{innovation}\label{eq:ch18-innovation}\\
  \mat{S}_k &= \mat{H}\mat{P}^-_k\mat{H}\T+\mat{R}, & &\text{innovation covariance}\label{eq:ch18-S}\\
  \Kgain_k &= \mat{P}^-_k\mat{H}\T\mat{S}_k\inv, & &\text{Kalman gain}\index{Kalman gain}\label{eq:ch18-gain}\\
  \hat{\state}_k &= \hat{\state}^-_k+\Kgain_k\vect{y}_k, & &\text{updated mean}\label{eq:ch18-update-mean}\\
  \mat{P}_k &= \mat{P}^-_k-\Kgain_k\mat{S}_k\Kgain_k\T=(\mat{I}-\Kgain_k\mat{H})\mat{P}^-_k, & &\text{updated covariance.}\label{eq:ch18-update-cov}
\end{align}
The innovation is the part of the measurement that the prediction did not
already know: if the sensor reported exactly what the filter expected,
$\vect{y}_k=\vect{0}$ and the mean does not move. Its covariance $\mat{S}_k$
is what the filter \emph{believes} the innovation's spread to be, and the two
together are the raw material of every diagnostic in \cref{sec:ch18-tuning}.
The two expressions for $\mat{P}_k$ agree because
$\Kgain_k\mat{S}_k\Kgain_k\T=\mat{P}^-_k\mat{H}\T\mat{S}_k\inv\mat{H}\mat{P}^-_k=\Kgain_k\mat{H}\mat{P}^-_k$.

\paragraph{The Joseph form.}
A third expression for the updated covariance, the \textbf{Joseph
form}\index{Joseph form}\index{Kalman filter!Joseph form},
\begin{equation}
  \mat{P}_k=(\mat{I}-\Kgain_k\mat{H})\mat{P}^-_k(\mat{I}-\Kgain_k\mat{H})\T+\Kgain_k\mat{R}\Kgain_k\T,
  \label{eq:ch18-joseph}
\end{equation}
is the one to implement. Expanding it gives
$(\mat{I}-\Kgain\mat{H})\mat{P}^--\mat{P}^-\mat{H}\T\Kgain\T+\Kgain\mat{S}\Kgain\T$
for any matrix $\Kgain$, and the last two terms cancel exactly when
$\Kgain=\mat{P}^-\mat{H}\T\mat{S}\inv$, so \cref{eq:ch18-joseph} equals
\cref{eq:ch18-update-cov} for the Kalman gain. The difference is what happens
when $\Kgain$ is \emph{not} exactly the Kalman gain, because of rounding or
because a fixed approximate gain is used on purpose: the Joseph form is the
covariance of the error
$\state_k-\hat{\state}_k=(\mat{I}-\Kgain\mat{H})(\state_k-\hat{\state}^-_k)-\Kgain\vect{v}_k$
for \emph{any} gain, a sum of two symmetric positive semidefinite matrices
and hence symmetric positive semidefinite whatever $\Kgain$ is, while the
short form can lose symmetry and even positive definiteness (the self-test
constructs such a case). \Cref{sec:ch18-properties} uses the same expansion
to prove that the Kalman gain is the best gain.

\subsection{The gain as a weighting between prediction and measurement}
\label{sec:ch18-gain}

Take the simplest case, a scalar position measured directly: $n=m=1$,
$\mat{H}=1$, prediction $\hat x^-$ with variance $P^-$, measurement $z$ with
variance $R$. Then $S=P^-+R$ and
\begin{equation}
  K=\frac{P^-}{P^-+R}\in(0,1),\qquad
  \hat x=(1-K)\,\hat x^-+K\,z,\qquad
  \frac1P=\frac1{P^-}+\frac1R .
  \label{eq:ch18-gain-1d}
\end{equation}
The updated mean is a weighted average of the prediction and the measurement,
and the weights are proportional to the \emph{precisions} $1/P^-$ and $1/R$:
$K=(1/R)/(1/P^-+1/R)$. The precision of the result is the sum of the two
precisions, the weighted-average rule of \cref{sec:ch18-intuition}.
Three limits are worth remembering. If the sensor is perfect, $R\to0$, then
$K\to1$ and the filter simply takes the measurement. If the prediction is
certain, $P^-\to0$, then $K\to0$ and the measurement is ignored. If the
measurement is missing, treat it as infinitely noisy, $R\to\infty$: $K\to0$
and $P=P^-$, so a missing measurement is handled by skipping the update
(\cref{sec:ch18-missing}).

\begin{example}[One-dimensional update]
\label{ex:ch18-1d}
The prediction says the intruder is at $\hat x^-=2$~m with variance $P^-=1$~m$^2$
(standard deviation $1$~m); the sensor reports $z=3$~m with variance
$R=0.25$~m$^2$ (standard deviation $0.5$~m). Then $S=1.25$, $K=1/1.25=0.8$,
the innovation is $y=1$~m, and
\[
  \hat x=2+0.8\cdot1=2.8~\mathrm{m},\qquad P=(1-0.8)\cdot1=0.2~\mathrm{m^2}
  \quad(\text{standard deviation }0.447~\mathrm{m}).
\]
The precision form gives the same numbers: $1/P=1+4=5$ and
$\hat x=P\,(\hat x^-/P^-+z/R)=0.2\,(2+12)=2.8$. The estimate lies four times
closer to the measurement than to the prediction, because the sensor is four
times more precise, and it is more precise than either: $0.447$~m against
$1$~m and $0.5$~m. \Cref{fig:ch18-gain1d} shows the three densities. The
posterior is proportional to the product of the prediction density and the
likelihood, and the product of two Gaussians is a Gaussian whose peak lies
between the two and whose width is smaller than both.
\end{example}

  (grey, dashed) and the likelihood of the measurement (red, dotted) multiply
  to the posterior (blue), which is narrower than both and centred at

In the matrix case the same reading survives. Invert the updated covariance of
\cref{eq:ch18-update-cov} with the Woodbury identity of \cref{ch:appB}:
\begin{equation}
  \mat{P}_k\inv=(\mat{P}^-_k)\inv+\mat{H}\T\mat{R}\inv\mat{H},\qquad
  \hat{\state}_k=\mat{P}_k\bigl((\mat{P}^-_k)\inv\hat{\state}^-_k+\mat{H}\T\mat{R}\inv\meas_k\bigr).
  \label{eq:ch18-information}
\end{equation}
Precisions add, and the updated mean is the precision-weighted average of the
prediction and of the measurement mapped into state space: the
\textbf{information form}\index{information filter} of the update
(\cref{sec:ch18-variants}). The gain form \cref{eq:ch18-gain} is preferred in
practice because it inverts the $m\times m$ matrix $\mat{S}_k$ rather than
$n\times n$ matrices and works when $\mat{P}^-_k$ is singular.

One feature of the matrix gain has no scalar analogue. $\Kgain_k$ is
$n\times m$: it corrects \emph{every} component of the state, including
those the sensor does not see. For the CV model with a position sensor along
one axis, $\mat{H}=(1\;0)$, $\mat{P}^-=\begin{psmallmatrix}a^-&b^-\\
b^-&c^-\end{psmallmatrix}$ and $\mat{R}=r$,
\begin{equation}
  S=a^-+r,\qquad
  \Kgain=\frac1{a^-+r}\begin{pmatrix}a^-\\ b^-\end{pmatrix}
  =\begin{pmatrix}k_p\\ k_v\end{pmatrix}.
  \label{eq:ch18-gain-cv}
\end{equation}
The velocity is corrected by $k_v\,y$ with $k_v=b^-/S$: an innovation in the
position is attributed partly to a velocity error, in proportion to the
covariance $b^-$ between the two, which the predict step created in
\cref{eq:ch18-predict-cv}. This is how a filter fed with positions alone
produces a velocity estimate, and why that estimate is so much better than a
finite difference: the filter divides by the innovation covariance, not by
$\dt$.

\subsection{Pseudocode}
\label{sec:ch18-pseudocode}

\Cref{alg:ch18-kf} states the two steps as functions of the current belief;
\cref{alg:ch18-track} is the loop that a tracker runs on a measurement stream,
with the practical additions --- initialisation, missing measurements, gating
and prediction ahead --- that \cref{sec:ch18-tuning,sec:ch18-prediction}
explain in detail.

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
\caption{The Kalman filter: one predict step and one update step}
\label{alg:ch18-kf}
\KwIn{belief $(\hat{\state},\mat{P})$; model $\mat{F},\mat{B},\mat{H},\mat{Q},\mat{R}$; control $\ctrl$; measurement $\meas$}
\KwOut{the predicted belief $(\hat{\state}^-,\mat{P}^-)$, or the updated belief $(\hat{\state},\mat{P})$}
\Fn{\KalmanPredict{$\hat{\state}$, $\mat{P}$, $\ctrl$}}{
  $\hat{\state}^-\gets\mat{F}\hat{\state}+\mat{B}\ctrl$\label{alg:ch18-kf:pmean}\;
  $\mat{P}^-\gets\mat{F}\mat{P}\mat{F}\T+\mat{Q}$\label{alg:ch18-kf:pcov}\;
  \KwRet{$(\hat{\state}^-,\mat{P}^-)$}\;
}
\Fn{\KalmanUpdate{$\hat{\state}^-$, $\mat{P}^-$, $\meas$}}{
  $\vect{y}\gets\meas-\mat{H}\hat{\state}^-$\tcp*{innovation}\label{alg:ch18-kf:innov}
  $\mat{S}\gets\mat{H}\mat{P}^-\mat{H}\T+\mat{R}$\label{alg:ch18-kf:S}\;
  $\Kgain\gets\mat{P}^-\mat{H}\T\mat{S}\inv$\tcp*{solve $\Kgain\mat{S}=\mat{P}^-\mat{H}\T$; do not invert $\mat{S}$}\label{alg:ch18-kf:gain}
  $\hat{\state}\gets\hat{\state}^-+\Kgain\vect{y}$\label{alg:ch18-kf:mean}\;
  $\mat{P}\gets(\mat{I}-\Kgain\mat{H})\mat{P}^-(\mat{I}-\Kgain\mat{H})\T+\Kgain\mat{R}\Kgain\T$\tcp*{Joseph form}\label{alg:ch18-kf:cov}
  $\mat{P}\gets\tfrac12(\mat{P}+\mat{P}\T)$\tcp*{remove round-off asymmetry}\label{alg:ch18-kf:sym}
  \KwRet{$(\hat{\state},\mat{P})$}\;
}
\end{algorithm}

Lines~\ref{alg:ch18-kf:pmean}--\ref{alg:ch18-kf:pcov} are
\cref{eq:ch18-predict}; for an intruder $\mat{B}\ctrl$ is dropped.
Lines~\ref{alg:ch18-kf:innov}--\ref{alg:ch18-kf:S} compute the innovation and
its covariance, which the implementation also returns, because the tracking
loop needs them for gating and the engineer for tuning.
Line~\ref{alg:ch18-kf:gain} is \cref{eq:ch18-gain} written as a linear solve
with the symmetric positive definite $\mat{S}$: cheaper and better
conditioned than an explicit inverse, a habit that pays off in the larger
filters of \cref{ch:ch19}. Line~\ref{alg:ch18-kf:cov} is the Joseph form and
line~\ref{alg:ch18-kf:sym} keeps $\mat{P}$ exactly symmetric over millions of
steps (\cref{sec:ch18-implementation}). A predict step costs $\bigO{n^3}$ and
an update $\bigO{n^2m+m^3}$; for the CV model in 3D, $n=6$ and $m=3$, a few
hundred floating-point operations, so a filter runs at kilohertz rates on a
flight computer.

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
\caption{Tracking an intruder from a stream of position measurements}
\label{alg:ch18-track}
\KwIn{sampling interval $\dt$; CV model $\mat{F},\mat{H},\mat{Q}$ of \cref{eq:ch18-cv}; sensor noise $\mat{R}$; gate threshold $\gamma$ (\cref{tab:ch18-chi2}); prediction horizon $J$ steps}
\KwOut{after every step, the estimate $(\hat{\state}_k,\mat{P}_k)$ and the predictions $(\hat{\state}_{k+j\mid k},\mat{P}_{k+j\mid k})$, $j=1,\dots,J$}
\Fn{\KalmanTrack{stream of measurements $\meas_1,\meas_2,\dots$ (some missing)}}{
  wait for two measurements $\meas_a,\meas_b$ received one step apart\;
  $(\hat{\state},\mat{P})\gets$ \KalmanInitTwo{$\meas_a$, $\meas_b$, $\dt$, $\mat{R}$}\tcp*{\cref{eq:ch18-init2}}\label{alg:ch18-track:init}
  $\mathit{misses}\gets0$\;
  \ForEach{following step $k$}{
    $(\hat{\state}^-,\mat{P}^-)\gets$ \KalmanPredict{$\hat{\state}$, $\mat{P}$, $\vect{0}$}\label{alg:ch18-track:predict}\;
    \eIf{$\meas_k$ is missing}{
      $(\hat{\state},\mat{P})\gets(\hat{\state}^-,\mat{P}^-)$; $\mathit{misses}\gets\mathit{misses}+1$\tcp*{predict-only step}\label{alg:ch18-track:miss}
    }{
      $\vect{y}\gets\meas_k-\mat{H}\hat{\state}^-$; $\mat{S}\gets\mat{H}\mat{P}^-\mat{H}\T+\mat{R}$; $d^2\gets\vect{y}\T\mat{S}\inv\vect{y}$\label{alg:ch18-track:nis}\;
      \eIf{$d^2>\gamma$}{
        $(\hat{\state},\mat{P})\gets(\hat{\state}^-,\mat{P}^-)$; $\mathit{misses}\gets\mathit{misses}+1$\tcp*{gated out: treat as missing}\label{alg:ch18-track:gate}
      }{
        $(\hat{\state},\mat{P})\gets$ \KalmanUpdate{$\hat{\state}^-$, $\mat{P}^-$, $\meas_k$}; $\mathit{misses}\gets0$\label{alg:ch18-track:update}\;
      }
    }
    \If{$\mathit{misses}>M_{\max}$}{drop the track and restart from line~\ref{alg:ch18-track:init}\label{alg:ch18-track:drop}\;}
    deliver $(\hat{\state},\mat{P})$ and \KalmanAhead{$\hat{\state}$, $\mat{P}$, $J$} to the local layer\tcp*{\cref{eq:ch18-ahead}}\label{alg:ch18-track:deliver}
  }
}
\end{algorithm}

\Cref{alg:ch18-track} wraps the two steps in the loop that the prediction
layer of \cref{ch:ch24} runs. Line~\ref{alg:ch18-track:init} starts the track
from two measurements, which fix the position and, by differencing, a first
velocity with the honest covariance \cref{eq:ch18-init2}. The predict step
(line~\ref{alg:ch18-track:predict}) runs at every sampling instant; a missing
measurement (line~\ref{alg:ch18-track:miss}) leaves the prediction as the new
estimate, with its larger covariance. Line~\ref{alg:ch18-track:nis} computes
the squared Mahalanobis distance of the measurement from its prediction and
line~\ref{alg:ch18-track:gate} rejects implausible measurements, treating
them as missing rather than resetting anything. After $M_{\max}$ consecutive
misses (line~\ref{alg:ch18-track:drop}) the covariance has grown so much that
any measurement would pass the gate, and the track is dropped and restarted.
Line~\ref{alg:ch18-track:deliver} hands the estimate, its covariance and the
$J$-step prediction to the safety layer.

\section{A worked example}
\label{sec:ch18-example}

\begin{example}[Five steps of a two-dimensional constant-velocity filter]
\label{ex:ch18-five}
A drone flies in the plane with the true state $(1,0.5)$~m/s of velocity
starting at the origin, so that its true position at step $k$ is $(k,0.5k)$.
The sensor reports its position once per second, $\dt=1$~s, with independent
errors of standard deviation $\sigma_p=0.5$~m per axis, $\mat{R}=0.25\,\mat{I}_2$.
The filter uses the CV model with $q=0.1$~m$^2$/s$^3$ and starts from a
deliberately poor initial belief: $\hat{\state}_0=(0,0,0.5,0)$ --- the
velocity is half the true one and points along $x$ --- with
$\mat{P}_0=\mat{I}_4$. The five measurements, drawn once with a fixed seed and
rounded, are
\[
  \meas_1=(1.28,0.31),\ \meas_2=(1.71,0.92),\ \meas_3=(3.03,1.81),\
  \meas_4=(4.31,1.79),\ \meas_5=(5.34,2.88).
\]
\end{example}

Because $\mat{F}$, $\mat{Q}$, $\mat{H}$, $\mat{R}$ and $\mat{P}_0$ are
block-diagonal with identical blocks for the $x$ and $y$ axes, the
four-dimensional filter is two identical two-dimensional filters run side by
side, sharing one $2\times2$ covariance
$\begin{psmallmatrix}a&b\\ b&c\end{psmallmatrix}$ and one gain $(k_p,k_v)$,
and the whole computation fits on a sheet of paper. With $\dt=1$ the
per-axis recursion, from \cref{eq:ch18-predict-cv} and
\cref{eq:ch18-gain-cv}, is
\begin{align}
  \text{predict:}&\quad a^-=a+2b+c+\tfrac{q}{3},\quad b^-=b+c+\tfrac{q}{2},\quad c^-=c+q,\notag\\
  \text{update:}&\quad S=a^-+r,\quad k_p=\frac{a^-}{S},\quad k_v=\frac{b^-}{S},\notag\\
  &\quad a=(1-k_p)\,a^-,\quad b=(1-k_p)\,b^-,\quad c=c^--k_v b^- ,
  \label{eq:ch18-example-recursion}
\end{align}
with $r=\sigma_p^2=0.25$; the updated $a$, $b$, $c$ follow from
$(\mat{I}-\Kgain\mat{H})\mat{P}^-$, whose first column is scaled by $1-k_p$
and whose $(2,2)$ entry loses $k_v b^-$.

\paragraph{Step 1 by hand.}
Predict: $\hat{\state}^-_1=\mat{F}\hat{\state}_0=(0.5,0,0.5,0)$, and from
$a=c=1$, $b=0$:
$a^-=1+0+1+0.0333=2.0333$, $b^-=0+1+0.05=1.05$, $c^-=1.1$. The position
variance has doubled because the velocity was uncertain. Update:
$S=2.0333+0.25=2.2833$, $k_p=0.8905$, $k_v=0.4599$. The innovation is
$\vect{y}_1=\meas_1-\hat{\pos}^-_1=(0.78,0.31)$, so
\begin{align*}
  \hat{\state}_1&=(0.5+0.8905\cdot0.78,\ 0+0.8905\cdot0.31,\ 0.5+0.4599\cdot0.78,\ 0+0.4599\cdot0.31)\\
  &=(1.195,\,0.276,\,0.859,\,0.143),
\end{align*}
and $a=0.1095\cdot2.0333=0.2226$, $b=0.1095\cdot1.05=0.1150$,
$c=1.1-0.4599\cdot1.05=0.6172$. One position measurement has moved the
velocity estimate from $(0.5,0)$ to $(0.86,0.14)$, towards the true
$(1,0.5)$, through the covariance term $b^-$. The position standard
deviation is now $\sqrt{0.2226}=0.47$~m, slightly \emph{better} than the
sensor's $0.5$~m, because the prior still contributed a little.

\section{Properties: optimality, consistency, observability}
\label{sec:ch18-properties}

\begin{theorem}[The Kalman filter is exact and optimal for the linear-Gaussian model]
\label{thm:ch18-optimal}
Under \cref{def:ch18-model}, the posterior $p(\state_k\mid\meas_{1:k})$ is
the Gaussian $\Normal(\hat{\state}_k,\mat{P}_k)$ whose parameters are
produced by \cref{eq:ch18-predict} and
\cref{eq:ch18-innovation}--\cref{eq:ch18-update-cov}, started from
$(\hat{\state}_0,\mat{P}_0)$. Consequently $\hat{\state}_k$ is the
\textbf{minimum mean-square-error (MMSE) estimator}\index{MMSE estimator}
of $\state_k$: for every function $g$ of the measurements,
$\E\bigl[\norm{\state_k-g(\meas_{1:k})}^2\bigr]\ge\E\bigl[\norm{\state_k-\hat{\state}_k}^2\bigr]=\tr\mat{P}_k$,
and $\mat{P}_k=\E\bigl[(\state_k-\hat{\state}_k)(\state_k-\hat{\state}_k)\T\bigr]$
is the covariance of the estimation error.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Induction on $k$. The claim holds for $k=0$ by the choice of the initial
belief. Assume $\state_{k-1}\mid\meas_{1:k-1}\sim\Normal(\hat{\state}_{k-1},\mat{P}_{k-1})$.
The noise $\vect{w}_k$ is independent of $\state_{k-1}$ and of
$\meas_{1:k-1}$, so fact (i) of \cref{thm:ch18-gaussian-facts} gives
$\state_k\mid\meas_{1:k-1}\sim\Normal(\hat{\state}^-_k,\mat{P}^-_k)$ with
\cref{eq:ch18-predict}. The noise $\vect{v}_k$ is independent of everything
before it, so, conditionally on $\meas_{1:k-1}$, the pair
$(\state_k,\meas_k)$ is jointly Gaussian with the parameters
\cref{eq:ch18-joint}, and fact (ii) gives
$\state_k\mid\meas_{1:k}\sim\Normal(\hat{\state}_k,\mat{P}_k)$ with
\cref{eq:ch18-update-mean}--\cref{eq:ch18-update-cov}; conditioning on
$\meas_{1:k-1}$ and then on $\meas_k$ is conditioning on $\meas_{1:k}$. This
proves the first claim, and it identifies $\hat{\state}_k$ with the
conditional mean $\E[\state_k\mid\meas_{1:k}]$ and $\mat{P}_k$ with the
conditional covariance, which is the covariance of the error
$\state_k-\hat{\state}_k$ because the error has conditional mean zero.

For the MMSE property, let $g$ be any estimator and write
$\state_k-g=(\state_k-\hat{\state}_k)+(\hat{\state}_k-g)$. The second term is a
function of $\meas_{1:k}$, and the first has conditional mean zero given
$\meas_{1:k}$, so the conditional expectation of their inner product
vanishes, and
$\E\norm{\state_k-g}^2=\E\norm{\state_k-\hat{\state}_k}^2+\E\norm{\hat{\state}_k-g}^2\ge\E\norm{\state_k-\hat{\state}_k}^2$,
with equality only for $g=\hat{\state}_k$ almost surely.
\end{proof}

The optimality is over \emph{all} estimators, linear or not, because the
posterior is exactly Gaussian and the conditional mean is the MMSE estimator
for any distribution. When the noises are not Gaussian but still have zero
mean, the stated covariances and the stated independence, the posterior is no
longer Gaussian and a nonlinear estimator may do better --- but no
\emph{linear} one can.

\begin{proposition}[The Kalman gain is the best gain]
\label{thm:ch18-best-gain}
Let $\hat{\state}^-$ be an unbiased prediction with error covariance
$\mat{P}^-$, let $\meas=\mat{H}\state+\vect{v}$ with
$\vect{v}\sim(\vect{0},\mat{R})$ independent of the prediction error, and
consider the estimators $\hat{\state}(\Kgain)=\hat{\state}^-+\Kgain(\meas-\mat{H}\hat{\state}^-)$
for an arbitrary $n\times m$ matrix $\Kgain$. Their error covariance is
\begin{equation}
  \mat{P}(\Kgain)=\underbrace{\mat{P}^--\mat{P}^-\mat{H}\T\mat{S}\inv\mat{H}\mat{P}^-}_{\mat{P}(\Kgain^*)}
  +(\Kgain-\Kgain^*)\,\mat{S}\,(\Kgain-\Kgain^*)\T,\qquad
  \Kgain^*=\mat{P}^-\mat{H}\T\mat{S}\inv,
  \label{eq:ch18-best-gain}
\end{equation}
with $\mat{S}=\mat{H}\mat{P}^-\mat{H}\T+\mat{R}$. Hence $\mat{P}(\Kgain)-\mat{P}(\Kgain^*)$
is positive semidefinite for every $\Kgain$, and the Kalman gain minimises
every diagonal entry of the error covariance, in particular its trace, the
mean-square error. No Gaussian assumption is needed: the recursion is the
\textbf{best linear unbiased estimator}\index{best linear unbiased estimator}
for any noise with the given second moments.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The error is $\state-\hat{\state}(\Kgain)=(\mat{I}-\Kgain\mat{H})(\state-\hat{\state}^-)-\Kgain\vect{v}$,
a sum of two independent zero-mean terms, so its covariance is the Joseph
form $\mat{P}(\Kgain)=(\mat{I}-\Kgain\mat{H})\mat{P}^-(\mat{I}-\Kgain\mat{H})\T+\Kgain\mat{R}\Kgain\T$,
which expands to
$\mat{P}^--\Kgain\mat{H}\mat{P}^--\mat{P}^-\mat{H}\T\Kgain\T+\Kgain\mat{S}\Kgain\T$.
Expanding $(\Kgain-\Kgain^*)\mat{S}(\Kgain-\Kgain^*)\T$ with
$\Kgain^*\mat{S}=\mat{P}^-\mat{H}\T$ gives
$\Kgain\mat{S}\Kgain\T-\Kgain\mat{H}\mat{P}^--\mat{P}^-\mat{H}\T\Kgain\T+\mat{P}^-\mat{H}\T\mat{S}\inv\mat{H}\mat{P}^-$,
and subtracting the two expressions leaves exactly
$\mat{P}^--\mat{P}^-\mat{H}\T\mat{S}\inv\mat{H}\mat{P}^-$, which is
\cref{eq:ch18-best-gain}. The last term of \cref{eq:ch18-best-gain} is
positive semidefinite because $\mat{S}$ is, and it vanishes only for
$\Kgain=\Kgain^*$ since $\mat{S}\succ\mat{0}$ when $\mat{R}\succ\mat{0}$.
\end{proof}

This is the argument of Kalman's original paper, which derives the filter by
orthogonal projection and never mentions Gaussians \cite{kalman1960new}; the
self-test verifies \cref{eq:ch18-best-gain} for a gain $50\%$ too large.

\begin{proposition}[Data-independent covariance and steady state]
\label{thm:ch18-steady}
The sequences $\mat{P}^-_k$, $\mat{S}_k$, $\Kgain_k$ and $\mat{P}_k$ are
determined by $\mat{F},\mat{H},\mat{Q},\mat{R}$ and $\mat{P}_0$ alone and can
be computed before any measurement arrives. If the model is time-invariant,
$(\mat{F},\mat{H})$ is observable and $\mat{Q}\succ\mat{0}$, then
$\mat{P}^-_k$ converges for every $\mat{P}_0\succeq\mat{0}$ to the unique
positive definite solution $\mat{P}^-_\infty$ of the \textbf{discrete
algebraic Riccati equation}\index{Riccati equation}
\begin{equation}
  \mat{P}^-_\infty=\mat{F}\mat{P}^-_\infty\mat{F}\T+\mat{Q}
  -\mat{F}\mat{P}^-_\infty\mat{H}\T\bigl(\mat{H}\mat{P}^-_\infty\mat{H}\T+\mat{R}\bigr)\inv\mat{H}\mat{P}^-_\infty\mat{F}\T,
  \label{eq:ch18-riccati}
\end{equation}
the gain converges to $\Kgain_\infty=\mat{P}^-_\infty\mat{H}\T\mat{S}_\infty\inv$,
and the filter with this constant gain is stable.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The measurement enters \cref{eq:ch18-predict} and
\cref{eq:ch18-S}--\cref{eq:ch18-update-cov} only through the mean.
Substituting the update into the predict step gives the Riccati recursion
whose fixed points solve \cref{eq:ch18-riccati}; observability bounds
$\mat{P}^-_k$ from above, $\mat{Q}\succ\mat{0}$ from below, and a
monotonicity argument gives convergence and uniqueness
\cite[ch.~5]{barshalom2001estimation}. The conditions weaken to
detectability of $(\mat{F},\mat{H})$ and stabilisability of
$(\mat{F},\mat{Q}^{1/2})$.
\end{proof}


\paragraph{Observability.}
The pair $(\mat{F},\mat{H})$ is \textbf{observable}\index{observability} if
the observability matrix
\[
  \mathcal{O}=\begin{pmatrix}\mat{H}\\ \mat{H}\mat{F}\\ \vdots\\ \mat{H}\mat{F}^{n-1}\end{pmatrix}
\]
has rank $n$: $n$ consecutive noise-free measurements then determine the
state. For the CV model along one axis with a position sensor, $\mat{H}=(1\;0)$
and $\mat{H}\mat{F}=(1\;\dt)$: rank $2$, observable, and the filter can
recover the velocity from positions. With a velocity-only sensor,
$\mat{H}=(0\;1)$ and $\mat{H}\mat{F}=(0\;1)$: rank $1$. The velocity is then
estimated well, but the position is a velocity integrated from an unknown
start, and its variance grows without bound (the self-test runs this case
for $300$ steps). A filter on an unobservable model does not fail loudly: it
runs, reports a mean, and the only warning is a covariance that never stops
growing. Check the rank once, when the model is built;
\code{observability\_rank} does it.

\paragraph{Complexity and memory.}
The filter is recursive: memory $\bigO{n^2}$ and time $\bigO{n^3}$ per step,
independent of $k$ (\cref{sec:ch18-pseudocode}); it is the batch
least-squares fit to all measurements so far, reorganised so that each
measurement is absorbed once and forgotten.

\section{Tuning, diagnostics, gating, and imperfect sensors}
\label{sec:ch18-tuning}

The theory assumes that $\mat{Q}$ and $\mat{R}$ are the true noise
covariances. In practice $\mat{R}$ is roughly known and $\mat{Q}$ is a
modelling choice, sensors drop reports and occasionally return garbage, and
there may be several intruders. \Cref{alg:ch18-track} already contains the
corresponding logic; this section explains it.

\subsection{What the two noise covariances do}
\label{sec:ch18-qr}

$\mat{R}$ is the easier of the two: a datasheet gives the sensor's error
standard deviation, and a static calibration --- a few hundred reports on a
target at a known position --- gives the sample covariance directly, with a
check that the errors are unbiased and uncorrelated in time. An $\mat{R}$
wrong by a factor of two costs a little accuracy, not the unbiasedness, and
the diagnostics below reveal it. $\mat{Q}$ is a statement about the intruder,
which comes with no datasheet. The intensity $q$ says how fast the velocity
wanders: after a time $T$ without measurements the velocity uncertainty grows
by $\sqrt{qT}$, whence the rule of thumb $q\approx a_{\max}^2T_a$ of
\cref{sec:ch18-models}. Whatever the starting value, its effect is a
trade-off between two failure modes, shown in \cref{fig:ch18-tuning} and
\cref{tab:ch18-tuning}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{$\mat{Q}$ too small: sluggish.} The filter believes its own
  model, the gain becomes small, and the estimate follows the true motion
  with a lag: straight flight is tracked beautifully, but in a turn the
  estimate overshoots the corner and takes seconds to catch up. The
  innovations are large and, tellingly, \emph{correlated in time}.
  \item \textbf{$\mat{Q}$ too large: noisy.} The filter believes the sensor,
  the gain stays large, and the estimate copies the measurement noise; the
  velocity estimate in particular becomes almost as bad as a finite
  difference. The innovations are small and \emph{negatively} correlated:
  each measurement overcorrects, and the next one corrects back.
\end{itemize}
\Cref{fig:ch18-tuning} shows a drone that flies straight at $3$~m/s, turns by
$90^\circ$ in four seconds and flies straight again, tracked from the same
measurements ($\sigma_p=1$~m at $10$~Hz) with three values of $q$. The
intermediate value is best overall and nearly as good as the small one on the
straight legs; the small value pays for its smoothness in the turn, the large
value pays everywhere: a factor of $100$ above the best $q$ costs $56\%$ in
position error and turns the velocity estimate into noise, a factor of $100$
below doubles the error and misses the turn.

\subsection{The innovation as a diagnostic}
\label{sec:ch18-diagnostics}

You cannot compare the estimate with the truth, because the truth is what
you are estimating. You \emph{can} compare the innovations with what the
filter predicted for them: under \cref{def:ch18-model}, with the correct
$\mat{Q}$ and $\mat{R}$, the innovation sequence is zero-mean, Gaussian with
covariance $\mat{S}_k$, and \emph{white} --- $\vect{y}_k$ is uncorrelated
with $\vect{y}_j$ for $j\ne k$ --- because each innovation is the part of
the measurement that the past could not predict. A filter whose innovations
pass these tests is called \textbf{consistent}\index{Kalman filter!consistency};
the tests are, for a run of $N$ steps:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Normalised innovation squared (NIS).}\index{NIS}
  $d^2_k=\vect{y}_k\T\mat{S}_k\inv\vect{y}_k$ is chi-square with $m$ degrees
  of freedom, mean $m$ and variance $2m$, so the time average
  $\bar d^2=\frac1N\sum_k d^2_k$ should lie in
  \begin{equation}
    m\pm1.96\sqrt{2m/N}
    \label{eq:ch18-nis-band}
  \end{equation}
  with probability $95\%$ (normal approximation of $\chi^2_{Nm}/N$). A mean
  above the band says the innovations are larger than the filter expects, so
  $\mat{Q}$ or $\mat{R}$ is too small --- usually $\mat{Q}$, since $\mat{R}$
  is measured; a mean below the band says the opposite.
  \item \textbf{Whiteness.} The lag-one autocorrelation
  $\rho=\sum_k y_ky_{k+1}/\sum_k y_k^2$ of each innovation component should
  not exceed $1.96/\sqrt N$ in absolute value. Positive $\rho$ is the
  signature of a sluggish filter, negative $\rho$ of a nervous one, and the
  test is more sensitive to a wrong $\mat{Q}$ than the NIS: in
  \cref{tab:ch18-tuning} the $q=100$ filter passes the NIS test and fails
  whiteness by a wide margin.
  \item \textbf{Normalised estimation error squared (NEES),}\index{NEES} in
  simulation only: $(\state_k-\hat{\state}_k)\T\mat{P}_k\inv(\state_k-\hat{\state}_k)$
  has mean $n$ if the reported covariance is honest. The self-test checks it
  over $300$ Monte-Carlo runs.
\end{enumerate}
To tune $q$: start from the rule of thumb, run the filter on recorded data,
and move $q$ up by factors of two or three while the mean NIS is high or
$\rho$ positive, down while $\rho$ is negative, until both tests pass. A
sensor whose errors are correlated in time (a drifting camera calibration,
say) fails the whiteness test for every $q$; the remedy is a state augmented
with the drift (\cref{sec:ch18-variants}).

\subsection{Gating with the Mahalanobis distance}
\label{sec:ch18-gating}

Real sensors occasionally return a measurement that has nothing to do with
the intruder: a reflection, a bird, a corrupted packet. Fed to
\cref{alg:ch18-kf}, an outlier $40$~m from the predicted position moves the
estimate by $\Kgain$ times $40$~m in one step, the velocity estimate with it,
and the safety layer reacts to a phantom. The defence is the same statistic
as the first diagnostic, the squared \textbf{Mahalanobis
distance}\index{Mahalanobis distance!gating} $d^2=\vect{y}\T\mat{S}\inv\vect{y}$
of the measurement from its prediction, which under the model is
$\chi^2_m$-distributed.

\begin{definition}[Validation gate]
\label{def:ch18-gate}
For a gate probability $P_G$ let $\gamma$ be the $P_G$-quantile of the
$\chi^2_m$ distribution, $\Prob(\chi^2_m\le\gamma)=P_G$. The
\textbf{validation gate}\index{gating} of a track is the ellipsoid
$\set{\meas:(\meas-\mat{H}\hat{\state}^-)\T\mat{S}\inv(\meas-\mat{H}\hat{\state}^-)\le\gamma}$;
a measurement outside it is \textbf{gated out} and treated as missing.
\end{definition}

  distance exceeds $\sqrt\gamma$, about three standard deviations for $m=2$.}

The gate is an ellipse in measurement space centred at the predicted
measurement with the shape of $\mat{S}$: wide where the prediction is
uncertain, narrow where it is precise, and growing after missed measurements,
so that a track that has not seen its target for a while accepts measurements
from a larger region. The price of a gate is the fraction $1-P_G$ of genuine
measurements it rejects, the benefit is immunity to anything beyond
$\sqrt\gamma$ standard deviations; $P_G=0.99$ is a common compromise. In the
self-test a $50$-metre outlier is rejected with $d^2\gg9.21$ while the
ungated filter jumps by metres.

\begin{pitfall}[Resetting the filter on every outlier]
A tempting reaction to a gated-out measurement is to reinitialise the track
from it: ``the filter has lost the target''. It has not. A single outlier is
exactly what the gate is for; skip the update and let the covariance grow a
little. Resetting throws away the velocity estimate and the covariance, both
of which took seconds to build, and replaces them with a track started on the
outlier itself. Drop a track only after \emph{several consecutive}
rejections, when the gate has grown so wide that the track no longer says
anything (line~\ref{alg:ch18-track:drop} of \cref{alg:ch18-track}), and
report the count of rejections to the safety layer as a health signal.
\end{pitfall}

\paragraph{Several intruders: data association.}
With several tracks and several measurements per scan, the gate also answers
\emph{which measurement belongs to which track}, the \textbf{data
association}\index{data association} problem of \cref{fig:ch18-gating}.
\textbf{Nearest-neighbour association}\index{data association!nearest neighbour}
computes $d^2$ for every (track, measurement) pair, discards the pairs
outside the gates, and assigns the rest greedily in increasing order of
$d^2$, each track and each measurement at most once
(\code{nearest\_neighbour\_association} in the code). Leftover measurements
are outliers or new intruders (a track is started when two consecutive
leftovers are consistent with a plausible speed); leftover tracks take a
predict-only step. The rule fails when two gates overlap and both
measurements fall in the overlap --- two intruders crossing --- where a wrong
assignment swaps the tracks; the probabilistic data association filter and
multiple-hypothesis tracking are the standard answers
\cite{barshalom2001estimation}.

\subsection{Missing measurements}
\label{sec:ch18-missing}

A sensor that drops reports --- an occluded camera, a lost packet, an empty
radar scan --- needs no special rule: the predict step runs at every sampling
instant and the update only when a measurement is available
(line~\ref{alg:ch18-track:miss} of \cref{alg:ch18-track}). The estimate after
a miss is the prediction, whose position variance has grown by
$2\dt\,b+\dt^2c+q\dt^3/3$ per axis, and nothing is lost by this bookkeeping:
the larger covariance is the honest description of what the filter knows,
the safety layer receives a larger ellipse to keep clear of, the gate widens
so that the next genuine measurement is accepted, and the next update shrinks
the covariance back (the self-test checks all three, and a sensor that drops
$30\%$ of its reports still beats the raw measurements). Only a long run of
misses is a problem, the same as a long run of rejections: at some point the
track is a guess, and it is better to drop it and wait for two fresh
measurements.

\subsection{Track initialisation from two measurements}
\label{sec:ch18-init}

The filter needs a starting belief. One position measurement fixes the
position but not the velocity; two measurements $\meas_a,\meas_b$ received
$\dt$ apart fix both, the velocity as the difference quotient, and the
covariance of this linear combination follows from
$\Cov(\meas_a)=\Cov(\meas_b)=\mat{R}$
with independent errors,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\state}_0=\begin{pmatrix}\meas_b\\ (\meas_b-\meas_a)/\dt\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  \mat{P}_0=\begin{pmatrix}\mat{R} & \mat{R}/\dt\\ \mat{R}/\dt & 2\mat{R}/\dt^2\end{pmatrix}.
  \label{eq:ch18-init2}
\end{equation}
The velocity block is the finite-difference variance of
\cref{sec:ch18-motivation}, $(14~\mathrm{m/s})^2$ for $\sigma_p=1$~m at
$10$~Hz, and the off-diagonal block records that the position and velocity
errors are correlated, since both contain the error of $\meas_b$ --- the
correlation that lets the next measurement correct the velocity as well as
the position. Two-point initialisation\index{track initialisation} is exact
for the CV model, needs no tuning, and its huge velocity variance is honest:
the right panel of \cref{fig:ch18-raw} shows the velocity estimate starting
from a wild value and converging within about two seconds, the time the
filter needs to see a few metres of travel. \code{init\_two\_point}
implements \cref{eq:ch18-init2}.

\section{Predicting ahead: uncertainty propagation}
\label{sec:ch18-prediction}

The safety layer of \cref{ch:ch24} asks where the intruder \emph{will be}
during the next few seconds and how sure the tracker is. Both questions have
the same answer, the predict step run $j$ times without an update, and since
mean and covariance evolve independently of the measurements the result is
closed form:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\state}_{k+j\mid k}=\mat{F}^{j}\hat{\state}_k,\qquad
  \mat{P}_{k+j\mid k}=\mat{F}^{j}\mat{P}_k(\mat{F}^{j})\T+\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}\mat{F}^{i}\mat{Q}(\mat{F}^{i})\T .
  \label{eq:ch18-ahead}
\end{equation}
The notation $\hat{\state}_{k+j\mid k}$ reads ``the estimate of the state
at step $k+j$ given the measurements up to step $k$''; $\hat{\state}^-_k$ is
the case $j=1$ of the previous step. For the CV model the sum collapses. The
matrix $\mat{F}$ built for a step $\dt$, call it $\mat{F}(\dt)$, satisfies
$\mat{F}(\dt)^j=\mat{F}(j\dt)$, and the accumulated process noise satisfies
$\sum_{i<j}\mat{F}(\dt)^i\mat{Q}(\dt)\mat{F}(\dt)^{i\mathsf{T}}=\mat{Q}(j\dt)$
(the self-test checks this identity, which is the reason $q$ is a rate and
$\mat{Q}$ is not a free matrix). With the horizon $h=j\dt$,
\begin{equation}
  \mat{P}_{k+j\mid k}=\mat{F}(h)\mat{P}_k\mat{F}(h)\T+\mat{Q}(h),\qquad
  \text{position variance per axis: } a(h)=a+2hb+h^2c+\tfrac{q}{3}h^3 ,
  \label{eq:ch18-ahead-cv}
\end{equation}
where $(a,b,c)$ are the entries of $\mat{P}_k$ for that axis. The three
growing terms have three meanings: $2hb$ is the correlation between the
current position and velocity errors; $h^2c$ is the velocity uncertainty
turned into position uncertainty, the dominant term for horizons of a second
or two and the reason a good velocity estimate matters more than a good
position estimate for avoidance; and $qh^3/3$ is the manoeuvre the intruder
may make meanwhile, which dominates for long horizons and cannot be reduced
by any sensor.

  \centering
  \inputfigure{ch18/prediction}

  \cref{sec:ch18-experiment} at $t=10$~s. The $2\sigma$ ellipse of the

  (blue) at $0.5$, $1$, \dots, $3$~s ahead grow to $4.4\times3.8$~m, roughly

  (possible manoeuvres). The dashed line is the true path over the next three

  \label{fig:ch18-prediction}

\Cref{fig:ch18-prediction} shows the ellipses for the experiment of
\cref{sec:ch18-experiment}, from the estimate at $t=10$~s: the position
standard deviation grows from $(0.39,0.23)$~m to $(0.84,0.64)$~m one second
ahead, $(1.46,1.21)$~m at two seconds and $(2.19,1.91)$~m at three. These
ellipses are what the local layer inflates the intruder's radius with: if the
intruder has radius $r$ and the ellipse at horizon $h$ has largest standard
deviation $\sigma_{\max}(h)$ (square root of the largest eigenvalue of the
position block of $\mat{P}_{k+j\mid k}$), keeping the distance
$r+\kappa\,\sigma_{\max}(h)$ from the predicted position guarantees
separation with the probability of the $\kappa$-sigma ellipse --- and in two
dimensions the $2\sigma$ ellipse contains the true position with probability
$1-e^{-2}=86.5\%$ only; $95\%$ needs $\kappa=\sqrt{5.99}=2.45$, by the same
chi-square table as the gate. Since the inflation grows with the horizon, a
safety layer that plans over $3$~s must keep clear of a disc several metres
larger than the intruder: the concrete cost of sensor noise and unknown
intentions, and the reason \cref{ch:ch24} triggers avoidance on a short
horizon and leaves long horizons to the replanning layer. \Cref{ch:ch20}
uses the same propagated Gaussian as its constant-velocity baseline, and
\cref{ch:ch13} describes the radius inflation on the \orca side.

\begin{pitfall}[Confusing the estimation covariance with the prediction covariance]
$\mat{P}_k$ says how well the tracker knows where the intruder is
\emph{now}; it converges to a small steady value and never grows, however
long you track. $\mat{P}_{k+j\mid k}$ says how well anyone can know where the
intruder will be $j$ steps from now; it grows without bound in $j$, because
it contains the intruder's future decisions. A planner that inflates the
radius by the current $2\sigma$ of $0.4$~m when the collision is two seconds
away and the honest figure is $1.5$~m is over-confident by a factor that
grows with the horizon, and the collision rate in the experiments of
\cref{ch:ch25} shows it. The converse mistake, gating a measurement with
$\mat{P}_{k+j\mid k}$ instead of $\mat{S}_k$, accepts every outlier.
\end{pitfall}

\section{An experiment on simulated data}
\label{sec:ch18-experiment}

The script \code{code/figures/gen\_ch18\_tracking.py} runs four experiments
on the filter of \code{ch18\_kalman.py}; every number quoted in this
chapter comes from it. Experiment A, the one behind \cref{fig:ch18-raw},
\cref{fig:ch18-prediction} and the left panel of \cref{fig:ch18-experiment},

follows the CV model with white-noise acceleration of intensity

with independent errors of standard deviation $1$~m along $x$ and $0.5$~m
along $y$. The filter is initialised from the first two reports by
\cref{eq:ch18-init2} and uses the true $q$ and $\mat{R}$.

The results are those of \cref{sec:ch18-motivation}: the position RMSE
drops from $1.175$~m for the raw measurements to $0.524$~m, the velocity RMSE
from $16.8$~m/s for finite differences to $0.79$~m/s. The error bars are
honest: the final standard deviations are $0.39$~m along $x$, $0.23$~m along
$y$ and $0.48$~m/s in $v_x$, and the mean NIS over the run is $2.21$, inside
the $95\%$ band $[1.72,2.28]$ of \cref{eq:ch18-nis-band}. The ellipses in
the left panel are elongated along $x$, the axis measured twice as noisily,
and the true path threads through all of them.

Experiment B, the right panel, asks how much the tuning of $q$ matters: the
scenario is simulated $20$ times with fresh noise and each data set is
filtered with $17$ values of $q$ from $0.01$ to $100$, the true value being
$0.2$. The position RMSE has its minimum, $0.469$~m, at $q=0.178$, the grid
point nearest the truth; it is $0.477$~m at $q=0.1$ and $0.503$~m at $q=1$,
so a factor of five either way costs less than $8\%$; far away the price is
severe but still below the raw $1.126$~m: $0.691$~m at $q=0.01$, where the
filter is too sluggish to follow the wandering velocity, and $0.773$~m at
$q=100$, where it copies the sensor noise. The velocity RMSE is asymmetric
--- $1.02$~m/s at the optimum, $1.11$~m/s at $q=0.01$, $3.29$~m/s at
$q=100$ --- because an over-large $q$ lets the sensor noise straight into the
velocity, while an under-sized $q$ merely smooths a velocity that changes
slowly in this scenario (on a turning target, \cref{fig:ch18-tuning}, the
small-$q$ side pays instead). The mean NIS falls monotonically from $2.74$
at $q=0.01$ to $1.58$ at $q=100$ and crosses $m=2$ at $q\approx0.3$, next to
the truth: choosing the $q$ at which the mean NIS equals $m$, the rule of
\cref{sec:ch18-diagnostics}, lands within a factor of two of the optimum
without ever seeing the true trajectory.

\section{Variants and extensions}
\label{sec:ch18-variants}

\paragraph{Constant gain.}
By \cref{thm:ch18-steady} the gain converges; running the CV filter with the
constant gain $(k_p,k_v)$ per axis from the start gives the
\textbf{alpha--beta filter}\index{alpha-beta filter@alpha--beta filter},
$\hat p\gets\hat p^-+\alpha y$, $\hat v\gets\hat v^-+(\beta/\dt)\,y$ with
$\alpha=k_p$, $\beta=k_v\dt$: no covariance, no matrix arithmetic, the same
steady-state accuracy, but no transient and no covariance output for the
safety layer.

\paragraph{The information filter.}
Carrying $\mat{P}\inv$ and $\mat{P}\inv\hat{\state}$ turns the update
\cref{eq:ch18-information} into two additions and makes the predict step the
expensive one; it is the natural form when several sensors observe the same
intruder, since their contributions $\mat{H}_i\T\mat{R}_i\inv\mat{H}_i$ add
in any order and a silent sensor adds zero. With a diagonal $\mat{R}$ the gain
form achieves the same by processing the components of $\meas_k$ one at a
time as scalar updates.

\paragraph{Smoothing.}
Offline, the measurements \emph{after} step $k$ are available too, and the
Rauch--Tung--Striebel smoother\index{smoother!Rauch--Tung--Striebel}
\cite{rauch1965maximum} runs a backward pass over the stored filter outputs
that roughly halves the error in the interior of a recorded trajectory: the
right tool for cleaning the trajectories on which the predictors of
\cref{ch:ch20} are trained, not a tracker, because it needs the future
\cite{sarkka2013bayesian}.

\paragraph{Richer states and manoeuvring targets.}
Anything the noise model cannot absorb can be added to the state --- the
acceleration (the CA model), a slowly varying sensor bias, a time-correlated
disturbance --- at the price of a larger $n$, a slower response to what the
extra states must first learn, and a fresh observability check. For an
intruder that alternates between straight flight and turns, the interacting
multiple model (IMM) estimator runs a CV and a CA or coordinated-turn filter
in parallel and mixes them by their likelihoods \cite{barshalom2001estimation}.

\paragraph{Nonlinear models.}
A radar that reports range and bearing, a camera that reports pixels, a turn
model with an unknown rate: each breaks the linearity of $\mat{H}$ or
$\mat{F}$. The extended Kalman filter linearises around the current estimate,
the unscented filter propagates a handful of chosen points, and the particle
filter abandons the Gaussian altogether; \cref{ch:ch19} presents all three and
compares them on a turning target. Everything in this chapter survives with
Jacobians in place of $\mat{F}$ and $\mat{H}$.

\section{Implementation notes}
\label{sec:ch18-implementation}

\Cref{lst:ch18-core} is the heart of \code{ch18\_kalman.py}: a class that
holds the model and the belief, with \code{predict} and \code{update}
methods. The rest of the file builds the CV and CA models, initialises
tracks, simulates measurements with misses and outliers, runs the diagnostics
of \cref{sec:ch18-diagnostics}, associates measurements with several tracks
and predicts ahead; its self-test checks the worked example to four
decimals, the process-noise integrals, the identity
\cref{eq:ch18-best-gain}, gating, misses, observability and the consistency
statistics on a $2000$-step simulation, in about three seconds.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={The predict and update steps of the \code{KalmanFilter} class (from \code{code/ch18\_kalman.py}). \code{predict} accepts \code{F} and \code{Q} built for the actual time step; \code{update} returns the innovation, its covariance, the gain and the NIS, rejects the measurement when a gate probability is given and the NIS exceeds the chi-square threshold, and uses the Joseph form by default.},label={lst:ch18-core}]
    def predict(self, u=None, F=None, Q=None):
        """Predict: x^- = F x + B u,  P^- = F P F^T + Q.
        Pass F and Q built for the actual time step if dt varies."""
        F = self.F if F is None else np.asarray(F, float)
        Q = self.Q if Q is None else np.asarray(Q, float)
        self.x = F @ self.x
        if u is not None and self.B is not None:
            self.x = self.x + self.B @ np.asarray(u, float)
        self.P = symmetrize(F @ self.P @ F.T + Q)
        return self.x.copy(), self.P.copy()

    def update(self, z, R=None, gate_prob=None, joseph=True):
        """Update with measurement z.  With gate_prob, z is rejected (state
        unchanged) when its NIS exceeds the chi-square threshold."""
        R = self.R if R is None else np.asarray(R, float)
        y, S = self.innovation(z, R)
        d2 = float(y @ np.linalg.solve(S, y))
        K = np.linalg.solve(S, self.H @ self.P).T      # K = P H^T S^-1
        if gate_prob is not None and d2 > chi2_threshold(self.m, gate_prob):
            return UpdateResult(y, S, K, d2, False)
        self.x = self.x + K @ y
        I_KH = np.eye(self.n) - K @ self.H
        if joseph:   # valid for any K, keeps P symmetric positive definite
            self.P = I_KH @ self.P @ I_KH.T + K @ R @ K.T
        else:        # the short form, exact only for the optimal K
            self.P = I_KH @ self.P
        self.P = symmetrize(self.P)
        return UpdateResult(y, S, K, d2, True)
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Keep the covariance symmetric and positive definite.}
$\mat{P}$ is symmetric in exact arithmetic and slightly asymmetric after a
few thousand floating-point steps, and the asymmetry feeds back into the
gain and grows; symmetrising after every step (line~\ref{alg:ch18-kf:sym} of
\cref{alg:ch18-kf}, \code{symmetrize} in the listing) costs $n^2$ additions
and removes the problem. Positive definiteness is protected by the Joseph
form: the short form $(\mat{I}-\Kgain\mat{H})\mat{P}^-$, a difference of two
matrices, can go indefinite when $\mat{P}^-$ has entries of very different
sizes, such as a position variance of $10^4$~m$^2$ after a long gap next to a
velocity variance of $10^{-2}$~m$^2$/s$^2$. Solve
$\Kgain\mat{S}=\mat{P}^-\mat{H}\T$ rather than forming $\mat{S}\inv$.
Square-root implementations that propagate a Cholesky factor of $\mat{P}$
\cite{maybeck1979stochastic} are the remedy for filters with hundreds of
states or in single precision; for the six-state tracker of this chapter,
symmetrisation and the Joseph form suffice, and the self-test confirms that
$\mat{P}$ stays symmetric positive definite over every step of a
$2000$-step run.

\paragraph{Units and scales.}
Keep the state in SI units, metres and metres per second, so that $q$ is in
m$^2$/s$^3$ and $\mat{R}$ in m$^2$; a mixed state (metres and degrees, say)
is legal but makes $\mat{Q}$ and $\mat{P}$ unreadable and their tuning
guesswork. The gate threshold and the NIS band are unit-free, which is the
point of normalising by $\mat{S}$.

\paragraph{Time-varying sampling.}
Sensors do not deliver at exactly $10$~Hz. Timestamp every report, and build
$\mat{F}(\dt)$ and $\mat{Q}(\dt)$ from the \emph{actual} interval since the
last report before each predict step (\code{predict(F=..., Q=...)} in the
listing, \code{cv\_model} to build them); a nominal $\dt$ used with reports
that arrive early or late puts a bias into the velocity estimate that no
tuning removes. When the sensor is silent, predict at the planner's cycle
time with the same rule.

\begin{pitfall}[A process noise that does not scale with the step]
Setting $\mat{Q}=q\,\mat{I}$ or $\mat{Q}=\diag(q_p,q_v)$ with fixed numbers,
independent of $\dt$, is the most common Kalman bug in student code. It
works at the rate at which it was tuned and breaks when the rate changes: at
twice the rate the filter injects twice as much noise per second and becomes
nervous, at half the rate it becomes sluggish, and with jittering timestamps
it is inconsistent from step to step. The white-noise-acceleration $\mat{Q}$
of \cref{eq:ch18-cv} scales correctly because it is the integral of a
continuous-time noise over the step, and $q$ is a property of the intruder,
not of the sensor rate; a diagonal shortcut should at least scale the
position term with $\dt^3$ and the velocity term with $\dt$.
\end{pitfall}

\begin{pitfall}[Feeding finite differences to the filter as velocity measurements]
When the sensor reports positions only, it is tempting to compute
$(\meas_k-\meas_{k-1})/\dt$ and hand it to the filter as a velocity
measurement with $\mat{H}=\mat{I}$. Do not. The differenced ``measurement''
is a function of two position reports the filter is also using, so its error
is correlated with the position measurements of the same and the previous
step, which violates the independence assumption of \cref{def:ch18-model}:
the filter counts the same sensor error twice, reports a covariance that is
too small, and fails the NIS test. The filter already extracts the velocity
from the positions, optimally, through the gain $k_v$ of
\cref{eq:ch18-gain-cv}. Use a velocity measurement only when a sensor
measures the velocity independently --- a Doppler radar, or a broadcast
containing the intruder's own velocity estimate --- with its own $\sigma_v$
in $\mat{R}$, as \cref{exr:ch18-coding} does.
\end{pitfall}

\section{Where this fits in the drone system}
\label{sec:ch18-drone}

\begin{dronebox}
The Kalman filter is the \textbf{prediction layer} of the hybrid architecture
of \cref{ch:ch24}, the only layer that deals with agents the swarm does not
control. It \emph{receives} timestamped sensor reports about unknown drones
(positions, or positions and velocities) with their noise levels, and the
safety horizon $h$ from the decision logic. It \emph{maintains} one track per
intruder --- a CV filter with gating and two-point initialisation
(\cref{alg:ch18-track}), nearest-neighbour association when several intruders
are visible, and a health count of consecutive misses --- and \emph{delivers},
at every cycle, $(\hat{\state}_k,\mat{P}_k)$ and the predictions
$(\hat{\state}_{k+j\mid k},\mat{P}_{k+j\mid k})$ up to the horizon
(\cref{eq:ch18-ahead}). The local safety layer (\orca in \cref{ch:ch13}, DWA
in \cref{ch:ch14}) takes the predicted position and velocity as the obstacle
and inflates its radius by $\kappa\,\sigma_{\max}(h)$; the time-to-collision
trigger of \cref{ch:ch24} is evaluated on the same prediction; the
constant-velocity predictor of \cref{ch:ch20} is \cref{eq:ch18-ahead} itself,
and the learned predictors of that chapter take the filtered history as
input; the replanning layer (\cref{ch:ch05,ch:ch24}) receives the predicted
occupancy when a detour must be planned; and the health count tells the
decision logic when an intruder is no longer tracked. This is the Week~9
topic of the study plan (\cref{ch:appA}), whose milestone is exactly this
interface: a stable estimated state with a covariance instead of raw noisy
observations.
\end{dronebox}

\section{Summary}
\begin{summary}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The linear-Gaussian model $\state_k=\mat{F}\state_{k-1}+\mat{B}\ctrl_k+\vect{w}_k$,
  $\meas_k=\mat{H}\state_k+\vect{v}_k$, $\vect{w}_k\sim\Normal(\vect{0},\mat{Q})$,
  $\vect{v}_k\sim\Normal(\vect{0},\mat{R})$, describes a drone whose
  accelerations are unknown and a sensor whose errors are independent.
  \item The filter keeps $\Normal(\hat{\state}_k,\mat{P}_k)$ and alternates
  predict, $\hat{\state}^-=\mat{F}\hat{\state}$, $\mat{P}^-=\mat{F}\mat{P}\mat{F}\T+\mat{Q}$,
  and update, $\vect{y}=\meas-\mat{H}\hat{\state}^-$, $\mat{S}=\mat{H}\mat{P}^-\mat{H}\T+\mat{R}$,
  $\Kgain=\mat{P}^-\mat{H}\T\mat{S}\inv$, $\hat{\state}=\hat{\state}^-+\Kgain\vect{y}$,
  $\mat{P}=(\mat{I}-\Kgain\mat{H})\mat{P}^-$ (implemented in the Joseph
  form); both steps are the affine and conditioning properties of Gaussians.
  \item The gain is a precision weighting, $K=P^-/(P^-+R)$ and $1/P=1/P^-+1/R$
  in one dimension; through the position--velocity covariance a position
  sensor also corrects the velocity.
  \item The constant-velocity model has $\mat{F}=\begin{psmallmatrix}\mat{I}&\dt\mat{I}\\ \mat{0}&\mat{I}\end{psmallmatrix}$
  and $\mat{Q}=q\begin{psmallmatrix}\dt^3/3&\dt^2/2\\ \dt^2/2&\dt\end{psmallmatrix}$
  per axis; $q$ is a rate, and $\mat{Q}$ must be rebuilt when $\dt$ changes.
  \item For the linear-Gaussian model the filter is the exact posterior and
  the MMSE estimator, and the best linear estimator otherwise; the covariance
  sequence is data-independent and converges when the model is observable.
  \item Tune $q$ with the innovations (mean NIS in $m\pm1.96\sqrt{2m/N}$,
  white innovations); gate with the Mahalanobis distance and a chi-square
  threshold; treat gated-out and missing measurements alike as predict-only
  steps; initialise from two measurements; drop a track only after several
  consecutive misses.
  \item Predicting $j$ steps ahead gives $\mat{F}^j\hat{\state}_k$ and a
  covariance that grows without bound; its ellipses, not $\mat{P}_k$, are
  what the safety layer inflates the intruder's radius with.
\end{itemize}
\end{summary}

\paragraph*{Further reading.}
The filter is Kalman's \cite{kalman1960new}, derived by orthogonal projection
without a Gaussian assumption. The tutorial of Welch and Bishop
\cite{welch1995introduction} is the shortest complete introduction and the
source of the $\mat{A}$, $\mat{H}$ notation found in much code;
\textcite[ch.~3]{thrun2005probabilistic} derive the filter as a Bayes filter
with Gaussian beliefs, the route taken in \cref{sec:ch18-algorithm}, and
continue with the nonlinear filters of \cref{ch:ch19}.
\textcite{barshalom2001estimation} is the reference for tracking: kinematic
models and their process noise, consistency tests, gating, data association
and manoeuvring-target estimators. \textcite{sarkka2013bayesian} gives the
modern Bayesian treatment with smoothers, \textcite{maybeck1979stochastic}
the engineering view including the numerical forms, and
\textcite{rauch1965maximum} the smoother.

\begin{exercise}[\difficulty{1}]
\label{exr:ch18-scales}
Two independent unbiased readings $z_1$ and $z_2$ of the same quantity have
variances $\sigma_1^2$ and $\sigma_2^2$. (a) Among the estimates
$\lambda z_1+(1-\lambda)z_2$, find the $\lambda$ that minimises the variance,
and show that the minimum variance $\sigma^2$ satisfies
$1/\sigma^2=1/\sigma_1^2+1/\sigma_2^2$. (b) Show that this is exactly the
one-dimensional Kalman update \cref{eq:ch18-gain-1d} with $z_1$ as the

$z_1=2$, $\sigma_1=1$, $z_2=3$, $\sigma_2=0.5$ and compare with
\cref{ex:ch18-1d}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[\difficulty{2}]
\label{exr:ch18-gain}
Derive the Kalman gain without Gaussians. Let
$\hat{\state}(\Kgain)=\hat{\state}^-+\Kgain(\meas-\mat{H}\hat{\state}^-)$
and let $\mat{P}(\Kgain)$ be its error covariance, the Joseph form. (a)
Expand $\mat{P}(\Kgain)$ and differentiate $\tr\mat{P}(\Kgain)$ with respect
to $\Kgain$ (use $\partial\tr(\mat{K}\mat{A})/\partial\mat{K}=\mat{A}\T$ and
$\partial\tr(\mat{K}\mat{S}\mat{K}\T)/\partial\mat{K}=2\mat{K}\mat{S}$ for
symmetric $\mat{S}$) to find the stationary point. (b) Show with
\cref{eq:ch18-best-gain} that it is the global minimiser, not merely a
stationary point, and that it minimises every diagonal entry of
$\mat{P}(\Kgain)$, not just the trace. (c) Show that at the optimum
$\mat{P}(\Kgain^*)=\mat{P}^--\Kgain^*\mat{S}\Kgain^{*\mathsf{T}}=(\mat{I}-\Kgain^*\mat{H})\mat{P}^-$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[\difficulty{2}]
\label{exr:ch18-q}

constant-acceleration model, where the jerk is white noise of intensity $q$:
show that the disturbance of $(p,v,a)$ over a step is

property used in \cref{sec:ch18-prediction}: for the CV model,

induction $\sum_{i<j}\mat{F}(\dt)^i\mat{Q}(\dt)\mat{F}(\dt)^{i\mathsf{T}}=\mat{Q}(j\dt)$.
(c) Check both results numerically with \code{process\_noise\_numeric} and
\code{cv\_model}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[\difficulty{2}]
