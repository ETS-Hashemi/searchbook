\section{Why this matters for a drone swarm}
\label{sec:ch19-motivation}

The Kalman filter of \cref{ch:ch18} gives the prediction layer of the hybrid
architecture (\cref{ch:ch24}) a stable estimate of an intruder's state and a
covariance that says how much to trust it. It rests on two assumptions: the
intruder moves according to a \emph{linear} model, and the sensor reports a
\emph{linear} function of the state. Both fail in the situations that matter
most for collision avoidance.

Picture a survey swarm over a construction site. An unknown quadrotor enters,
flies a straight leg, banks into a turn around a crane, and straightens out.
A constant-velocity model extrapolates the last velocity and lags behind in
every turn. The natural model for a banked drone is the
\textbf{coordinated-turn model}\index{coordinated-turn model}, whose state
carries speed, heading and turn rate; it predicts the arc the drone actually
flies, but its position update contains the sine and cosine of the heading.
At the same time, the sensor our drone carries, a radar, a scanning lidar or
a camera with a rangefinder, reports how \emph{far} the intruder is and in
which \emph{direction}: a range and a bearing, the square root and the
arctangent of the position. The Kalman filter has no place for a sine or an
arctangent.

This chapter presents the three standard answers, in increasing order of
generality and cost. The \textbf{extended Kalman filter}\index{extended Kalman filter}
(EKF) linearises the models around the current estimate and runs the Kalman
equations on the linearisation. The \textbf{unscented Kalman filter}
\index{unscented Kalman filter} (UKF) avoids derivatives: it pushes a
handful of chosen points through the true functions and fits a Gaussian to
what comes out. The \textbf{particle filter}\index{particle filter} (PF) drops
the Gaussian assumption and represents the belief by thousands of weighted
samples, which lets it keep ``went left around the building'' and ``went
right'' alive at once. All three live in the prediction layer of
\cref{ch:ch24}: they consume raw measurements and deliver a state estimate
with its uncertainty to the local avoidance layer (\cref{ch:ch13,ch:ch14}),
to the trajectory predictor of \cref{ch:ch20} and to the prediction-aware
constraints of the planner. In the study plan (\cref{ch:appA}) this is the
second half of Week~9.

\Cref{sec:ch19-intuition} states the one problem all three filters solve
differently; \cref{sec:ch19-problem} fixes notation and the two models;
\cref{sec:ch19-ekf,sec:ch19-ukf,sec:ch19-pf} develop the three filters, each
with pseudocode and a worked example, and compare them on a turning target;
\cref{sec:ch19-properties} collects properties, costs and a decision guide;
\cref{sec:ch19-variants,sec:ch19-implementation,sec:ch19-drone} cover
variants, implementation and the place in the drone system.

\section{The idea in plain words}
\label{sec:ch19-intuition}

Every filter step does the same thing twice: it takes a Gaussian belief,
pushes it through a function, and describes the result by a mean and a
covariance. As long as the function is affine, $\vect{y} = \mat{A}\state +
\vect{b}$, the result is Gaussian with mean $\mat{A}\vect{\mu} + \vect{b}$ and
covariance $\mat{A}\mat{\Sigma}\mat{A}\T$ (\cref{ch:ch02}), and the Kalman
filter is exact. As soon as the function bends, the output is no longer
Gaussian: push a Gaussian in range and bearing through the polar-to-Cartesian
map and you get a \emph{banana}, a curved region no ellipse describes well
(\cref{fig:ch19-idea}). The three filters are three approximations of that
banana.\index{linearisation error}\index{banana distribution}

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \inputfigure{ch19/idea}
  \caption{Three ways of pushing a Gaussian belief (left) through a
  nonlinear function $f$ into the curved true output distribution (shaded
  banana). (a) The EKF replaces $f$ by its tangent at the mean and moves the
  ellipse as if $f$ were linear; the ellipse sits on the image of the mean,
  not on the mean of the banana, and its ends miss the curved tails. (b) The
  UKF pushes $2n+1$ sigma points through the true $f$ and fits an ellipse to
  the images; the fitted mean lies inside the banana. (c) The particle filter
  pushes a cloud of random samples through $f$ and keeps the cloud as the
  belief, curvature included.}
  \label{fig:ch19-idea}
\end{figure}

The EKF replaces the bent function by its tangent at the current estimate.
It costs one Jacobian per step and is exactly the Kalman filter when the
function is linear, but its answer is the Gaussian the \emph{tangent}
produces: the mean sits where the tangent puts it, and the more the function
curves within the width of the belief, the larger the error. The UKF keeps
the true function and changes the representation of the input: it picks
$2n+1$ \emph{sigma points} whose sample mean and covariance are exactly those
of the belief, maps each through the true function and reads mean and
covariance off the images. No derivative is needed, and the mean is correct
up to the second-order Taylor term, which is precisely the term the EKF
drops. The particle filter goes further: it represents the belief by many
random samples with weights, moves every sample through the true function
with its own random noise, and re-weights the samples by how well they
explain the measurement. Nothing forces the result to be an ellipse, so the
belief can be curved, skewed or split in two. The price is the number of
samples.

\begin{keyidea}
A nonlinear filter has to push a belief through a bent function. The EKF
bends the belief with the tangent (one Jacobian), the UKF pushes $2n+1$
representative points through the real function (no Jacobian, second-order
accurate mean), and the particle filter pushes thousands of random samples
through it (no Gaussian at all, but $\bigO{N}$ work and the need to
resample). Choose the cheapest one whose assumptions your intruder and your
sensor satisfy.
\end{keyidea}

\section{Problem statement and notation}
\label{sec:ch19-problem}

\begin{definition}[Nonlinear state-space model]
\label{def:ch19-model}
A \textbf{nonlinear state-space model}\index{state-space model!nonlinear} has
a state $\state_k \in \R^n$ at times $k = 0, 1, \dots$ spaced by $\dt$,
measurements $\meas_k \in \R^m$, a motion model $f$ and a measurement model
$h$ with
\begin{equation}
  \state_k = f(\state_{k-1}) + \vect{w}_k, \qquad
  \meas_k = h(\state_k) + \vect{v}_k,
  \label{eq:ch19-model}
\end{equation}
where $\vect{w}_k \sim \Normal(\vect{0}, \mat{Q}_k)$ and $\vect{v}_k \sim
\Normal(\vect{0}, \mat{R}_k)$ are independent of each other and over time, and
$\state_0 \sim \Normal(\hat{\state}_0, \mat{P}_0)$. The \textbf{filtering
problem}\index{filtering problem} is to compute, at every $k$, the posterior
$p(\state_k \mid \meas_{1:k})$ or at least its mean $\hat{\state}_k$ and
covariance $\mat{P}_k$.
\end{definition}

For $f(\state) = \mat{F}\state$ and $h(\state) = \mat{H}\state$ this is the
linear-Gaussian model of \cref{ch:ch18}, whose notation we keep:
$\hat{\state}_k^-$, $\mat{P}_k^-$ are the \emph{predicted} mean and covariance
before $\meas_k$ is used, $\hat{\state}_k$, $\mat{P}_k$ the \emph{updated}
ones, $\vect{y}_k$ the innovation, $\mat{S}_k$ its covariance and $\Kgain_k$
the gain. All three filters approximate the \textbf{Bayes filter}
\index{Bayes filter} \cite{thrun2005probabilistic},
\begin{equation}
  p(\state_k \mid \meas_{1:k-1}) = \!\int\! p(\state_k \mid \state_{k-1})\,
    p(\state_{k-1} \mid \meas_{1:k-1})\, \mathrm{d}\state_{k-1},
  \qquad
  p(\state_k \mid \meas_{1:k}) \propto p(\meas_k \mid \state_k)\,
    p(\state_k \mid \meas_{1:k-1}),
  \label{eq:ch19-bayes}
\end{equation}
which pushes yesterday's posterior through the motion model and then applies
Bayes' rule of \cref{ch:ch02} with the likelihood $p(\meas_k \mid \state_k) =
\Normal(\meas_k; h(\state_k), \mat{R}_k)$. For linear-Gaussian models both
steps have closed forms; for the models below they do not, and each filter
replaces them by an approximation: a linearisation, a set of sigma points,
or a set of samples.

\subsection{The coordinated-turn model}
\label{sec:ch19-ct}

A drone flying at constant speed with a constant bank angle follows a
circular arc. The \textbf{coordinated-turn model}
\index{coordinated-turn model!definition} has the state
\begin{equation}
  \state = (x, y, v, \psi, \omega)\T,
  \label{eq:ch19-ct-state}
\end{equation}
with position $(x, y)$, speed $v \ge 0$, heading $\psi$ (counter-clockwise
from the $x$ axis) and turn rate $\omega = \dot{\psi}$; $\omega > 0$ turns
left, $\omega = 0$ is straight flight (\cref{fig:ch19-models}). Integrating
$\dot{x} = v\cos\psi$, $\dot{y} = v\sin\psi$, $\dot{\psi} = \omega$ over one
step gives the exact discrete model
\begin{equation}
  f(\state) =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    x + \dfrac{v}{\omega}\bigl(\sin(\psi + \omega\dt) - \sin\psi\bigr)\\[1.2ex]
    y - \dfrac{v}{\omega}\bigl(\cos(\psi + \omega\dt) - \cos\psi\bigr)\\[1.2ex]
    v\\
    \psi + \omega\dt\\
    \omega
  \end{pmatrix}
  \ (\omega \ne 0), \qquad
  f(\state) =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    x + v\dt\cos\psi\\
    y + v\dt\sin\psi\\
    v\\
    \psi\\
    0
  \end{pmatrix}
  \ (\omega = 0),
  \label{eq:ch19-ct}
\end{equation}
where the second form is the limit of the first; an implementation switches
to it below a small threshold on $\abs{\omega}$. The model is nonlinear
through $v\sin\psi$, $v\cos\psi$ and the division by $\omega$. Bar-Shalom,
Li and Kirubarajan~\cite{barshalom2001estimation} write the same motion with
Cartesian velocities, $(x, \dot{x}, y, \dot{y}, \omega)$, which is linear in
the first four components when $\omega$ is known; we use the polar form
because its Jacobian is compact and because speed and heading are what the
avoidance layer wants.

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \inputfigure{ch19/models}
  \caption{The two models of the chapter. The intruder at $(x, y)$ flies with
  speed $v$ along its heading $\psi$ and turns at the rate $\omega$, so its
  path (dashed) is an arc of radius $v/\abs{\omega}$. The sensor at $\vect{s}$
  measures the range $r$ and the bearing $\varphi$. Neither the arc nor the
  pair $(r, \varphi)$ is a linear function of the state.}
  \label{fig:ch19-models}
\end{figure}

Real intruders change speed and turn rate. We model a random longitudinal
acceleration $a_k \sim \Normal(0, \sigma_a^2)$ and a random turn acceleration
$\gamma_k \sim \Normal(0, \sigma_\gamma^2)$, constant during the step, so that
\begin{equation}
  \vect{w}_k = \mat{G}(\state)\begin{pmatrix} a_k\\ \gamma_k\end{pmatrix},
  \quad
  \mat{G}(\state) =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \tfrac12\dt^2\cos\psi & 0\\
    \tfrac12\dt^2\sin\psi & 0\\
    \dt & 0\\
    0 & \tfrac12\dt^2\\
    0 & \dt
  \end{pmatrix},
  \quad
  \mat{Q}(\state) = \mat{G}(\state)
  \begin{pmatrix}\sigma_a^2 & 0\\ 0 & \sigma_\gamma^2\end{pmatrix}
  \mat{G}(\state)\T,
  \label{eq:ch19-ct-noise}
\end{equation}
the white-noise-acceleration construction of \cref{ch:ch18} applied along
the heading and to the turn rate. $\mat{Q}$ depends on $\psi$, so the filters
evaluate it at the current estimate; $\sigma_a$ and $\sigma_\gamma$ say how
abruptly the intruder may manoeuvre.\index{process noise!coordinated turn}

\subsection{The range--bearing sensor and angles}
\label{sec:ch19-rb}

\begin{definition}[Range--bearing measurement]
\label{def:ch19-rb}
A sensor at the known position $\vect{s} = (s_x, s_y)$ measures the
\textbf{range}\index{range--bearing measurement} $r$ and the \textbf{bearing}
$\varphi \in (-\pi, \pi]$ of the intruder,
\begin{equation}
  h(\state) =
  \begin{pmatrix} r \\ \varphi \end{pmatrix} =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \sqrt{(x - s_x)^2 + (y - s_y)^2}\\
    \operatorname{atan2}(y - s_y,\, x - s_x)
  \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad
  \vect{v}_k \sim \Normal\!\bigl(\vect{0}, \diag(\sigma_r^2, \sigma_\varphi^2)\bigr).
  \label{eq:ch19-rb}
\end{equation}
$h$ depends only on the position; the filter learns $v$, $\psi$ and $\omega$
indirectly, from how the position changes over time.
\end{definition}

A bearing error of $\sigma_\varphi$ radians at range $r$ displaces the
position by about $r\sigma_\varphi$ across the line of sight, $5\,\mathrm{m}$
for $2^\circ$ at $150\,\mathrm{m}$, more than a typical range error; the
error region is a thin arc, the banana of \cref{fig:ch19-idea}.

Headings and bearings live on a circle. The difference between the bearings
$3.10$ and $-3.10$ is not $6.20$ but $-0.083$. We write
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{wrap}(\theta) = \theta - 2\pi\Bigl\lceil \frac{\theta - \pi}{2\pi} \Bigr\rceil
  \in (-\pi, \pi]
  \label{eq:ch19-wrap}
\end{equation}
for the representative of $\theta$ on the circle, and every difference of
two angles in this chapter, in an innovation, in a residual between a sigma
point and a mean, or in a likelihood, is $\operatorname{wrap}(\theta_1 -
\theta_2)$; we write $\meas \ominus \hat{\meas}$ for a componentwise
difference whose angular components are wrapped.\index{angle wrapping}
The heading of an estimate is wrapped after every update.

\begin{definition}[Jacobian]
\label{def:ch19-jacobian}
The \textbf{Jacobian}\index{Jacobian} of a differentiable $g \colon \R^n \to
\R^p$ at $\state$ is the $p \times n$ matrix $\partial g/\partial\state$ of
partial derivatives $\partial g_i/\partial x_j$; it is the matrix of the best
affine approximation, $g(\state + \vect{d}) = g(\state) + \frac{\partial
g}{\partial \state}(\state)\,\vect{d} + \bigO{\norm{\vect{d}}^2}$.
\end{definition}

\section{The extended Kalman filter}
\label{sec:ch19-ekf}

\subsection{Linearise, then run the Kalman filter}

The EKF replaces $f$ by its tangent at the current estimate and $h$ by its
tangent at the prediction,
\begin{equation}
  f(\state) \approx f(\hat{\state}_{k-1}) + \mat{F}_k(\state - \hat{\state}_{k-1}),
  \quad
  h(\state) \approx h(\hat{\state}_k^-) + \mat{H}_k(\state - \hat{\state}_k^-),
  \quad
  \mat{F}_k = \frac{\partial f}{\partial \state}(\hat{\state}_{k-1}),\
  \mat{H}_k = \frac{\partial h}{\partial \state}(\hat{\state}_k^-),
  \label{eq:ch19-ekf-linearise}
\end{equation}
and applies the Kalman equations of \cref{ch:ch18} to the tangents, with the
true $f$ and $h$ for the means:
\begin{align}
  \hat{\state}_k^- &= f(\hat{\state}_{k-1}),
  & \mat{P}_k^- &= \mat{F}_k \mat{P}_{k-1} \mat{F}_k\T + \mat{Q}_k,
  \label{eq:ch19-ekf-predict}\\
  \vect{y}_k &= \meas_k \ominus h(\hat{\state}_k^-),
  & \mat{S}_k &= \mat{H}_k \mat{P}_k^- \mat{H}_k\T + \mat{R}_k,
  \label{eq:ch19-ekf-innovation}\\
  \Kgain_k &= \mat{P}_k^- \mat{H}_k\T \mat{S}_k\inv,
  & \hat{\state}_k &= \hat{\state}_k^- + \Kgain_k \vect{y}_k,
  \qquad
  \mat{P}_k = (\mat{I} - \Kgain_k \mat{H}_k)\,\mat{P}_k^-.
  \label{eq:ch19-ekf-update}
\end{align}
Only the covariances and the gain use the linearisation. The Jacobians are
evaluated at the current estimate, so they change from step to step and
depend on the data; unlike the Kalman filter, the EKF covariance cannot be
computed in advance, and a wrong estimate produces a wrong Jacobian and a
wrong gain (\cref{sec:ch19-ekf-failure}).
\index{extended Kalman filter!equations}

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
\caption{One step of the extended Kalman filter}
\label{alg:ch19-ekf}
\KwIn{previous estimate $\hat{\state}_{k-1}$, $\mat{P}_{k-1}$; measurement $\meas_k$ or \KwNil; models $f$, $h$ with Jacobians; $\mat{Q}_k$, $\mat{R}_k$}
\KwOut{updated estimate $\hat{\state}_k$, $\mat{P}_k$}
\Fn{\NlfEkfStep{$\hat{\state}_{k-1}$, $\mat{P}_{k-1}$, $\meas_k$}}{
  $\mat{F}_k \gets \partial f / \partial\state$ at $\hat{\state}_{k-1}$; $\hat{\state}_k^- \gets f(\hat{\state}_{k-1})$, heading wrapped; $\mat{P}_k^- \gets \mat{F}_k \mat{P}_{k-1} \mat{F}_k\T + \mat{Q}_k$\label{alg:ch19-ekf:predict}\;
  \If{$\meas_k = $ \KwNil}{\KwRet $(\hat{\state}_k^-, \mat{P}_k^-)$\tcp*{missing measurement: predict only}\label{alg:ch19-ekf:missing}}
  $\mat{H}_k \gets \partial h / \partial\state$ at $\hat{\state}_k^-$\label{alg:ch19-ekf:H}\;
  $\vect{y}_k \gets \meas_k - h(\hat{\state}_k^-)$; $\vect{y}_k[j] \gets$ \NlfWrap{$\vect{y}_k[j]$} for every angular component $j$\label{alg:ch19-ekf:innovation}\;
  $\mat{S}_k \gets \mat{H}_k \mat{P}_k^- \mat{H}_k\T + \mat{R}_k$; $\Kgain_k \gets \mat{P}_k^- \mat{H}_k\T \mat{S}_k\inv$\label{alg:ch19-ekf:gain}\;
  $\hat{\state}_k \gets \hat{\state}_k^- + \Kgain_k \vect{y}_k$, heading wrapped\label{alg:ch19-ekf:update}\;
  $\mat{P}_k \gets (\mat{I} - \Kgain_k \mat{H}_k)\,\mat{P}_k^- (\mat{I} - \Kgain_k \mat{H}_k)\T + \Kgain_k \mat{R}_k \Kgain_k\T$\tcp*{Joseph form}\label{alg:ch19-ekf:joseph}
  \KwRet $(\hat{\state}_k, \mat{P}_k)$\;
}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Walkthrough.}
Line~\ref{alg:ch19-ekf:predict} is the predict step, with $\mat{F}_k$ at the
\emph{previous} estimate, the point around which $f$ is linearised.
Line~\ref{alg:ch19-ekf:missing} handles a step without measurement, for
instance while the sensor loses the intruder behind an obstacle: the
prediction becomes the estimate, as in \cref{ch:ch18}. The update linearises
$h$ at the \emph{predicted} estimate (line~\ref{alg:ch19-ekf:H}); the
innovation on line~\ref{alg:ch19-ekf:innovation} has its bearing component
wrapped, without which an intruder near the $\pm\pi$ seam produces an
innovation of almost $2\pi$ (\cref{sec:ch19-ekf-example}).
Lines~\ref{alg:ch19-ekf:gain}--\ref{alg:ch19-ekf:update} are the Kalman gain
and the corrected mean, and line~\ref{alg:ch19-ekf:joseph} the Joseph form of
the covariance update, algebraically equal to $(\mat{I} -
\Kgain_k\mat{H}_k)\mat{P}_k^-$ but symmetric and positive definite under
rounding.

\subsection{The Jacobians of the two models}
\label{sec:ch19-ekf-jacobians}

\begin{proposition}[Jacobian of the coordinated-turn model]
\label{thm:ch19-ct-jacobian}
Let $\psi' = \psi + \omega\dt$. For $\omega \ne 0$ the Jacobian of
\cref{eq:ch19-ct} is
\begin{equation}
  \mat{F} =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    1 & 0 & \dfrac{\sin\psi' - \sin\psi}{\omega}
      & \dfrac{v}{\omega}\bigl(\cos\psi' - \cos\psi\bigr)
      & \dfrac{v\dt\cos\psi'}{\omega} - \dfrac{v\,(\sin\psi' - \sin\psi)}{\omega^2}\\[1.6ex]
    0 & 1 & -\dfrac{\cos\psi' - \cos\psi}{\omega}
      & \dfrac{v}{\omega}\bigl(\sin\psi' - \sin\psi\bigr)
      & \dfrac{v\dt\sin\psi'}{\omega} + \dfrac{v\,(\cos\psi' - \cos\psi)}{\omega^2}\\[1.6ex]
    0 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0\\
    0 & 0 & 0 & 1 & \dt\\
    0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 1
  \end{pmatrix},
  \label{eq:ch19-ct-jacobian}
\end{equation}
and for $\omega = 0$ the first two rows become
$F_{13} = \dt\cos\psi$, $F_{14} = -v\dt\sin\psi$,
$F_{15} = -\tfrac12 v\dt^2\sin\psi$, $F_{23} = \dt\sin\psi$,
$F_{24} = v\dt\cos\psi$, $F_{25} = \tfrac12 v\dt^2\cos\psi$.
\index{Jacobian!coordinated turn}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Rows three to five are the derivatives of $v$, $\psi + \omega\dt$ and
$\omega$. In the first row, $v$ enters linearly with the factor
$(\sin\psi' - \sin\psi)/\omega$; differentiating $\frac{v}{\omega}(\sin\psi'
- \sin\psi)$ with respect to $\psi$ uses $\partial\psi'/\partial\psi = 1$,
and with respect to $\omega$ the quotient rule on $1/\omega$ together with
$\partial\psi'/\partial\omega = \dt$. The second row is the same computation
for $-\frac{v}{\omega}(\cos\psi' - \cos\psi)$. The $\omega = 0$ entries are
the limits: expanding $\sin\psi' = \sin\psi + \omega\dt\cos\psi -
\tfrac12\omega^2\dt^2\sin\psi + \bigO{\omega^3}$ gives $x' = x + v\dt\cos\psi
- \tfrac12 v\omega\dt^2\sin\psi + \bigO{\omega^2}$, whose $\omega$-derivative
at $0$ is $-\tfrac12 v\dt^2\sin\psi$; \cref{exr:ch19-ct-jacobian} completes
the remaining limits, and the self-test of the chapter code checks every
entry against finite differences.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Jacobian of the range--bearing model]
\label{thm:ch19-rb-jacobian}
With $d_x = x - s_x$, $d_y = y - s_y$ and $r = \sqrt{d_x^2 + d_y^2} > 0$,
\begin{equation}
  \mat{H} = \frac{\partial h}{\partial \state} =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    d_x/r & d_y/r & 0 & 0 & 0\\[0.6ex]
    -d_y/r^2 & d_x/r^2 & 0 & 0 & 0
  \end{pmatrix}.
  \label{eq:ch19-rb-jacobian}
\end{equation}
\index{Jacobian!range--bearing}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The chain rule on the square root gives the first row. For the bearing,
$\varphi = \operatorname{atan2}(d_y, d_x)$ has $\partial\varphi/\partial x =
-d_y/(d_x^2 + d_y^2)$ and $\partial\varphi/\partial y = d_x/(d_x^2 + d_y^2)$
wherever $r > 0$, on every branch of the arctangent. The last three columns
vanish because $h$ ignores $v$, $\psi$, $\omega$.
\end{proof}

The first row of $\mat{H}$ is the unit vector from the sensor to the
intruder: a range measurement constrains the position along the line of
sight. The second row is the perpendicular unit vector divided by $r$: a
bearing constrains the position across the line of sight, and the further
the intruder, the less a radian of bearing is worth.

\subsection{A worked example: one EKF step by hand}
\label{sec:ch19-ekf-example}

\begin{example}[One EKF step with a range--bearing measurement]
\label{ex:ch19-ekf}
Our drone hovers at $\vect{s} = (0, 0)$ and observes an intruder once per
second ($\dt = 1\,\mathrm{s}$). After step $k-1$,
\[
  \hat{\state}_{k-1} = (-100,\ -4,\ 10,\ \pi,\ 0.3)\T, \qquad
  \mat{P}_{k-1} = \diag(4,\ 4,\ 1,\ 0.04,\ 0.01),
\]
in metres, metres per second, radians and radians per second: the intruder

standard deviations are $2\,\mathrm{m}$, $1\,\mathrm{m/s}$, $0.2\,\mathrm{rad}$

1\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ and $\sigma_\gamma = 0.1\,\mathrm{rad/s^2}$, the sensor

\subsection{Where the EKF fails}
\label{sec:ch19-ekf-failure}

\paragraph{Strong nonlinearity.}
\Cref{fig:ch19-linearisation} makes the linearisation error visible. A
Gaussian belief in range and bearing, $r \sim \Normal(100, 5^2)$ and
$\varphi \sim \Normal(\pi/2, 0.3^2)$, is pushed through $(r, \varphi) \mapsto
(r\cos\varphi, r\sin\varphi)$, which is what a filter does when it
initialises a track from one measurement and, mirrored, what the update does
to a belief with a wide cross-range uncertainty. The true output, estimated
from $2\,000$ samples, has its mean at $(-0.2, 95.6)$, and the exact mean is
$100\,e^{-0.3^2/2} = 95.6$: the samples lie on an arc of radius $100$, and the
average of points on an arc is \emph{inside} the arc. The linearised map puts
the mean at the image of the input mean, $(0, 100)$, $4.4\,\mathrm{m}$ too far
out, and its ellipse is a straight bar that cannot follow the curved tails:
its standard deviation along $y$ is $5.0\,\mathrm{m}$ where the samples
spread by $7.7\,\mathrm{m}$, and its $2\sigma$ ellipse contains $74.6\,\%$ of
the samples instead of the nominal $86.5\,\%$, missing exactly the tails a
collision check cares about. Nothing in the EKF signals the error: it
reports a confident covariance around a biased mean. The unscented transform
of \cref{sec:ch19-ukf} puts the mean at $(0.0, 95.6)$ with five function
evaluations and no derivative, and its ellipse contains $91.6\,\%$ of the
samples.

\paragraph{A poor initial estimate.}
The Jacobians are evaluated at the estimate. If the estimate is far from the
truth, $\mat{F}_k$ and $\mat{H}_k$ describe the wrong tangent, the gain is
computed for a fictitious geometry, and an update can push the estimate
further away while the covariance, which knows nothing about the error, keeps
shrinking: the filter has \textbf{diverged}\index{divergence (EKF)}. Whether
this happens depends on how strongly the measurements constrain the state. A
range--bearing sensor measures the position directly, so a position error is
corrected in one step and a heading error through the correlations that the
process noise builds up over a few steps; in the experiment of
\cref{sec:ch19-ukf-compare} the EKF recovers from a $90^\circ$ heading error
and an overconfident covariance in every run. Divergence is the real danger
with weak or bearing-only measurements, long gaps, and an initial covariance
small enough to make the filter ignore its innovations. The cures are an
honest $\mat{P}_0$, a filter whose approximation does not depend on a
tangent, and, when the measurements cannot resolve the ambiguity, a particle
filter that keeps several hypotheses alive. The same experiment shows the
other side of the coin: a particle filter started with an overconfident
cloud that contains no particle near the truth fares worse than the EKF.

\section{The unscented Kalman filter}
\label{sec:ch19-ukf}

\subsection{The unscented transform}
\label{sec:ch19-ut}

The EKF approximates the \emph{function}. Julier and
Uhlmann~\cite{julier1997new,julier2004unscented} proposed to approximate the
\emph{distribution} instead: represent the Gaussian by a few points with
exactly its mean and covariance, push the points through the true function,
and read mean and covariance off the images. The construction is the
\textbf{unscented transform}\index{unscented transform} (UT), its points are
the \textbf{sigma points}\index{sigma points}.

\begin{definition}[Sigma points and the unscented transform]
\label{def:ch19-ut}
Let $\state \in \R^n$ have mean $\bar{\state}$ and covariance $\mat{P}$, and
let $g \colon \R^n \to \R^p$. Fix $\alpha \in (0, 1]$, $\beta \ge 0$ and
$\kappa$, set
\begin{equation}
  \lambda = \alpha^2 (n + \kappa) - n,
  \label{eq:ch19-lambda}
\end{equation}
and let $\mat{L}$ be a matrix square root of $(n + \lambda)\mat{P}$, that is,
$\mat{L}\mat{L}\T = (n + \lambda)\mat{P}$, with columns $\vect{l}_1, \dots,
\vect{l}_n$. The $2n + 1$ \textbf{sigma points} and their weights are
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \mathcal{X}_0 &= \bar{\state}, &
    W_0^{(m)} &= \frac{\lambda}{n + \lambda}, &
    W_0^{(c)} &= \frac{\lambda}{n + \lambda} + 1 - \alpha^2 + \beta,\\
    \mathcal{X}_i &= \bar{\state} + \vect{l}_i, \quad
    \mathcal{X}_{n+i} = \bar{\state} - \vect{l}_i, &
    W_i^{(m)} &= W_i^{(c)} = W_{n+i}^{(m)} = W_{n+i}^{(c)} = \frac{1}{2(n + \lambda)}, & & i = 1, \dots, n.
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:ch19-sigma-points}
\end{equation}
The \textbf{unscented transform} of $(\bar{\state}, \mat{P})$ through $g$
maps every point, $\mathcal{Y}_i = g(\mathcal{X}_i)$, and returns
\begin{equation}
  \bar{\vect{y}} = \sum_{i=0}^{2n} W_i^{(m)}\mathcal{Y}_i, \quad
  \mat{P}_{yy} = \sum_{i=0}^{2n} W_i^{(c)}(\mathcal{Y}_i - \bar{\vect{y}})
    (\mathcal{Y}_i - \bar{\vect{y}})\T, \quad
  \mat{P}_{xy} = \sum_{i=0}^{2n} W_i^{(c)}(\mathcal{X}_i - \bar{\state})
    (\mathcal{Y}_i - \bar{\vect{y}})\T,
  \label{eq:ch19-ut}
\end{equation}
the approximate mean and covariance of $g(\state)$ and the cross-covariance
between $\state$ and $g(\state)$.
\end{definition}

The points sit on the principal axes of the covariance ellipsoid,
$\sqrt{n + \lambda} = \alpha\sqrt{n + \kappa}$ standard deviations from the
mean (\cref{fig:ch19-linearisation}, right). \Cref{thm:ch19-ut} proves that
the mean weights sum to one, that the weighted mean and covariance of the
points are exactly $\bar{\state}$ and $\mat{P}$, and that the transform is
exact for affine $g$; \cref{thm:ch19-ut-second-order} that its mean is
correct to second order for any smooth $g$. The parameters control what
happens beyond that: $\alpha$ scales the spread, from $\sqrt{n+\kappa}$
standard deviations at $\alpha = 1$ to a central-difference-like cluster at
$\alpha = 10^{-3}$, the usual range being $10^{-3} \le \alpha \le 1$;
$\beta$ enters only $W_0^{(c)}$ and encodes the fourth moment of the
distribution, $\beta = 2$ being optimal for a Gaussian
\cite{wan2000unscented}; $\kappa$ is a secondary spread parameter, $\kappa =
0$ or $\kappa = 3 - n$, the latter Julier and Uhlmann's original choice,
which matches the Gaussian's fourth moment for $n \le 3$ but makes $\lambda$
and $W_0^{(m)}$ negative for larger $n$. The chapter code uses $\alpha = 1$,
$\beta = 2$, $\kappa = 0$ for the five-dimensional tracker, so $\lambda = 0$,
the points are at $\sqrt{5} \approx 2.24$ standard deviations,
$W_0^{(m)} = 0$, $W_0^{(c)} = 2$ and all other weights are $0.1$; for the
two-dimensional example of \cref{fig:ch19-linearisation} it uses
$\kappa = 3 - n = 1$, so $\lambda = 1$, points at $\sqrt{3}$ standard
deviations, $W_0^{(m)} = 1/3$, $W_0^{(c)} = 7/3$, $W_i = 1/6$.
\index{unscented transform!parameters}

\paragraph{The matrix square root.}
Any $\mat{L}$ with $\mat{L}\mat{L}\T = (n + \lambda)\mat{P}$ works; the standard
choice is the \textbf{Cholesky factor}\index{Cholesky factorisation}, the
lower-triangular $\mat{L}$ with positive diagonal that
\code{numpy.linalg.cholesky} computes in $\bigO{n^3}$. For a diagonal
$\mat{P}$ it is the diagonal matrix of standard deviations scaled by
$\sqrt{n + \lambda}$, which makes the worked example easy to check by hand.
The factorisation needs a symmetric positive definite $\mat{P}$;
\cref{sec:ch19-implementation} says what to do when rounding breaks that.

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
\caption{Sigma points and the unscented transform}
\label{alg:ch19-ut}
\KwIn{mean $\bar{\state} \in \R^n$, covariance $\mat{P}$, parameters $\alpha, \beta, \kappa$; function $g$; the angular components of $\state$ and of $g(\state)$}
\KwOut{sigma points and weights; transformed mean, covariance and cross-covariance}
\Fn{\NlfSigmaPoints{$\bar{\state}$, $\mat{P}$, $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\kappa$}}{
  $\lambda \gets \alpha^2(n + \kappa) - n$; $\mat{L} \gets$ Cholesky factor of $(n + \lambda)\mat{P}$\label{alg:ch19-ut:chol}\;
  $\mathcal{X}_0 \gets \bar{\state}$; $\mathcal{X}_i \gets \bar{\state} + \vect{l}_i$; $\mathcal{X}_{n+i} \gets \bar{\state} - \vect{l}_i$ for $i = 1, \dots, n$\label{alg:ch19-ut:points}\;
  $W_0^{(m)} \gets \lambda/(n+\lambda)$; $W_0^{(c)} \gets W_0^{(m)} + 1 - \alpha^2 + \beta$; $W_i^{(m)} = W_i^{(c)} \gets 1/(2(n+\lambda))$ for $i = 1, \dots, 2n$\label{alg:ch19-ut:weights}\;
  \KwRet $(\mathcal{X}, W^{(m)}, W^{(c)})$\;
}
\Fn{\NlfUnscentedTransform{$\mathcal{X}$, $W^{(m)}$, $W^{(c)}$, $g$}}{
  $\mathcal{Y}_i \gets g(\mathcal{X}_i)$ for $i = 0, \dots, 2n$\label{alg:ch19-ut:map}\;
  $\bar{\vect{y}} \gets \sum_i W_i^{(m)} \mathcal{Y}_i$; for an angular component $j$: $\bar{y}_j \gets \mathcal{Y}_{0,j} + \sum_i W_i^{(m)}\,$\NlfWrap{$\mathcal{Y}_{i,j} - \mathcal{Y}_{0,j}$}\label{alg:ch19-ut:mean}\;
  $\vect{e}_i \gets \mathcal{Y}_i \ominus \bar{\vect{y}}$; $\vect{d}_i \gets \mathcal{X}_i \ominus \bar{\state}$\label{alg:ch19-ut:residuals}\;
  $\mat{P}_{yy} \gets \sum_i W_i^{(c)} \vect{e}_i \vect{e}_i\T$; $\mat{P}_{xy} \gets \sum_i W_i^{(c)} \vect{d}_i \vect{e}_i\T$\label{alg:ch19-ut:cov}\;
  \KwRet $(\bar{\vect{y}}, \mat{P}_{yy}, \mat{P}_{xy})$\;
}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Walkthrough.}
Line~\ref{alg:ch19-ut:chol} is the only expensive step and the reason the
UKF costs about as much as the EKF. Lines~\ref{alg:ch19-ut:points}
and~\ref{alg:ch19-ut:weights} build the symmetric point set and the two
weight vectors; only the mean weights sum to one. The mean on
line~\ref{alg:ch19-ut:mean} needs care for angles: the plain weighted mean
of the bearings $3.1$ and $-3.1$ is $0$, the opposite direction. Wrapped
residuals relative to the central image $\mathcal{Y}_0$, averaged and added
back, give the right answer as long as the images spread over less than half
a turn; line~\ref{alg:ch19-ut:residuals} wraps the residuals for the same
reason. The cross-covariance $\mat{P}_{xy}$ of line~\ref{alg:ch19-ut:cov} is
what makes the UKF possible: it plays the role of $\mat{P}\mat{H}\T$ without an
$\mat{H}$.

\subsection{The filter}
\label{sec:ch19-ukf-step}

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
\caption{One step of the unscented Kalman filter (additive noise)}
\label{alg:ch19-ukf}
\KwIn{previous estimate $\hat{\state}_{k-1}$, $\mat{P}_{k-1}$; measurement $\meas_k$ or \KwNil; models $f$, $h$; $\mat{Q}_k$, $\mat{R}_k$; parameters $\alpha, \beta, \kappa$}
\KwOut{updated estimate $\hat{\state}_k$, $\mat{P}_k$}
\Fn{\NlfUkfStep{$\hat{\state}_{k-1}$, $\mat{P}_{k-1}$, $\meas_k$}}{
  $(\mathcal{X}, W^{(m)}, W^{(c)}) \gets$ \NlfSigmaPoints{$\hat{\state}_{k-1}$, $\mat{P}_{k-1}$, $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\kappa$}\label{alg:ch19-ukf:sp1}\;
  $(\hat{\state}_k^-, \mat{P}_k^-, \cdot) \gets$ \NlfUnscentedTransform{$\mathcal{X}$, $W^{(m)}$, $W^{(c)}$, $f$}; $\mat{P}_k^- \gets \mat{P}_k^- + \mat{Q}_k$\label{alg:ch19-ukf:predict}\;
  \If{$\meas_k = $ \KwNil}{\KwRet $(\hat{\state}_k^-, \mat{P}_k^-)$\;}
  $(\mathcal{X}^-, W^{(m)}, W^{(c)}) \gets$ \NlfSigmaPoints{$\hat{\state}_k^-$, $\mat{P}_k^-$, $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\kappa$}\tcp*{redraw around the prediction}\label{alg:ch19-ukf:sp2}
  $(\hat{\meas}_k, \mat{S}_k, \mat{C}_k) \gets$ \NlfUnscentedTransform{$\mathcal{X}^-$, $W^{(m)}$, $W^{(c)}$, $h$}; $\mat{S}_k \gets \mat{S}_k + \mat{R}_k$\label{alg:ch19-ukf:measure}\;
  $\Kgain_k \gets \mat{C}_k \mat{S}_k\inv$; $\vect{y}_k \gets \meas_k \ominus \hat{\meas}_k$\label{alg:ch19-ukf:gain}\;
  $\hat{\state}_k \gets \hat{\state}_k^- + \Kgain_k \vect{y}_k$, heading wrapped; $\mat{P}_k \gets \mat{P}_k^- - \Kgain_k \mat{S}_k \Kgain_k\T$\label{alg:ch19-ukf:update}\;
  \KwRet $(\hat{\state}_k, \mat{P}_k)$\;
}
\end{algorithm}

\Cref{alg:ch19-ukf} is the EKF with both linearisations replaced by
unscented transforms \cite{wan2000unscented}. Line~\ref{alg:ch19-ukf:sp1}
draws sigma points around the previous estimate and
line~\ref{alg:ch19-ukf:predict} pushes them through the motion model; the
process noise is additive, so $\mat{Q}_k$ is simply added.
Line~\ref{alg:ch19-ukf:sp2} draws a fresh set around the prediction: reusing
the propagated points saves a factorisation but they no longer represent
$\mat{P}_k^-$ once $\mat{Q}_k$ has been added. Line~\ref{alg:ch19-ukf:measure}
maps the new points through $h$ and returns the predicted measurement, the
innovation covariance and the cross-covariance $\mat{C}_k$; the gain
$\mat{C}_k\mat{S}_k\inv$ on line~\ref{alg:ch19-ukf:gain} equals
\cref{eq:ch19-ekf-update} when $\mat{C}_k = \mat{P}_k^-\mat{H}_k\T$, and the
covariance update $\mat{P}_k^- - \Kgain_k\mat{S}_k\Kgain_k\T$ equals
$(\mat{I} - \Kgain_k\mat{H}_k)\mat{P}_k^-$ in the linear case and needs no
$\mat{H}_k$. Every evaluation of $f$ and $h$ is of the true function; the only
approximation is the Gaussian fitted to $2n+1$ images.
\index{unscented Kalman filter!equations}

\section{The particle filter}
\label{sec:ch19-pf}

\subsection{Particles, weights and the bootstrap filter}

\begin{definition}[Weighted particle set]
\label{def:ch19-particles}
A \textbf{weighted particle set}\index{particle} of size $N$ is a collection
$\{(\state^{(i)}, w^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N$ of states, the \textbf{particles}, and
non-negative \textbf{weights}\index{importance weight} with $\sum_i w^{(i)} =
1$. It represents the density
\begin{equation}
  p(\state) \approx \sum_{i=1}^N w^{(i)}\,\delta(\state - \state^{(i)}),
  \qquad\text{so that}\qquad
  \E[\phi(\state)] \approx \sum_{i=1}^N w^{(i)}\,\phi(\state^{(i)})
  \label{eq:ch19-particle-approx}
\end{equation}
for any function $\phi$; the mean and the covariance are the cases
$\phi(\state) = \state$ and $\phi(\state) = (\state - \hat{\state})(\state -
\hat{\state})\T$.
\end{definition}

The representation makes no assumption about the shape of $p$: a set with
half of its weight around one state and half around another is a belief with
two modes. Where do particles and weights come from? The idea is
\textbf{importance sampling}\index{importance sampling}: we cannot draw from
the posterior, but we can draw from the motion model, so we draw each new
particle from $p(\state_k \mid \state_{k-1}^{(i)})$ and correct for the
difference between where we sampled and where we wanted to sample by a
weight, the ratio of target to proposal density. For the recursion
\cref{eq:ch19-bayes} with the motion model as proposal everything cancels
except the likelihood \cite{arulampalam2002tutorial}:
\begin{equation}
  \state_k^{(i)} \sim p(\state_k \mid \state_{k-1}^{(i)}), \qquad
  w_k^{(i)} \propto w_{k-1}^{(i)}\, p(\meas_k \mid \state_k^{(i)}).
  \label{eq:ch19-sis}
\end{equation}
This choice is the \textbf{bootstrap filter}\index{bootstrap filter} of
Gordon, Salmond and Smith~\cite{gordon1993novel}, also called sequential
importance resampling (SIR)\index{sequential importance resampling}. It needs
only the ability to sample the motion model and to evaluate the likelihood.
For the range--bearing sensor,
\begin{equation}
  \log p(\meas_k \mid \state^{(i)}) = -\frac12\,\vect{y}^{(i)\mathsf{T}}\mat{R}\inv\vect{y}^{(i)} + \text{const},
  \qquad \vect{y}^{(i)} = \meas_k \ominus h(\state^{(i)}),
  \label{eq:ch19-loglik}
\end{equation}
and the constant cancels in the normalisation. Other likelihoods are equally
welcome: a map that says ``no intruder inside a building'' is the likelihood
that is $0$ inside and $1$ outside, which \cref{sec:ch19-pf-example} uses.

\subsection{Degeneracy, the effective sample size and resampling}
\label{sec:ch19-resampling}

Applied naively, \cref{eq:ch19-sis} fails within a few steps. Every particle
is a random guess of the whole trajectory, most guesses drift away from the
truth, and after normalisation a single particle carries almost all the
weight: \textbf{weight degeneracy}\index{degeneracy (particle filter)}, which
is unavoidable because the variance of the weights can only grow over time
\cite{doucet2001sequential}. The standard diagnostic is the following.

\begin{definition}[Effective sample size]
\label{def:ch19-ess}
The \textbf{effective sample size}\index{effective sample size} of a
normalised weight vector is
\begin{equation}
  N_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^N \bigl(w^{(i)}\bigr)^2}.
  \label{eq:ch19-ess}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

$N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ lies between $1$ (one particle has all the weight) and $N$
(all weights equal), see \cref{thm:ch19-ess}, and it is roughly the number of
equally weighted particles that would give an estimate of the same variance.
The cure for degeneracy is \textbf{resampling}\index{resampling}: draw $N$
particles from the current set with probabilities equal to the weights, so
that heavy particles are copied and light ones disappear, and reset all
weights to $1/N$. Resampling is applied when $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ falls below a
threshold $N_{\mathrm{thr}}$, typically $N/2$; the original bootstrap filter
resampled at every step, which is simpler but wasteful. Copies of one
particle are identical until the process noise separates them, and when the
noise is small or measurements are missing for a while the set collapses
onto a few distinct states: \textbf{sample impoverishment}
\index{sample impoverishment}. Resampling only when needed limits it;
\cref{sec:ch19-variants} lists stronger remedies.

How the draws are made matters. Independent draws (multinomial resampling)
add unnecessary randomness. \textbf{Systematic resampling}
\index{resampling!systematic} \cite{kitagawa1996monte,arulampalam2002tutorial}
draws a single uniform $u \in [0, 1/N)$, places the points $u, u + 1/N,
\dots, u + (N-1)/N$ on the unit interval, and selects particle $i$ once for
every point in its slot $(c_{i-1}, c_i]$ of the cumulative weights
(\cref{alg:ch19-resample}). Each particle is copied $\lfloor N w^{(i)}
\rfloor$ or $\lceil N w^{(i)} \rceil$ times with expectation $N w^{(i)}$
(\cref{thm:ch19-systematic}), in a single $\bigO{N}$ pass: the grid points
come in increasing order, so the pointer $i$ on
line~\ref{alg:ch19-resample:advance} only moves forward. Clamp the last
cumulative sum to $1$ so that rounding cannot run the loop past the end.

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
\caption{Systematic resampling}
\label{alg:ch19-resample}
\KwIn{normalised weights $w^{(1)}, \dots, w^{(N)}$}
\KwOut{indices $\mathit{idx}[1..N]$ of the particles to keep (with repetitions)}
\Fn{\NlfSystematicResample{$w$}}{
  $u \gets$ uniform random number in $[0, 1/N)$; $c_i \gets \sum_{l \le i} w^{(l)}$ for $i = 1, \dots, N$, with $c_N \gets 1$\label{alg:ch19-resample:u}\;
  $i \gets 1$\;
  \For{$j \gets 1$ \KwTo $N$}{
    $u_j \gets u + (j - 1)/N$\label{alg:ch19-resample:grid}\;
    \While{$c_i < u_j$}{$i \gets i + 1$\label{alg:ch19-resample:advance}\;}
    $\mathit{idx}[j] \gets i$\label{alg:ch19-resample:select}\;
  }
  \KwRet $\mathit{idx}$\;
}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{The bootstrap filter step}
\label{sec:ch19-pf-step}

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
\caption{One step of the bootstrap particle filter}
\label{alg:ch19-pf}
\KwIn{particles $\state_{k-1}^{(i)}$ with log-weights $\ell^{(i)}$, $i = 1, \dots, N$; measurement $\meas_k$ or \KwNil; motion model $f$ with noise sampler; likelihood $p(\meas \mid \state)$; threshold $N_{\mathrm{thr}}$}
\KwOut{particles $\state_k^{(i)}$, log-weights $\ell^{(i)}$, estimate $\hat{\state}_k$, $\mat{P}_k$}
\Fn{\NlfPfStep{$\{\state_{k-1}^{(i)}, \ell^{(i)}\}$, $\meas_k$}}{
  \ForEach{$i$}{
    draw $\vect{w}^{(i)} \sim \Normal(\vect{0}, \mat{Q}(\state_{k-1}^{(i)}))$; $\state_k^{(i)} \gets f(\state_{k-1}^{(i)}) + \vect{w}^{(i)}$\tcp*{propagate}\label{alg:ch19-pf:propagate}
  }
  \If{$\meas_k \ne$ \KwNil}{
    $\ell^{(i)} \gets \ell^{(i)} + \log p(\meas_k \mid \state_k^{(i)})$ for all $i$\tcp*{weight}\label{alg:ch19-pf:weight}
  }
  $w^{(i)} \gets \exp\bigl(\ell^{(i)} - \max_j \ell^{(j)}\bigr)$; $w^{(i)} \gets w^{(i)} / \sum_j w^{(j)}$; $\ell^{(i)} \gets \log w^{(i)}$\tcp*{normalise}\label{alg:ch19-pf:normalise}
  $\hat{\state}_k \gets \sum_i w^{(i)}\state_k^{(i)}$ (circular mean for angles); $\mat{P}_k \gets \sum_i w^{(i)}(\state_k^{(i)} \ominus \hat{\state}_k)(\state_k^{(i)} \ominus \hat{\state}_k)\T$\label{alg:ch19-pf:estimate}\;
  \If{$1 / \sum_i (w^{(i)})^2 < N_{\mathrm{thr}}$}{
    $\mathit{idx} \gets$ \NlfSystematicResample{$w$}; $\state_k^{(i)} \gets \state_k^{(\mathit{idx}[i])}$; $\ell^{(i)} \gets -\log N$ for all $i$\tcp*{resample}\label{alg:ch19-pf:resample}
  }
  \KwRet $(\{\state_k^{(i)}, \ell^{(i)}\}, \hat{\state}_k, \mat{P}_k)$\;
}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Walkthrough.}
Line~\ref{alg:ch19-pf:propagate} is the predict step: every particle follows
the true motion model with its own noise, so the cloud spreads exactly as the
model says, curvature and all. Line~\ref{alg:ch19-pf:weight} is the update; a
missing measurement skips it and the cloud keeps spreading.
Line~\ref{alg:ch19-pf:normalise} normalises in the log domain, subtracting
the largest log-weight first so that the exponentials cannot underflow: with
a sharp likelihood, raw values of $10^{-300}$ are routine.
Line~\ref{alg:ch19-pf:estimate} extracts a point estimate and covariance for
consumers that need one, the heading averaged as an angle through the
weighted mean of $(\cos\psi, \sin\psi)$; when the cloud is bimodal the mean
lies between the modes and is not a plausible state, and \cref{sec:ch19-drone}
argues that the planner should then receive the cloud.
Line~\ref{alg:ch19-pf:resample} resamples only when the effective sample size
has dropped below the threshold.
\index{particle filter!bootstrap}\index{log-sum-exp}

\subsection{A worked example: an intruder behind a building}
\label{sec:ch19-pf-example}

\begin{example}[A multimodal posterior]
\label{ex:ch19-occlusion}
An intruder flies north at $8\,\mathrm{m/s}$ towards a building occupying
$[-10, 10] \times [50, 70]$ metres; our sensor at the origin measures range
and bearing every $\dt = 0.5\,\mathrm{s}$ with $\sigma_r = 2\,\mathrm{m}$ and
$\sigma_\varphi = 0.05\,\mathrm{rad}$, but loses the intruder inside the
street canyon $35 \le y \le 75$, where no measurement arrives. The map is
known, so a state inside the building has likelihood $0$. Unseen, the
intruder turns left at $1\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ for one second when it is
$13\,\mathrm{m}$ short of the building, flies north-west for two seconds,
turns back north and passes the building on the west side. A bootstrap
filter with $N = 2\,000$ particles, $\sigma_a = 0.5\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$,
$\sigma_\gamma = 0.3\,\mathrm{rad/s^2}$ and $N_{\mathrm{thr}} = N/2$ tracks it
(\code{occlusion\_example()} in the chapter code;
\code{gen\_ch19\_particles.py} draws \cref{fig:ch19-particles}).
\end{example}

At $k = 7$ ($t = 3.5\,\mathrm{s}$) the sensor delivers its last measurement

resamples. From $k = 8$ on only line~\ref{alg:ch19-pf:propagate} of
\cref{alg:ch19-pf} runs: every particle follows its own acceleration and
turn-rate noise, and the cloud fans out northwards. Between $k = 10$ and
$k = 12$ the leading particles reach the south face of the building at
$y = 50$; those inside receive weight zero, $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ falls from
$1\,911$ to $861$, and the filter resamples, copying the survivors on both
sides. At $k = 15$ ($t = 7.5\,\mathrm{s}$, orange) the belief has two lobes,
$68\,\%$ of the particles west of the building and $28\,\%$ east of it, the
remaining $4\,\%$ lagging south of it. The filter does not know which lobe
holds the intruder, and it says so. A Gaussian filter cannot: the UKF run on
the same measurements, with predict-only steps in the canyon, has its mean at
$(-3.0, 61.4)$ at $k = 15$, inside the building, and keeps it there from
$k = 11$ to $k = 17$; a planner that inflates that mean by its covariance
would guard a region the intruder cannot be in and leave both streets
uncovered. At $k = 20$ ($t = 10\,\mathrm{s}$) the intruder reappears at

is gone. The collapse is sample impoverishment in action: the $2\,000$
particles are now copies of a few dozen ancestors, and the process noise
needs several steps to spread them again ($N_{\mathrm{eff}} = 432$ at $k =
21$, above $1\,000$ at $k = 22$). Over the run the filter resamples $13$
times in $29$ steps. \Cref{exr:ch19-occlusion} explores roughening, other
resampling rules and a right turn.

\section{Properties, costs and a decision guide}
\label{sec:ch19-properties}

None of the three filters is optimal in the sense in which the Kalman filter
is optimal for linear-Gaussian models (\cref{ch:ch18}); each approximates the
Bayes filter, and what can be proved is how good the approximation is and
what it costs.

\begin{proposition}[The EKF contains the Kalman filter]
\label{thm:ch19-ekf-linear}
If $f(\state) = \mat{F}\state + \vect{b}$ and $h(\state) = \mat{H}\state +
\vect{c}$ are affine, \cref{alg:ch19-ekf} computes exactly the Kalman filter
of \cref{ch:ch18}, hence the exact posterior mean and covariance.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The Jacobians of affine maps are the constant matrices $\mat{F}$, $\mat{H}$, the
approximations \cref{eq:ch19-ekf-linearise} are equalities, and
\cref{eq:ch19-ekf-predict,eq:ch19-ekf-innovation,eq:ch19-ekf-update} are the
Kalman equations, exact for linear-Gaussian models by the main theorem of
\cref{ch:ch18}.
\end{proof}

For nonlinear models the EKF has no such guarantee: its predicted mean
$f(\hat{\state}_{k-1})$ differs from the true mean of $f(\state_{k-1})$ by
the second-order term $\tfrac12\sum_{jk} P_{jk}\,\partial^2 f/\partial
x_j\partial x_k$ and higher, its covariance ignores the same terms, and it
may diverge from a poor start. Convergence results exist under bounds on
the nonlinearity and the initial error \cite{sarkka2013bayesian}; in
practice the covariance is validated by looking at the innovations, as in
\cref{ch:ch18}.

\begin{proposition}[Properties of the sigma points]
\label{thm:ch19-ut}
For every $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\kappa$ with $n + \lambda > 0$, the sigma points
and weights of \cref{def:ch19-ut} satisfy:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item $\sum_{i=0}^{2n} W_i^{(m)} = 1$;
  \item $\sum_{i} W_i^{(m)}\mathcal{X}_i = \bar{\state}$ and
        $\sum_{i} W_i^{(c)}(\mathcal{X}_i - \bar{\state})(\mathcal{X}_i - \bar{\state})\T = \mat{P}$;
  \item if $g(\state) = \mat{A}\state + \vect{b}$ is affine, the unscented
        transform returns $\bar{\vect{y}} = \mat{A}\bar{\state} + \vect{b}$,
        $\mat{P}_{yy} = \mat{A}\mat{P}\mat{A}\T$ and $\mat{P}_{xy} = \mat{P}\mat{A}\T$,
        the exact values; consequently the UKF reduces to the Kalman filter
        for linear models.
\end{enumerate}
\index{unscented transform!properties}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(i) $\frac{\lambda}{n+\lambda} + 2n \cdot \frac{1}{2(n+\lambda)} = 1$.
(ii) The points come in pairs $\bar{\state} \pm \vect{l}_i$ with equal
weights, so the $\pm\vect{l}_i$ cancel in the mean, which leaves
$\bar{\state}\sum_i W_i^{(m)} = \bar{\state}$; in the covariance the term
$i = 0$ vanishes and each pair contributes $2 \cdot
\frac{1}{2(n+\lambda)}\vect{l}_i\vect{l}_i\T$, so the sum is
$\frac{1}{n+\lambda}\mat{L}\mat{L}\T = \mat{P}$.
(iii) $\mathcal{Y}_i = \mat{A}\mathcal{X}_i + \vect{b}$, hence by (i) and (ii)
$\bar{\vect{y}} = \mat{A}\bar{\state} + \vect{b}$, and $\mathcal{Y}_i -
\bar{\vect{y}} = \mat{A}(\mathcal{X}_i - \bar{\state})$ gives $\mat{P}_{yy} =
\mat{A}\mat{P}\mat{A}\T$ and $\mat{P}_{xy} = \mat{P}\mat{A}\T$; with these values
\cref{alg:ch19-ukf} performs the Kalman equations. The self-test of the
chapter code checks (i) and (iii) numerically.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Second-order accuracy of the unscented mean]
\label{thm:ch19-ut-second-order}
For smooth $g$ and any parameters, the unscented mean agrees with the Taylor
expansion of $\E[g(\state)]$ about $\bar{\state}$ up to and including the
second-order terms; the EKF mean $g(\bar{\state})$ agrees only to first
order. For Gaussian $\state$ and $\beta = 2$ the unscented covariance also
matches some fourth-order terms of the true covariance.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Write $\state = \bar{\state} + \vect{d}$ and expand $g(\bar{\state} + \vect{d})
= g(\bar{\state}) + \mat{G}\vect{d} + \tfrac12\sum_{jk} d_j d_k\,\partial_{jk}g
+ \bigO{\norm{\vect{d}}^3}$. Taking expectations, $\E[\vect{d}] = 0$ kills
the linear term and $\E[d_j d_k] = P_{jk}$ gives $\E[g(\state)] =
g(\bar{\state}) + \tfrac12\sum_{jk} P_{jk}\,\partial_{jk}g + \dots$. For the
sigma points, $\vect{d}_i = \pm\vect{l}_i$: the linear terms cancel in
pairs and the quadratic terms give $\tfrac12\sum_{jk}\partial_{jk}g \cdot
\frac{1}{n+\lambda}\sum_{i=1}^n l_{i,j}l_{i,k} = \tfrac12\sum_{jk}
P_{jk}\,\partial_{jk}g$, the same term. The fourth-order statement is in
\textcite{julier2004unscented} and \textcite{wan2000unscented}.
\Cref{fig:ch19-linearisation} shows the second-order term at work: the true
mean of $r\sin\varphi$ is $100\,e^{-\sigma_\varphi^2/2} = 95.6$, the EKF gives
$100$, the UT $95.6$.
\end{proof}

The UT is not exact beyond second order, and a badly scaled point set samples
the function where it does not represent the belief: with $n = 5$ the points
sit $2.2\sigma$ out, and for a heading belief with $\sigma_\psi = 1\,\mathrm{rad}$
they straddle more than half the circle; negative weights ($\lambda < 0$)
can make $\mat{P}_{yy}$ indefinite. The fix is a smaller $\alpha$ or another
$\kappa$.

\begin{proposition}[Bounds on the effective sample size]
\label{thm:ch19-ess}
For normalised weights, $1 \le N_{\mathrm{eff}} \le N$, with $N_{\mathrm{eff}}
= N$ if and only if all weights equal $1/N$ and $N_{\mathrm{eff}} = 1$ if and
only if one weight equals $1$. After resampling, $N_{\mathrm{eff}} = N$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality, $1 = \bigl(\sum_i w^{(i)}\bigr)^2 \le
N\sum_i (w^{(i)})^2$ with equality if and only if all weights are equal, so
$N_{\mathrm{eff}} \le N$. Conversely $\sum_i (w^{(i)})^2 \le \max_i w^{(i)}
\sum_i w^{(i)} = \max_i w^{(i)} \le 1$, with equality if and only if a single
weight is $1$. Resampling sets every weight to $1/N$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Systematic resampling is unbiased]
\label{thm:ch19-systematic}
Let $n_i$ be the number of copies of particle $i$ produced by
\cref{alg:ch19-resample}. Then $n_i \in \{\lfloor N w^{(i)}\rfloor, \lfloor
N w^{(i)}\rfloor + 1\}$ and $\E[n_i] = N w^{(i)}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$n_i$ is the number of points of the grid $u + (j-1)/N$, of spacing $1/N$,
that fall into the slot $(c_{i-1}, c_i]$ of length $w^{(i)}$. An interval of
length $\ell$ contains $\lfloor N\ell\rfloor$ or $\lfloor N\ell\rfloor + 1$
points of such a grid, the second case with probability equal to the
fractional part of $N\ell$ because $u$ is uniform on $[0, 1/N)$; hence
$\E[n_i] = \lfloor N w^{(i)}\rfloor + (N w^{(i)} - \lfloor N w^{(i)}\rfloor)
= N w^{(i)}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Convergence of the bootstrap filter]
\label{thm:ch19-pf-convergence}
Under mild conditions (a bounded continuous likelihood and a motion model
that can be sampled), for every bounded function $\phi$ the particle
estimate $\sum_i w_k^{(i)}\phi(\state_k^{(i)})$ of \cref{alg:ch19-pf}
converges to $\E[\phi(\state_k) \mid \meas_{1:k}]$ as $N \to \infty$, with a
mean-square error bounded by $c_k \norm{\phi}_\infty^2 / N$ for a constant
$c_k$ independent of $N$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
By induction over $k$: propagation, weighting and resampling are each a
Monte Carlo operation with $\bigO{1/\sqrt{N}}$ mean-square error for bounded
test functions, and the errors add up over the three operations and over
time. Crisan and Doucet~\cite{crisan2002survey} give the argument and the
conditions under which $c_k$ stays bounded in $k$; see also
\textcite{doucet2001sequential}.
\end{proof}

The particle filter is therefore consistent: with enough particles it
computes the Bayes filter for any model, Gaussian or not, unimodal or not.
The catch is ``enough'': the constant $c_k$ and the particle count needed for
a given accuracy grow exponentially with the state dimension, because the
likelihood concentrates in a region whose volume fraction shrinks with every
dimension. Five or six dimensions are comfortable with a few thousand
particles; a dozen are not.

\paragraph{Costs and a decision guide.}
\label{sec:ch19-decision}
\Cref{tab:ch19-properties} summarises the three filters. Per step, the EKF
evaluates $f$, $h$ and two Jacobians once and performs $\bigO{n^3 + m^3}$
matrix arithmetic; the UKF performs two Cholesky factorisations and $2n+1$
evaluations of $f$ and of $h$, again $\bigO{n^3}$; the particle filter
evaluates $f$ and the likelihood $N$ times and resamples in $\bigO{N}$, so
its cost is $\bigO{N(n + m)}$. In the experiment of
\cref{sec:ch19-ukf-compare} one step takes $0.3\,\mathrm{ms}$ for the EKF,
$0.5\,\mathrm{ms}$ for the UKF and $1.3\,\mathrm{ms}$ for the particle filter
with $N = 2\,000$, in \python with NumPy: all far below a sensor cycle, but
the ratio matters when a drone tracks a dozen intruders on an embedded
processor. \Cref{tab:ch19-decision} turns the comparison into advice. First
ask whether anything is nonlinear at all: a constant-velocity intruder seen
with Cartesian positions is the Kalman filter's home ground. Then ask how
strongly the models bend across the belief: a turning intruder seen with
positions, or a range--bearing sensor at long range with a few degrees of
noise, is mild, the EKF and the UKF give the same answer and the EKF is
cheaper to run, though the UKF is cheaper to \emph{write}; bearing-only or
short-range sensing, large bearing noise and abrupt manoeuvres favour the
UKF. Finally ask whether the belief can have more than one mode: occlusions,
junctions, maps and discrete manoeuvre choices call for the particle filter,
or for an interacting multiple-model filter (\cref{sec:ch19-variants}) when
the hypotheses are few and Gaussian.

\section{Variants and extensions}
\label{sec:ch19-variants}

The \textbf{iterated EKF}\index{iterated EKF} repeats the update a few times,
re-linearising $h$ at the updated estimate, which helps when a measurement is
both informative and strongly nonlinear, as a bearing at short range; the
second-order EKF adds the Hessian terms of \cref{thm:ch19-ut-second-order},
which the UKF gets for free. The \textbf{square-root UKF} propagates the
Cholesky factor of $\mat{P}$ and never forms a covariance that could lose
positive definiteness; the \textbf{cubature Kalman filter}
\index{cubature Kalman filter} of Arasaratnam and
Haykin~\cite{arasaratnam2009cubature} uses $2n$ points at $\sqrt{n}$
standard deviations with equal weights, the UT with $\alpha = 1$, $\beta =
0$, $\kappa = 0$ without the centre point, and has no parameters and no
negative weights; the augmented UKF appends the noise variables to the state
so that non-additive noise such as $\mat{G}(\state)\vect{\eta}_k$ passes
through the sigma points. \textcite{sarkka2013bayesian} treats the whole
sigma-point family and the corresponding smoothers. On the particle side,
the regularised particle filter adds a small jitter (roughening) to the
copies produced by resampling, the auxiliary particle filter looks one
measurement ahead before resampling, and the \textbf{Rao--Blackwellised
particle filter}\index{Rao--Blackwellised particle filter} samples only the
nonlinear part of the state and runs a Kalman filter per particle for the
linear part, which is how simultaneous localisation and mapping keeps
thousands of landmarks tractable; \textcite{arulampalam2002tutorial} and
\textcite{doucet2001sequential} survey these and better proposal
distributions. For an intruder that switches between a few regimes
(straight, left turn, right turn), the \textbf{interacting multiple model}
\index{interacting multiple model} (IMM) filter of Blom and
Bar-Shalom~\cite{blom1988imm} runs one EKF or UKF per regime and mixes them
by transition probabilities: two Gaussians instead of two thousand
particles, as long as the hypotheses do not interact with a map. All of
these have three-dimensional versions: add altitude and vertical speed to
the state and an elevation angle to the sensor (\cref{exr:ch19-coding}).

\section{Implementation notes}
\label{sec:ch19-implementation}

The chapter code, \code{code/ch19\_nonlinear\_filters.py}, contains the
coordinated-turn model with Jacobian and noise, the range--bearing sensor
with Jacobian and log-likelihood, the classes \code{ExtendedKalmanFilter},
\code{UnscentedKalmanFilter} and \code{ParticleFilter}, systematic
resampling, the turning-target simulator and the two worked examples;
\cref{lst:ch19-ekf,lst:ch19-ut,lst:ch19-pf} are excerpts.

\paragraph{Covariances.}
\index{Cholesky factorisation!failure}
Rounding erodes the symmetry and positive definiteness of every covariance:
$\mat{F}\mat{P}\mat{F}\T$ is symmetric only up to rounding, and $(\mat{I} -
\Kgain\mat{H})\mat{P}^-$ subtracts nearly equal matrices. The EKF uses the
Joseph form; the UKF's update $\mat{P}^- - \Kgain\mat{S}\Kgain\T$ has no Joseph
form, so the code symmetrises $\mat{P} \gets \tfrac12(\mat{P} + \mat{P}\T)$ after
every step and adds a jitter $\epsilon\mat{I}$, $\epsilon = 10^{-9}$, before
each Cholesky factorisation (\cref{lst:ch19-ut}). When
\code{numpy.linalg.cholesky} still fails, the cause is a perfectly known
component (zero variance), a $\lambda < 0$ that produced a negative weight,
or a diverged filter, and it must be fixed at the source. A related detail
hides in the motion model: for $\abs{\omega} < 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ the
code evaluates $f$ and $\mat{F}$ by their series in $\omega$ to second order,
because the divisions by $\omega$ and $\omega^2$ in
\cref{eq:ch19-ct,eq:ch19-ct-jacobian} would turn rounding noise into metres;
the self-test checks that both branches agree at the switch.

\paragraph{Angles.}
\index{angle wrapping!implementation}
The bearing innovation, the heading component of every state difference, the
mean of sigma points and the covariance residuals all go through
\code{wrap\_angle}, which implements \cref{eq:ch19-wrap} for scalars and
arrays; the circular mean of the particles' headings comes from the weighted
sums of $\cos\psi$ and $\sin\psi$. Representing the heading by
$(\cos\psi, \sin\psi)$ would remove most special cases at the price of a
six-dimensional state with a constraint the filter does not know about; the
wrapped angle is the lesser evil.

\paragraph{Particles in NumPy.}
\index{vectorisation}
The loop over particles of \cref{alg:ch19-pf} is a set of array operations
in the code: the particles are an $N \times n$ array, $f$ and $h$ accept such
arrays, and the log-likelihood is one vectorised expression; a \python loop
over $2\,000$ particles would be a hundred times slower. Log-weights are
kept throughout and normalised with the log-sum-exp trick, and systematic
resampling is \code{numpy.searchsorted} of the grid points in the cumulative
weights, the pointer walk of \cref{alg:ch19-resample} in one call. The
particle count is a budget: the error falls like $1/\sqrt{N}$
(\cref{thm:ch19-pf-convergence}), and $1\,000$ to $5\,000$ particles are a
reasonable range for the five-dimensional intruder; measure the effect of $N$
on your own scenario (\cref{exr:ch19-coding}).

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={The EKF step (from \code{code/ch19\_nonlinear\_filters.py}). The Jacobians are evaluated at the previous estimate (predict) and at the prediction (update); innovation and heading are wrapped; the covariance uses the Joseph form.},label={lst:ch19-ekf}]
def predict(self):
    """Linearise f at the previous estimate, then move mean and covariance."""
    F = self.motion.jacobian(self.x)              # Jacobian at x_{k-1}
    Q = self.motion.Q(self.x)
    self.x = self.motion.f(self.x)                 # true f for the mean
    self.P = F @ self.P @ F.T + Q
    return self.x, self.P

def update(self, z):
    """Linearise h at the prediction and apply the Kalman update."""
    H = self.sensor.jacobian(self.x)              # Jacobian at x_k^-
    z_pred = self.sensor.h(self.x)                 # true h for the prediction
    y = self.sensor.residual(z, z_pred)            # innovation, bearing wrapped
    S = H @ self.P @ H.T + self.sensor.R
    K = np.linalg.solve(S.T, (self.P @ H.T).T).T   # K = P H^T S^{-1}
    self.x = self.x + K @ y
    for j in self.motion.angle_index:
        self.x[j] = wrap_angle(self.x[j])
    IKH = np.eye(len(self.x)) - K @ H
    self.P = IKH @ self.P @ IKH.T + K @ self.sensor.R @ K.T   # Joseph form
    self.last = dict(H=H, z_pred=z_pred, y=y, S=S, K=K)
    return self.x, self.P
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Sigma points and the unscented transform (from \code{code/ch19\_nonlinear\_filters.py}); \code{weighted\_mean} and \code{residuals} average and subtract with wrapped angular components.},label={lst:ch19-ut}]
def sigma_points(mean, cov, alpha=1.0, beta=2.0, kappa=0.0, jitter=1e-9):
    """The 2n+1 sigma points (rows) and the weight vectors W_m, W_c."""
    mean = np.asarray(mean, dtype=float)
    n = mean.size
    lam = alpha ** 2 * (n + kappa) - n
    P = 0.5 * (cov + cov.T) + jitter * np.eye(n)     # symmetrise, guard the factorisation
    L = np.linalg.cholesky((n + lam) * P)            # columns l_i: L L^T = (n+lam) P
    X = np.empty((2 * n + 1, n))
    X[0] = mean
    X[1:n + 1] = mean + L.T                          # row i of L.T is column i of L
    X[n + 1:] = mean - L.T
    W_m = np.full(2 * n + 1, 1.0 / (2.0 * (n + lam)))
    W_c = W_m.copy()
    W_m[0] = lam / (n + lam)
    W_c[0] = lam / (n + lam) + 1.0 - alpha ** 2 + beta
    return X, W_m, W_c


def unscented_transform(X, W_m, W_c, g, x_mean=None, angle_in=(), angle_out=()):
    """Push sigma points through g; return mean, covariance and cross-covariance."""
    Y = g(X)                                         # g must accept an (2n+1, n) array
    y_mean = weighted_mean(Y, W_m, angle_out)
    E = residuals(Y, y_mean, angle_out)
    if x_mean is None:
        x_mean = weighted_mean(X, W_m, angle_in)
    D = residuals(X, x_mean, angle_in)
    P_yy = (E.T * W_c) @ E                           # sum_i W_c[i] e_i e_i^T
    P_xy = (D.T * W_c) @ E
    return y_mean, P_yy, P_xy
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Systematic resampling and the bootstrap step of the particle filter (from \code{code/ch19\_nonlinear\_filters.py}), vectorised over the $N \times 5$ particle array with log-weights.},label={lst:ch19-pf}]
def systematic_resample(weights, rng):
    """Indices of the particles to keep: one uniform draw, N evenly spaced points."""
    n = len(weights)
    positions = (rng.random() + np.arange(n)) / n
    cumulative = np.cumsum(weights)
    cumulative[-1] = 1.0                             # guard against rounding
    return np.searchsorted(cumulative, positions)

def predict(self):
    """Propagate every particle through f with its own noise sample."""
    self.X = self.motion.f(self.X) + self.motion.sample_noise(self.X, self.rng)
    self.X[:, 3] = wrap_angle(self.X[:, 3])
    return self.X

def update(self, z=None):
    """Weight by the likelihood (if any), normalise, estimate, resample if needed."""
    if z is not None:
        self.logw += self.sensor.log_likelihood(z, self.X)
    if self.constraint is not None:
        self.logw[~self.constraint(self.X)] = -np.inf
    top = np.max(self.logw)
    if not np.isfinite(top):                     # every particle died: restart flat
        self.logw[:] = -math.log(self.n)
        top = self.logw[0]
    w = np.exp(self.logw - top)                  # log-sum-exp normalisation
    w /= np.sum(w)
    with np.errstate(divide="ignore"):
        self.logw = np.log(w)
    self.n_eff = effective_sample_size(w)
    self.x, self.P = self.estimate(w)
    if self.n_eff < self.threshold:
        idx = systematic_resample(w, self.rng)
        self.X = self.X[idx]
        if self.roughening is not None:              # optional jitter of the copies
            self.X = self.X + self.rng.normal(size=self.X.shape) * self.roughening
        self.logw = np.full(self.n, -math.log(self.n))
        self.resample_count += 1
    return self.x, self.P
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{pitfall}[Jacobians evaluated at the wrong point]
The EKF linearises $f$ at the \emph{previous} estimate and $h$ at the
\emph{predicted} one. Evaluating $\mat{H}_k$ at $\hat{\state}_{k-1}$, a natural
slip when both Jacobians are computed at the top of the step, uses the
geometry of where the intruder \emph{was}; at $12\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and
$50\,\mathrm{m}$ range that is a $7^\circ$ error in the bearing row of
$\mat{H}$, and the covariance quietly becomes wrong. A finite-difference check
of the Jacobians at random states, as in the self-test, catches sign and
index mistakes in \cref{sec:ch19-ekf-jacobians}; only a test against a
simulated track catches the wrong evaluation point.
\end{pitfall}

\begin{pitfall}[Forgetting to wrap angles]
An unwrapped bearing innovation near the $\pm\pi$ seam is about $2\pi$
instead of nearly zero. In \cref{ex:ch19-ekf} the EKF would multiply
$6.21\,\mathrm{rad}$ by a gain of $-47$ and move the estimate by hundreds of
metres in one step; the next innovation is huge as well, and the track is
lost. The bug is invisible while the intruder stays away from the seam,
which is why it survives testing: place the sensor so that the seam crosses
the test trajectory, and wrap every angle difference, not only the
innovation. The particle filter has the same bug in its likelihood, where it
silently assigns weight zero to every particle on the far side of the seam.
\end{pitfall}

\begin{pitfall}[Resampling at every step]
Resampling replaces distinct particles by copies that stay identical until
the process noise separates them. Resampling while $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is
still close to $N$ throws away diversity for nothing; over a long gap without
measurements, as in \cref{ex:ch19-occlusion}, a filter that resamples every
step ends with a cloud descended from a handful of ancestors and can no
longer represent two lobes. Resample when $N_{\mathrm{eff}} < N/2$, add
roughening if the process noise is small, and monitor the number of distinct
particles.
\end{pitfall}

\begin{pitfall}[Too few particles in six dimensions]
A particle filter that works with $500$ particles on a planar coordinated
turn will not work with $500$ particles on a seven-dimensional
three-dimensional state. A precise likelihood concentrates in a region whose
volume fraction shrinks with every dimension, so most particles receive
negligible weight, $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ collapses after every measurement, and
the estimate jumps between a few survivors. The symptoms are a small
$N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ after every update and an error that does not improve with
more measurements. Increase $N$ until the error curve flattens, or move the
linear part of the state into a Kalman filter per particle
(\cref{sec:ch19-variants}).
\end{pitfall}

\section{Where this fits in the drone system}
\label{sec:ch19-drone}
\begin{dronebox}
\index{particle filter!in the drone system}\index{prediction layer!nonlinear filters}%
In the hybrid architecture of \cref{ch:ch24} the filters of this chapter are
the prediction layer's trackers for \emph{manoeuvring} intruders, taking over
from the Kalman filter of \cref{ch:ch18} whenever the intruder turns or the
sensor reports range and bearing. They receive raw measurements, possibly
with gaps, and deliver at every cycle position, speed, heading and turn rate
with a covariance. Three consumers use that output: the local avoidance layer
(\cref{ch:ch13,ch:ch14}), which needs the intruder's velocity now; the
trajectory predictor of \cref{ch:ch20}, for which the coordinated-turn model
is a stronger baseline than constant velocity; and the prediction-aware
constraints of \cref{ch:ch24}, which inflate the intruder's footprint by its
uncertainty. The particle filter adds what the Gaussian filters cannot: when
the belief is multimodal, the cloud itself is the right thing to hand over.
Propagating every particle over the safety horizon yields, at each future
time, an empirical distribution of where the intruder may be, from which the
planner can compute the probability that a candidate path collides or block
the cells that more than a chosen fraction of the particles visit, without
pretending that the intruder is in one place. In the study plan
(\cref{ch:appA}) this chapter completes Week~9.
\end{dronebox}

\section{Summary}
\begin{summary}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Turning intruders (the coordinated-turn model with turn rate
  $\omega$ in the state) and range--bearing sensors make the models
  nonlinear; all three filters approximate the same Bayes filter, predicting
  through $f$ and updating with the likelihood of $\meas_k$.
  \item The EKF linearises $f$ at the previous estimate and $h$ at the
  prediction with the Jacobians $\mat{F}_k$, $\mat{H}_k$ and runs the Kalman
  equations: exact for linear models, cheap, biased when the functions curve
  across the belief, and liable to diverge from a poor start when the
  measurements are weak.
  \item The unscented transform represents a Gaussian by $2n+1$ sigma points
  $\bar{\state} \pm \vect{l}_i$ from the Cholesky factor of $(n+\lambda)\mat{P}$
  with weights $W^{(m)}$, $W^{(c)}$; it is exact for affine functions and
  second-order accurate in general, and the UKF replaces both linearisations
  by it, without Jacobians.
  \item The bootstrap particle filter propagates $N$ samples through the
  motion model with sampled noise, weights them by the likelihood,
  normalises in the log domain and resamples systematically when
  $N_{\mathrm{eff}} = 1/\sum_i (w^{(i)})^2$ drops below $N/2$; it represents
  multimodal beliefs and arbitrary likelihoods, including maps, at a cost
  linear in $N$, with $N$ growing quickly with the state dimension.
  \item Wrap every angle difference, evaluate Jacobians at the right point,
  keep covariances symmetric, vectorise particles; use the EKF for mild
  nonlinearity, the UKF for range--bearing tracking without Jacobians, the
  particle filter for occlusions, maps and multiple hypotheses, and hand the
  planner the cloud when the belief is not one blob.
\end{itemize}
\end{summary}

\paragraph{Further reading.}

\begin{exercise}[\difficulty{1}]
\label{exr:ch19-weights}
For $n = 5$ compute $\lambda$, the distance of the sigma points from the
mean in standard deviations, and $W_0^{(m)}$, $W_0^{(c)}$, $W_i^{(m)}$ for
$(\alpha, \beta, \kappa) = (1, 2, 0)$, $(1, 2, -2)$ and $(10^{-3}, 2, 0)$.
Which sets have a negative $W_0^{(m)}$? Verify \cref{thm:ch19-ut}(i) for
each, and explain why a weight of $-10^6$ on the centre point is numerically
dangerous although the transform is mathematically second-order accurate.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[\difficulty{2}]

\begin{exercise}[\difficulty{3} Coding]
\label{exr:ch19-coding}
The second half of the Week~9 exercise of the study plan (\cref{ch:appA}).
Simulate a drone that flies straight legs and turns, observed with
range--bearing measurements at $2$--$10\,\mathrm{Hz}$ with occasional
dropouts, and track it with an EKF, a UKF and a particle filter using the
coordinated-turn model. (a) Reproduce \cref{tab:ch19-rmse}: position RMSE of
each filter and of the raw measurements over $100$ runs, and the number of
lost tracks. (b) Vary the bearing noise from $1^\circ$ to $15^\circ$ and the
minimum range from $20$ to $200\,\mathrm{m}$, plot the RMSE of EKF and UKF
against each, and find where the two separate. (c) Vary $N$ from $100$ to
$10\,000$, plot RMSE and running time, and identify the $N$ beyond which the
curve flattens. (d) Add altitude and vertical speed to the state and an
elevation angle to the sensor and repeat (a) in three dimensions. Keep the
code: \cref{ch:ch20} uses the estimates as input to trajectory prediction
and \cref{ch:ch24} feeds them into the planner.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[\difficulty{3}]
\label{exr:ch19-occlusion}
Extend \cref{ex:ch19-occlusion}. (a) Plot the UKF estimate and its $2\sigma$
ellipse while the intruder is hidden and relate it to the building. (b) Let
the intruder go right instead of left and confirm that the particle filter
needs no change. (c) Replace the resampling rule by ``resample every step'',
count the distinct particles when the intruder reappears, and explain the
result with \cref{sec:ch19-resampling}. (d) Add roughening (Gaussian jitter
of $0.5\,\mathrm{m}$ in position and $0.05\,\mathrm{rad}$ in heading after
each resampling; the code has a \code{roughening} option) and repeat (c).
(e) Propagate the hidden-phase cloud $3\,\mathrm{s}$ ahead and compute, for a
drone of ours that plans to cross the canyon at $(0, 80)$, the probability
that the intruder is within $10\,\mathrm{m}$ of it at that time; compare with
what a single Gaussian would say.
\end{exercise}
