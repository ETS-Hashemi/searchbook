\section{Why this matters for a drone swarm}
\label{sec:ch19-motivation}

The Kalman filter of \cref{ch:ch18} gives the prediction layer of the hybrid
architecture (\cref{ch:ch24}) a stable estimate of an intruder's position and
velocity, together with a covariance that says how much to trust it. It
rests on two assumptions: the intruder moves according to a \emph{linear}
model, and the sensor reports a \emph{linear} function of the state. Both
assumptions fail in the situations that matter most for collision avoidance.

Consider a survey swarm working over a construction site. An unknown
quadrotor enters the site, flies a straight leg, then banks into a turn to
circle a crane, and straightens out again. A constant-velocity model, which
extrapolates the last velocity, lags behind during every turn: the predicted
position runs straight on while the intruder curves away, and the filter
attributes the mismatch to noise. The natural model for a turning aircraft
is the \textbf{coordinated-turn model}\index{coordinated-turn model}, whose
state carries a speed, a heading and a turn rate. It predicts the arc that a
banked drone actually flies, but its position update contains the sine and
cosine of the heading, so it is nonlinear. At the same time, the sensor that
our drone carries is rarely a device that reports Cartesian coordinates. A
radar, a scanning lidar or a camera with a rangefinder reports how \emph{far}
the intruder is and in which \emph{direction}: a range and a bearing. Range
and bearing are the square root and the arctangent of the position, again
nonlinear. The Kalman filter has no place to put a sine or an arctangent.

This chapter presents the three standard answers, in increasing order of
generality and cost. The \textbf{extended Kalman filter}\index{extended Kalman filter}
(EKF) linearises the models around the current estimate and runs the
Kalman equations on the linearisation. The \textbf{unscented Kalman filter}
\index{unscented Kalman filter} (UKF) avoids derivatives altogether: it pushes
a handful of carefully chosen points through the true nonlinear functions
and fits a Gaussian to what comes out. The \textbf{particle filter}
\index{particle filter} (PF) drops the Gaussian assumption entirely and
represents the belief by thousands of weighted samples, which lets it keep
two hypotheses alive at once, for example ``the intruder went left around
the building'' and ``it went right''. All three sit in the prediction layer
of \cref{ch:ch24}: they consume the raw measurements of the sensor and deliver
a state estimate with its uncertainty to the local avoidance layer
(\cref{ch:ch13,ch:ch14}), to the trajectory predictor of \cref{ch:ch20} and to
the prediction-aware constraints of the planner. In the study plan of
\cref{ch:appA} this is the second half of Week~9.

The chapter proceeds as follows. \Cref{sec:ch19-intuition} explains the one
problem that all three filters solve differently: pushing a Gaussian through
a nonlinear function. \Cref{sec:ch19-problem} fixes the notation, the
coordinated-turn model and the range--bearing sensor. \Cref{sec:ch19-ekf}
derives the EKF, writes out the Jacobians and runs one step by hand;
\cref{sec:ch19-ukf} does the same for the unscented transform and the UKF and
compares the two filters on a turning target; \cref{sec:ch19-pf} develops
the bootstrap particle filter with systematic resampling and shows it
tracking an intruder that disappears behind a building. \Cref{sec:ch19-properties}
collects the properties and costs of the three filters and a decision
guide, \cref{sec:ch19-variants} the main variants, \cref{sec:ch19-implementation}
the implementation, and \cref{sec:ch19-drone} their place in the drone
system.

\section{The idea in plain words}
\label{sec:ch19-intuition}

Every step of every filter in \cref{ch:ch18} does the same thing twice: it
takes a Gaussian belief, pushes it through a function, and describes the
result again by a mean and a covariance. In the predict step the function is
the motion model; in the update step it is the measurement model. As long
as the function is affine, $\vect{y} = \mat{A}\state + \vect{b}$, the result is
again Gaussian, with mean $\mat{A}\vect{\mu} + \vect{b}$ and covariance
$\mat{A}\mat{\Sigma}\mat{A}\T$ (\cref{ch:ch02}), and the Kalman filter is exact.
As soon as the function bends, the output is no longer Gaussian. Push a
Gaussian in range and bearing through the polar-to-Cartesian map and you get
a \emph{banana}: a curved region that no ellipse describes well
(\cref{fig:ch19-idea}). The three filters of this chapter are three ways of
approximating that banana.
\index{linearisation error}\index{banana distribution}

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \inputfigure{ch19/idea}
  \caption{Three ways of pushing a Gaussian belief (left) through a
  nonlinear function $f$ into the curved true output distribution (shaded
  banana on the right). (a) The EKF replaces $f$ by its tangent at the mean
  and moves the ellipse as if $f$ were linear; the ellipse sits on the mean
  of the tangent, not on the mean of the banana, and its ends miss the curved
  tails. (b) The UKF pushes $2n+1$ sigma points through the true $f$ and
  fits an ellipse to the images; the fitted mean lies inside the banana.
  (c) The particle filter pushes a cloud of random samples through $f$ and
  keeps the cloud itself as the belief, curvature included.}
  \label{fig:ch19-idea}
\end{figure}

The EKF replaces the bent function by its tangent at the current estimate.
It costs one Jacobian per step and is exactly the Kalman filter when the
function happens to be linear, but its answer is the Gaussian that the
\emph{tangent} produces: the mean is placed where the tangent puts it, not
where the true output distribution has its centre, and the more the function
curves within the width of the belief, the larger the error. The UKF keeps
the true function and changes the representation of the input instead. It
picks $2n+1$ points, called sigma points, whose sample mean and covariance
are exactly those of the belief, pushes each of them through the true
function, and computes the mean and covariance of the images. No derivative
is needed, and the mean is correct up to second-order terms of the Taylor
expansion, which is precisely the term that the EKF drops. The particle
filter goes one step further: it represents the belief by a large set of
random samples with weights, propagates every sample through the true
function with its own random noise, and re-weights the samples by how well
they explain the measurement. Nothing forces the result to be an ellipse,
so the belief can be curved, skewed or split in two. The price is the number
of samples.

\begin{keyidea}
A nonlinear filter has to push a belief through a bent function. The EKF
bends the belief with the tangent (one Jacobian), the UKF pushes $2n+1$
representative points through the real function (no Jacobian, second-order
accurate mean), and the particle filter pushes thousands of random samples
through it (no Gaussian at all, but $\bigO{N}$ work and the need to
resample). Choose the cheapest one whose assumptions your intruder and your
sensor satisfy.
\end{keyidea}

\section{Problem statement and notation}
\label{sec:ch19-problem}

\subsection{Nonlinear state-space models and the Bayes filter}

\begin{definition}[Nonlinear state-space model]
\label{def:ch19-model}
A \textbf{nonlinear state-space model}\index{state-space model!nonlinear}
consists of a state $\state_k \in \R^n$ at discrete times $k = 0, 1, 2,
\dots$ (spaced by $\dt$), measurements $\meas_k \in \R^m$, a motion model
$f \colon \R^n \to \R^n$ and a measurement model $h \colon \R^n \to \R^m$ with
\begin{equation}
  \state_k = f(\state_{k-1}) + \vect{w}_k, \qquad
  \meas_k = h(\state_k) + \vect{v}_k,
  \label{eq:ch19-model}
\end{equation}
where the process noise $\vect{w}_k \sim \Normal(\vect{0}, \mat{Q}_k)$ and
the measurement noise $\vect{v}_k \sim \Normal(\vect{0}, \mat{R}_k)$ are
independent of each other and over time. The initial state has the
Gaussian prior $\state_0 \sim \Normal(\hat{\state}_0, \mat{P}_0)$. The
\textbf{filtering problem}\index{filtering problem} is to compute, at every
$k$, the posterior density $p(\state_k \mid \meas_{1:k})$ of the current
state given all measurements so far, or at least its mean
$\hat{\state}_k$ and covariance $\mat{P}_k$.
\end{definition}

When $f(\state) = \mat{F}\state$ and $h(\state) = \mat{H}\state$ this is the
linear-Gaussian model of \cref{ch:ch18}, and we keep that chapter's notation:
$\hat{\state}_k^-$ and $\mat{P}_k^-$ are the \emph{predicted} mean and
covariance before the measurement $\meas_k$ is used, $\hat{\state}_k$ and
$\mat{P}_k$ the \emph{updated} ones after it, $\vect{y}_k$ is the innovation,
$\mat{S}_k$ its covariance and $\Kgain_k$ the gain. The Gaussian noise in
\cref{eq:ch19-model} is a convenience for the EKF and the UKF; the particle
filter only needs to be able to \emph{sample} $\vect{w}_k$ and to
\emph{evaluate} the likelihood $p(\meas_k \mid \state_k)$.

All three filters approximate the same two-step recursion, the
\textbf{Bayes filter}\index{Bayes filter}
\cite{thrun2005probabilistic}:
\begin{align}
  p(\state_k \mid \meas_{1:k-1}) &= \int p(\state_k \mid \state_{k-1})\,
    p(\state_{k-1} \mid \meas_{1:k-1})\, \mathrm{d}\state_{k-1},
    \label{eq:ch19-bayes-predict}\\
  p(\state_k \mid \meas_{1:k}) &= \frac{p(\meas_k \mid \state_k)\,
    p(\state_k \mid \meas_{1:k-1})}{\int p(\meas_k \mid \state_k')\,
    p(\state_k' \mid \meas_{1:k-1})\, \mathrm{d}\state_k'}.
    \label{eq:ch19-bayes-update}
\end{align}
The predict step \cref{eq:ch19-bayes-predict} pushes yesterday's posterior
through the motion model, the update step \cref{eq:ch19-bayes-update} is
Bayes' rule of \cref{ch:ch02} with the prediction as the prior and
$p(\meas_k \mid \state_k) = \Normal(\meas_k; h(\state_k), \mat{R}_k)$ as the
likelihood. For linear-Gaussian models both integrals have closed forms and
the recursion is the Kalman filter. For the models below they do not, and
each filter of this chapter replaces the integrals by an approximation:
a linearisation, a set of sigma points, or a set of samples.

\subsection{The coordinated-turn model}
\label{sec:ch19-ct}

A drone that flies at constant speed while banking at a constant angle
follows a circular arc. The \textbf{coordinated-turn model}
\index{coordinated-turn model!definition} describes this motion with the
five-dimensional state
\begin{equation}
  \state = (x, y, v, \psi, \omega)\T,
  \label{eq:ch19-ct-state}
\end{equation}
where $(x, y)$ is the position in the plane, $v \ge 0$ the speed,
$\psi$ the heading (the direction of the velocity, measured
counter-clockwise from the $x$ axis) and $\omega$ the turn rate, the time
derivative of the heading; $\omega > 0$ turns left, $\omega < 0$ right,
$\omega = 0$ is straight flight (\cref{fig:ch19-models}). Integrating
$\dot{x} = v\cos\psi$, $\dot{y} = v\sin\psi$, $\dot{\psi} = \omega$ with
constant $v$ and $\omega$ over one step $\dt$ gives the exact discrete-time
motion model
\begin{equation}
  f(\state) =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    x + \dfrac{v}{\omega}\bigl(\sin(\psi + \omega\dt) - \sin\psi\bigr)\\[1.2ex]
    y - \dfrac{v}{\omega}\bigl(\cos(\psi + \omega\dt) - \cos\psi\bigr)\\[1.2ex]
    v\\
    \psi + \omega\dt\\
    \omega
  \end{pmatrix}
  \quad (\omega \ne 0), \qquad
  f(\state) =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    x + v\dt\cos\psi\\
    y + v\dt\sin\psi\\
    v\\
    \psi\\
    0
  \end{pmatrix}
  \quad (\omega = 0).
  \label{eq:ch19-ct}
\end{equation}
The second form is the limit of the first as $\omega \to 0$, and an
implementation switches to it below a small threshold on $\abs{\omega}$ to
avoid dividing by zero. The model is nonlinear because of the products
$v\sin\psi$ and $v\cos\psi$ and the division by $\omega$.
Bar-Shalom, Li and Kirubarajan~\cite{barshalom2001estimation} write the same
motion with Cartesian velocity components, $(x, \dot{x}, y, \dot{y}, \omega)$;
that form is linear in the first four components when $\omega$ is known, and
the two forms are related by $\dot{x} = v\cos\psi$, $\dot{y} = v\sin\psi$.
We use the polar form because its Jacobian is more compact and because
speed and heading are what the avoidance layer wants to know.

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \inputfigure{ch19/models}
  \caption{The two models of the chapter. The intruder at $(x, y)$ flies with
  speed $v$ along its heading $\psi$ and turns at the rate $\omega$, so its
  path (dashed) is a circular arc of radius $v/\abs{\omega}$. The sensor at
  $\vect{s}$ measures the range $r$ and the bearing $\varphi$ of the intruder,
  both measured in the sensor's frame. Neither the arc nor the pair
  $(r, \varphi)$ is a linear function of the state.}
  \label{fig:ch19-models}
\end{figure}

Real intruders change speed and turn rate. We model these changes as
noise: a random longitudinal acceleration $a_k \sim \Normal(0, \sigma_a^2)$
and a random turn acceleration $\gamma_k \sim \Normal(0, \sigma_\gamma^2)$,
both constant during the step, enter the state through
\begin{equation}
  \vect{w}_k = \mat{G}(\state)\,\vect{\eta}_k, \qquad
  \vect{\eta}_k = (a_k, \gamma_k)\T, \qquad
  \mat{G}(\state) =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \tfrac12\dt^2\cos\psi & 0\\
    \tfrac12\dt^2\sin\psi & 0\\
    \dt & 0\\
    0 & \tfrac12\dt^2\\
    0 & \dt
  \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad
  \mat{Q}(\state) = \mat{G}(\state)
  \begin{pmatrix}\sigma_a^2 & 0\\ 0 & \sigma_\gamma^2\end{pmatrix}
  \mat{G}(\state)\T.
  \label{eq:ch19-ct-noise}
\end{equation}
This is the same construction as the white-noise-acceleration $\mat{Q}$ of
the constant-velocity model in \cref{ch:ch18}, applied along the heading and
to the turn rate. $\mat{Q}$ depends on the heading, so the filters evaluate
it at the current estimate. The two standard deviations are the knobs that
tell the filter how abruptly the intruder may manoeuvre; \cref{sec:ch19-ukf}
uses $\sigma_a = 1.5\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ and $\sigma_\gamma = 0.3\,\mathrm{rad/s^2}$.
\index{process noise!coordinated turn}

\subsection{The range--bearing sensor}
\label{sec:ch19-rb}

\begin{definition}[Range--bearing measurement]
\label{def:ch19-rb}
A sensor at the known position $\vect{s} = (s_x, s_y)$ measures the
\textbf{range}\index{range--bearing measurement} $r$ and the \textbf{bearing}
$\varphi$ of the intruder,
\begin{equation}
  h(\state) =
  \begin{pmatrix} r \\ \varphi \end{pmatrix} =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \sqrt{(x - s_x)^2 + (y - s_y)^2}\\
    \operatorname{atan2}(y - s_y,\, x - s_x)
  \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad
  \meas_k = h(\state_k) + \vect{v}_k, \quad
  \vect{v}_k \sim \Normal\!\bigl(\vect{0}, \diag(\sigma_r^2, \sigma_\varphi^2)\bigr).
  \label{eq:ch19-rb}
\end{equation}
The bearing is an angle in $(-\pi, \pi]$. It depends only on the position, so
$h$ ignores $v$, $\psi$ and $\omega$; the filter learns those three
indirectly, from how the position changes over time.
\end{definition}

The measurement noise of a bearing sensor is often the dominant one. A
bearing error of $\sigma_\varphi$ radians at range $r$ displaces the position
by about $r\sigma_\varphi$ across the line of sight: $2^\circ$ at
$150\,\mathrm{m}$ is $5\,\mathrm{m}$, more than a typical range error. The
error region is therefore a thin arc, not an ellipse, which is the banana of
\cref{fig:ch19-idea}.

\subsection{Angles}
\label{sec:ch19-angles}

Headings and bearings live on a circle, and the filters must respect that.
The difference between the bearings $3.10$ and $-3.10$ is not $6.20$ but
$-0.083$: the two directions are $4.8^\circ$ apart across the $\pm\pi$
seam. We write
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{wrap}(\theta) = \theta - 2\pi\Bigl\lceil \frac{\theta - \pi}{2\pi} \Bigr\rceil
  \in (-\pi, \pi]
  \label{eq:ch19-wrap}
\end{equation}
for the representative of $\theta$ on the circle, and every difference of
two angles in this chapter, in an innovation, in a residual between a sigma
point and a mean, or in a likelihood, is understood as
$\operatorname{wrap}(\theta_1 - \theta_2)$.\index{angle wrapping} The heading
component of an estimate is wrapped after every update. Forgetting this is
the most common bug in a range--bearing tracker, and \cref{sec:ch19-ekf-example}
shows what it does.

\subsection{Jacobians}

\begin{definition}[Jacobian]
\label{def:ch19-jacobian}
The \textbf{Jacobian}\index{Jacobian} of a differentiable function
$g \colon \R^n \to \R^p$ at $\state$ is the $p \times n$ matrix of partial
derivatives
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial g}{\partial \state}(\state) =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \partial g_1/\partial x_1 & \cdots & \partial g_1/\partial x_n\\
    \vdots & & \vdots\\
    \partial g_p/\partial x_1 & \cdots & \partial g_p/\partial x_n
  \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
It is the matrix of the best affine approximation of $g$ near $\state$:
$g(\state + \vect{d}) = g(\state) + \frac{\partial g}{\partial \state}(\state)\,
\vect{d} + \bigO{\norm{\vect{d}}^2}$.
\end{definition}

The EKF needs the Jacobians of $f$ and $h$; \cref{sec:ch19-ekf-jacobians}
writes them out for the two models above. The UKF and the particle filter
need none.

\section{The extended Kalman filter}
\label{sec:ch19-ekf}

\subsection{Linearise, then run the Kalman filter}

Suppose the belief after step $k-1$ is $\Normal(\hat{\state}_{k-1},
\mat{P}_{k-1})$ and we want the predicted belief. The EKF replaces $f$ by
its tangent at the current estimate,
\begin{equation}
  f(\state) \approx f(\hat{\state}_{k-1}) + \mat{F}_k\,(\state - \hat{\state}_{k-1}),
  \qquad
  \mat{F}_k = \frac{\partial f}{\partial \state}(\hat{\state}_{k-1}),
  \label{eq:ch19-ekf-linearise-f}
\end{equation}
and applies the affine rule of \cref{ch:ch02}: the mean goes through the
function, the covariance is squeezed between the Jacobian and its
transpose. For the update it linearises $h$ at the \emph{predicted} mean,
\begin{equation}
  h(\state) \approx h(\hat{\state}_k^-) + \mat{H}_k\,(\state - \hat{\state}_k^-),
  \qquad
  \mat{H}_k = \frac{\partial h}{\partial \state}(\hat{\state}_k^-),
  \label{eq:ch19-ekf-linearise-h}
\end{equation}
and runs the Kalman update of \cref{ch:ch18} with $\mat{H}_k$ in place of the
measurement matrix and the true $h(\hat{\state}_k^-)$ in place of
$\mat{H}\hat{\state}_k^-$. Written out, one step of the EKF is
\begin{align}
  \hat{\state}_k^- &= f(\hat{\state}_{k-1}),
  & \mat{P}_k^- &= \mat{F}_k \mat{P}_{k-1} \mat{F}_k\T + \mat{Q}_k,
  \label{eq:ch19-ekf-predict}\\
  \vect{y}_k &= \meas_k \ominus h(\hat{\state}_k^-),
  & \mat{S}_k &= \mat{H}_k \mat{P}_k^- \mat{H}_k\T + \mat{R}_k,
  \label{eq:ch19-ekf-innovation}\\
  \Kgain_k &= \mat{P}_k^- \mat{H}_k\T \mat{S}_k\inv,
  & \hat{\state}_k &= \hat{\state}_k^- + \Kgain_k \vect{y}_k,
  \qquad
  \mat{P}_k = (\mat{I} - \Kgain_k \mat{H}_k)\,\mat{P}_k^-,
  \label{eq:ch19-ekf-update}
\end{align}
where $\ominus$ subtracts componentwise and wraps the angular components
\cref{eq:ch19-wrap}. Two points deserve emphasis. First, the mean is
propagated through the \emph{true} functions $f$ and $h$; only the
covariance and the gain use the linearisation. Second, the Jacobians are
evaluated at the current estimate, so $\mat{F}_k$ and $\mat{H}_k$ change from
step to step and depend on the data; unlike the Kalman filter, the EKF
covariance cannot be computed in advance, and a wrong estimate produces a
wrong Jacobian, which produces a wrong gain (\cref{sec:ch19-ekf-failure}).
\index{extended Kalman filter!equations}

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
\caption{One step of the extended Kalman filter}
\label{alg:ch19-ekf}
\KwIn{previous estimate $\hat{\state}_{k-1}$, $\mat{P}_{k-1}$; measurement $\meas_k$ (or \KwNil if none); models $f$, $h$ with Jacobians, noise $\mat{Q}_k$, $\mat{R}_k$; index set $\mathcal{A}$ of the angular components of $\meas$ and of $\state$}
\KwOut{updated estimate $\hat{\state}_k$, $\mat{P}_k$}
\Fn{\NlfEkfStep{$\hat{\state}_{k-1}$, $\mat{P}_{k-1}$, $\meas_k$}}{
  $\mat{F}_k \gets \partial f / \partial\state$ evaluated at $\hat{\state}_{k-1}$\label{alg:ch19-ekf:F}\;
  $\hat{\state}_k^- \gets f(\hat{\state}_{k-1})$; wrap the angular components of $\hat{\state}_k^-$\label{alg:ch19-ekf:predict}\;
  $\mat{P}_k^- \gets \mat{F}_k \mat{P}_{k-1} \mat{F}_k\T + \mat{Q}_k$\label{alg:ch19-ekf:P}\;
  \If{$\meas_k = $ \KwNil}{\KwRet $(\hat{\state}_k^-, \mat{P}_k^-)$\tcp*{missing measurement: predict only}\label{alg:ch19-ekf:missing}}
  $\mat{H}_k \gets \partial h / \partial\state$ evaluated at $\hat{\state}_k^-$\label{alg:ch19-ekf:H}\;
  $\vect{y}_k \gets \meas_k - h(\hat{\state}_k^-)$; $\vect{y}_k[j] \gets$ \NlfWrap{$\vect{y}_k[j]$} for $j \in \mathcal{A}$\label{alg:ch19-ekf:innovation}\;
  $\mat{S}_k \gets \mat{H}_k \mat{P}_k^- \mat{H}_k\T + \mat{R}_k$; $\Kgain_k \gets \mat{P}_k^- \mat{H}_k\T \mat{S}_k\inv$\label{alg:ch19-ekf:gain}\;
  $\hat{\state}_k \gets \hat{\state}_k^- + \Kgain_k \vect{y}_k$; wrap the angular components of $\hat{\state}_k$\label{alg:ch19-ekf:update}\;
  $\mat{P}_k \gets (\mat{I} - \Kgain_k \mat{H}_k)\,\mat{P}_k^- (\mat{I} - \Kgain_k \mat{H}_k)\T + \Kgain_k \mat{R}_k \Kgain_k\T$\tcp*{Joseph form}\label{alg:ch19-ekf:joseph}
  \KwRet $(\hat{\state}_k, \mat{P}_k)$\;
}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Walkthrough.}
Lines~\ref{alg:ch19-ekf:F}--\ref{alg:ch19-ekf:P} are the predict step
\cref{eq:ch19-ekf-predict}. The Jacobian on line~\ref{alg:ch19-ekf:F} is
evaluated at the \emph{previous} estimate, the point around which $f$ is
linearised; the heading component of the predicted mean is wrapped on
line~\ref{alg:ch19-ekf:predict} so that it stays in $(-\pi, \pi]$.
Line~\ref{alg:ch19-ekf:missing} handles a step without a measurement, for
instance when the sensor loses the intruder behind an obstacle: the
prediction becomes the estimate, exactly as in \cref{ch:ch18}. The update
starts on line~\ref{alg:ch19-ekf:H} with the Jacobian of $h$ at the
\emph{predicted} mean, the point around which $h$ is linearised. The
innovation on line~\ref{alg:ch19-ekf:innovation} compares the actual
measurement with the predicted one, and its bearing component is wrapped;
without the wrap, an intruder near the $\pm\pi$ seam produces an innovation
of almost $2\pi$ and the filter jumps to a wrong estimate
(\cref{sec:ch19-ekf-example}). Lines~\ref{alg:ch19-ekf:gain}
and~\ref{alg:ch19-ekf:update} are the Kalman gain and the corrected mean.
Line~\ref{alg:ch19-ekf:joseph} uses the Joseph form of the covariance
update, which is algebraically equal to $(\mat{I} - \Kgain_k\mat{H}_k)\mat{P}_k^-$
but stays symmetric and positive definite under rounding; the plain form is
fine on paper and risky in a filter that runs for thousands of steps.

\subsection{The Jacobians of the two models}
\label{sec:ch19-ekf-jacobians}

\begin{proposition}[Jacobian of the coordinated-turn model]
\label{thm:ch19-ct-jacobian}
Let $\psi' = \psi + \omega\dt$. For $\omega \ne 0$ the Jacobian of
\cref{eq:ch19-ct} is
\begin{equation}
  \mat{F} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial \state} =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    1 & 0 & \dfrac{\sin\psi' - \sin\psi}{\omega}
      & \dfrac{v}{\omega}\bigl(\cos\psi' - \cos\psi\bigr)
      & \dfrac{v\dt\cos\psi'}{\omega} - \dfrac{v\,(\sin\psi' - \sin\psi)}{\omega^2}\\[1.6ex]
    0 & 1 & -\dfrac{\cos\psi' - \cos\psi}{\omega}
      & \dfrac{v}{\omega}\bigl(\sin\psi' - \sin\psi\bigr)
      & \dfrac{v\dt\sin\psi'}{\omega} + \dfrac{v\,(\cos\psi' - \cos\psi)}{\omega^2}\\[1.6ex]
    0 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0\\
    0 & 0 & 0 & 1 & \dt\\
    0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 1
  \end{pmatrix},
  \label{eq:ch19-ct-jacobian}
\end{equation}
and for $\omega = 0$ the entries of the first two rows become
$F_{13} = \dt\cos\psi$, $F_{14} = -v\dt\sin\psi$,
$F_{15} = -\tfrac12 v\dt^2\sin\psi$, $F_{23} = \dt\sin\psi$,
$F_{24} = v\dt\cos\psi$ and $F_{25} = \tfrac12 v\dt^2\cos\psi$.
\index{Jacobian!coordinated turn}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Rows three to five are the derivatives of $v$, $\psi + \omega\dt$ and
$\omega$. In the first row, $x$ enters only through the additive term
(entry~$1$), $y$ not at all, and $v$ linearly with the factor
$(\sin\psi' - \sin\psi)/\omega$. Differentiating
$\frac{v}{\omega}(\sin\psi' - \sin\psi)$ with respect to $\psi$ uses
$\partial\psi'/\partial\psi = 1$ and gives $\frac{v}{\omega}(\cos\psi' -
\cos\psi)$. Differentiating it with respect to $\omega$ uses the quotient
rule on $1/\omega$ and $\partial\psi'/\partial\omega = \dt$:
$-\frac{v}{\omega^2}(\sin\psi' - \sin\psi) + \frac{v}{\omega}\dt\cos\psi'$.
The second row is the same computation for $-\frac{v}{\omega}(\cos\psi' -
\cos\psi)$. The $\omega = 0$ entries follow from the second form of
\cref{eq:ch19-ct}, or as limits of the first form: expanding
$\sin(\psi + \omega\dt) = \sin\psi + \omega\dt\cos\psi -
\tfrac12\omega^2\dt^2\sin\psi + \bigO{\omega^3}$ gives
$x' = x + v\dt\cos\psi - \tfrac12 v\omega\dt^2\sin\psi + \bigO{\omega^2}$,
whose derivative with respect to $\omega$ at $0$ is
$-\tfrac12 v\dt^2\sin\psi$. \Cref{exr:ch19-ct-jacobian} asks you to carry
out the remaining limits; the self-test of the chapter code checks every
entry against finite differences.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Jacobian of the range--bearing model]
\label{thm:ch19-rb-jacobian}
With $d_x = x - s_x$, $d_y = y - s_y$ and $r = \sqrt{d_x^2 + d_y^2} > 0$,
the Jacobian of \cref{eq:ch19-rb} is
\begin{equation}
  \mat{H} = \frac{\partial h}{\partial \state} =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \dfrac{d_x}{r} & \dfrac{d_y}{r} & 0 & 0 & 0\\[1.4ex]
    -\dfrac{d_y}{r^2} & \dfrac{d_x}{r^2} & 0 & 0 & 0
  \end{pmatrix}.
  \label{eq:ch19-rb-jacobian}
\end{equation}
\index{Jacobian!range--bearing}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$\partial r/\partial x = d_x/r$ and $\partial r/\partial y = d_y/r$ by the
chain rule on the square root. For the bearing, $\varphi =
\operatorname{atan2}(d_y, d_x)$ has $\partial\varphi/\partial x =
-d_y/(d_x^2 + d_y^2)$ and $\partial\varphi/\partial y = d_x/(d_x^2 + d_y^2)$
wherever $r > 0$, on every branch of the arctangent. The last three columns
vanish because $h$ does not depend on $v$, $\psi$, $\omega$.
\end{proof}

The first row of $\mat{H}$ is the unit vector from the sensor to the
intruder: a range measurement constrains the position along the line of
sight. The second row is the perpendicular unit vector divided by $r$: a
bearing measurement constrains the position across the line of sight, and
the further the intruder, the less a radian of bearing is worth. This is
the geometry behind the banana.

\subsection{A worked example: one EKF step by hand}
\label{sec:ch19-ekf-example}

\begin{example}[One EKF step with a range--bearing measurement]
\label{ex:ch19-ekf}
Our drone hovers at the origin, $\vect{s} = (0, 0)$, and observes an intruder
once per second ($\dt = 1\,\mathrm{s}$). After step $k-1$ the estimate is
\[
  \hat{\state}_{k-1} = (-100,\ -4,\ 10,\ \pi,\ 0.3)\T, \qquad
  \mat{P}_{k-1} = \diag(4,\ 4,\ 1,\ 0.04,\ 0.01),
\]
in metres, metres per second, radians and radians per second: the intruder
is $100\,\mathrm{m}$ to the west, flies west ($\psi = \pi$) at
$10\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and turns left at $0.3\,\mathrm{rad/s}$, so its path
curves towards the south. The standard deviations are $2\,\mathrm{m}$ in
position, $1\,\mathrm{m/s}$ in speed, $0.2\,\mathrm{rad}$ ($11.5^\circ$) in
heading and $0.1\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ in turn rate. The process noise has
$\sigma_a = 1\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ and $\sigma_\gamma = 0.1\,\mathrm{rad/s^2}$,
the sensor $\sigma_r = 2\,\mathrm{m}$ and $\sigma_\varphi = 0.03\,\mathrm{rad}$
($1.7^\circ$). The new measurement is $\meas_k = (111.5\,\mathrm{m},\
3.12\,\mathrm{rad})$. The instance is \code{worked\_example()} in
\code{ch19\_nonlinear\_filters.py}, and every number below is printed by
its self-test.
\end{example}

\paragraph{Predict.}
With $v/\omega = 33.33$, $\psi' = \pi + 0.3$, $\sin\psi' = -0.2955$ and
$\cos\psi' = -0.9553$, \cref{eq:ch19-ct} gives the predicted position
$x^- = -100 + 33.33\,(-0.2955 - 0) = -109.85$ and
$y^- = -4 - 33.33\,(-0.9553 + 1) = -5.49$: the intruder has moved
$9.85\,\mathrm{m}$ west and $1.49\,\mathrm{m}$ south. The heading becomes
$\pi + 0.3$, which wraps to $-2.8416$; speed and turn rate are unchanged.
The Jacobian \cref{eq:ch19-ct-jacobian} at $\hat{\state}_{k-1}$ has the
non-trivial entries
\[
  F_{13} = -0.9851,\quad F_{14} = 1.4888,\quad F_{15} = 0.9910,\quad
  F_{23} = -0.1489,\quad F_{24} = -9.8507,\quad F_{25} = -4.8881,
\]
so a heading error of $0.1\,\mathrm{rad}$ moves the predicted position by
about $1\,\mathrm{m}$ across the direction of flight ($F_{24}$), and the
predicted covariance $\mat{P}_k^- = \mat{F}_k\mat{P}_{k-1}\mat{F}_k\T + \mat{Q}_k$
grows accordingly: its position block becomes
\[
  \begin{pmatrix} 5.319 & -0.488 \\ -0.488 & 8.143 \end{pmatrix}
  \quad (\text{standard deviations } 2.31\,\mathrm{m} \text{ and } 2.85\,\mathrm{m}),
\]
larger in $y$ than in $x$ because the heading uncertainty acts across the
westward flight, and $\mat{P}_k^-$ now carries a covariance of $-0.443$
between $y$ and $\psi$ and of $-1.485$ between $x$ and $v$: the update will
use these correlations to correct heading and speed from a position
measurement.

\paragraph{Update.}
The predicted measurement is $h(\hat{\state}_k^-) = (r, \varphi) =
(109.99, -3.0917)$: the intruder is expected slightly south of the negative
$x$ axis, so its bearing is just above $-\pi$. The actual bearing,
$3.12$, is just below $+\pi$. Subtracting naively gives $3.12 - (-3.0917) =
6.2117$, an innovation of $356^\circ$ that would send the estimate to the
other side of the sensor; wrapping gives $\operatorname{wrap}(6.2117) =
-0.0715\,\mathrm{rad}$, and the innovation is $\vect{y}_k = (1.51,\
-0.0715)$: the intruder is $1.5\,\mathrm{m}$ further away and $4.1^\circ$
clockwise of the prediction. The Jacobian \cref{eq:ch19-rb-jacobian} at the
predicted position is
\[
  \mat{H}_k = \begin{pmatrix} -0.9988 & -0.0499 & 0 & 0 & 0\\
  0.00045 & -0.00908 & 0 & 0 & 0\end{pmatrix},
\]
the innovation covariance is $\mat{S}_k = \diag(9.28, 0.00158)$ up to an
off-diagonal term of $-0.003$, and the gain maps the range innovation
mostly into $x$ and the bearing innovation mostly into $y$: $K_{11} =
-0.569$ and $K_{22} = -47.1$. The second value is not a typo: a radian of
bearing at $110\,\mathrm{m}$ is $110\,\mathrm{m}$ of displacement across the
line of sight, so the bearing gain is of the order of $-r$. The rows of
$\Kgain_k$ for the unmeasured components are where the correlations of
$\mat{P}_k^-$ act: $K_{42} = 2.56$ turns a bearing innovation into a heading
correction and $K_{31} = 0.16$ a range innovation into a speed correction.
\Cref{tab:ch19-ekf-trace} lists the resulting estimate. The position moved
$1.09\,\mathrm{m}$ further west and $3.35\,\mathrm{m}$ north, the latter
almost entirely from the bearing; the heading turned by $-0.19\,\mathrm{rad}$
towards due west (an intruder found north of the predicted arc is best
explained by a less southerly heading), the speed rose by $0.19\,\mathrm{m/s}$
and the turn rate fell slightly. The updated standard deviations of the
position are $1.51\,\mathrm{m}$ along the line of sight, set by the range
noise, and $2.16\,\mathrm{m}$ across it, set by the bearing noise at this
range.
\Cref{exr:ch19-wrapping} asks what the filter would have done with the
unwrapped innovation.

\subsection{Where the EKF fails}
\label{sec:ch19-ekf-failure}

The EKF is exact for linear models and very good whenever the functions are
nearly linear over the width of the belief. Two situations break it.

\paragraph{Strong nonlinearity.}
\Cref{fig:ch19-linearisation} makes the linearisation error visible. A
Gaussian belief in range and bearing, $r \sim \Normal(100, 5^2)$ and
$\varphi \sim \Normal(\pi/2, 0.3^2)$, is pushed through the polar-to-Cartesian
map $(r, \varphi) \mapsto (r\cos\varphi, r\sin\varphi)$; this is what a
filter does when it initialises a track from a single measurement, and,
mirrored, what the update does to a belief whose cross-range uncertainty is
large. The true output, estimated from $2\,000$ samples, has its mean at
$(-0.2, 95.6)$, and the exact mean is $100\,e^{-0.3^2/2} = 95.6$: the
samples lie on an arc of radius $100$, and the average of points on an arc
is \emph{inside} the arc. The linearised map puts the mean at the image of
the input mean, $(0, 100)$, $4.4\,\mathrm{m}$ too far out, and its ellipse
is a straight bar that cannot follow the curved tails: its standard
deviation along $y$ is $5.0\,\mathrm{m}$ where the samples spread by
$7.7\,\mathrm{m}$, and its $2\sigma$ ellipse contains $74.6\,\%$ of the
samples instead of the nominal $86.5\,\%$; the ones it misses are exactly
the tails that a collision check would care about. Nothing in the EKF
signals the error; it reports a confident covariance around a biased mean.
The unscented transform of \cref{sec:ch19-ukf} puts the mean at
$(0.0, 95.6)$ with five function evaluations and no derivative, and its
ellipse contains $91.6\,\%$ of the samples.

\end{figure}

\paragraph{A poor initial estimate.}
The Jacobians are evaluated at the estimate. If the estimate is far from
the truth, $\mat{F}_k$ and $\mat{H}_k$ describe the wrong tangent, the gain is
computed for a fictitious geometry, and an update can push the estimate
further away instead of closer, while the covariance, which knows nothing
about the error, keeps shrinking: the filter has
\textbf{diverged}\index{divergence (EKF)}. Whether this happens depends on
how strongly the measurements constrain the state. A range--bearing sensor
measures the position directly, so a position error is corrected in one
step, and a heading error is corrected through the correlations that the
process noise builds up between position and heading over a few steps; in
the experiment of \cref{sec:ch19-ukf-compare} the EKF recovers from a
$90^\circ$ heading error and an overconfident covariance in every run.
Divergence is the real danger with weak or bearing-only measurements, long
gaps, and an initial covariance small enough to make the filter ignore its
innovations. The cures are an honest $\mat{P}_0$, a filter whose
approximation does not depend on a tangent, and, when the measurements
cannot resolve the ambiguity, a particle filter that keeps several
hypotheses alive. The same experiment shows the other side of the coin: a
particle filter started with an overconfident cloud that contains no
particle near the truth fares worse than the EKF.

\section{The unscented Kalman filter}
\label{sec:ch19-ukf}

\subsection{The unscented transform}
\label{sec:ch19-ut}

The EKF approximates the \emph{function}. Julier and
Uhlmann~\cite{julier1997new,julier2004unscented} proposed to approximate the
\emph{distribution} instead: represent the Gaussian by a small set of
points that has exactly its mean and covariance, push the points through
the true function, and read the mean and covariance off the images. The
construction is the \textbf{unscented transform}\index{unscented transform}
(UT); its points are the \textbf{sigma points}\index{sigma points}.

\begin{definition}[Sigma points and the unscented transform]
\label{def:ch19-ut}
Let $\state \in \R^n$ have mean $\bar{\state}$ and covariance $\mat{P}$, and
let $g \colon \R^n \to \R^p$. Fix the parameters $\alpha \in (0, 1]$,
$\beta \ge 0$ and $\kappa$, set
\begin{equation}
  \lambda = \alpha^2 (n + \kappa) - n,
  \label{eq:ch19-lambda}
\end{equation}
and let $\mat{L}$ be a matrix square root of $(n + \lambda)\mat{P}$, that is,
$\mat{L}\mat{L}\T = (n + \lambda)\mat{P}$, with columns $\vect{l}_1, \dots,
\vect{l}_n$. The $2n + 1$ \textbf{sigma points} and their weights are
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \mathcal{X}_0 &= \bar{\state}, &
    W_0^{(m)} &= \frac{\lambda}{n + \lambda}, &
    W_0^{(c)} &= \frac{\lambda}{n + \lambda} + 1 - \alpha^2 + \beta,\\
    \mathcal{X}_i &= \bar{\state} + \vect{l}_i, &
    W_i^{(m)} &= W_i^{(c)} = \frac{1}{2(n + \lambda)}, & & i = 1, \dots, n,\\
    \mathcal{X}_{n+i} &= \bar{\state} - \vect{l}_i, &
    W_{n+i}^{(m)} &= W_{n+i}^{(c)} = \frac{1}{2(n + \lambda)}, & & i = 1, \dots, n.
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:ch19-sigma-points}
\end{equation}
The \textbf{unscented transform} of $(\bar{\state}, \mat{P})$ through $g$
maps every sigma point, $\mathcal{Y}_i = g(\mathcal{X}_i)$, and returns
\begin{equation}
  \bar{\vect{y}} = \sum_{i=0}^{2n} W_i^{(m)}\,\mathcal{Y}_i, \qquad
  \mat{P}_{yy} = \sum_{i=0}^{2n} W_i^{(c)}\,(\mathcal{Y}_i - \bar{\vect{y}})
    (\mathcal{Y}_i - \bar{\vect{y}})\T, \qquad
  \mat{P}_{xy} = \sum_{i=0}^{2n} W_i^{(c)}\,(\mathcal{X}_i - \bar{\state})
    (\mathcal{Y}_i - \bar{\vect{y}})\T,
  \label{eq:ch19-ut}
\end{equation}
the approximate mean and covariance of $g(\state)$ and the cross-covariance
between $\state$ and $g(\state)$.
\end{definition}

The points sit on the principal axes of the covariance ellipsoid at a
distance of $\sqrt{n + \lambda} = \alpha\sqrt{n + \kappa}$ standard
deviations from the mean, one pair per column of $\mat{L}$
(\cref{fig:ch19-linearisation}, right). \Cref{sec:ch19-properties} proves
that the mean weights sum to one, that the weighted sample mean and
covariance of the sigma points are exactly $\bar{\state}$ and $\mat{P}$
whatever the parameters, that the transform is exact for affine $g$, and
that its mean is correct to second order for any smooth $g$. The three
parameters control what happens beyond that:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\alpha$ scales the spread of the points. $\alpha = 1$ places them at
  $\sqrt{n + \kappa}$ standard deviations; small values such as $10^{-3}$
  pull them close to the mean, which makes the transform behave like a
  central-difference linearisation and avoids sampling far-away regions
  where $g$ may be badly behaved. The usual range is
  $10^{-3} \le \alpha \le 1$.
  \item $\beta$ enters only $W_0^{(c)}$ and encodes knowledge of the
  distribution's fourth moment; $\beta = 2$ is optimal for a Gaussian
  \cite{wan2000unscented}.
  \item $\kappa$ is a secondary spread parameter; the common choices are
  $\kappa = 0$ and $\kappa = 3 - n$, the latter being Julier and Uhlmann's
  original choice, which matches the fourth moment of a Gaussian for
  $n \le 3$ but makes $\lambda$ and $W_0^{(m)}$ negative for larger $n$.
\end{itemize}
The chapter code uses $\alpha = 1$, $\beta = 2$, $\kappa = 0$ for the
five-dimensional tracking problem, so $\lambda = 0$, the points are at
$\sqrt{5} \approx 2.24$ standard deviations, $W_0^{(m)} = 0$,
$W_0^{(c)} = 2$ and all other weights are $0.1$; for the two-dimensional
example of \cref{fig:ch19-linearisation} it uses $\kappa = 3 - n = 1$, so
$\lambda = 1$, the points are at $\sqrt{3}$ standard deviations,
$W_0^{(m)} = 1/3$, $W_0^{(c)} = 7/3$ and $W_i = 1/6$.
\index{unscented transform!parameters}

\paragraph{The matrix square root.}
Any $\mat{L}$ with $\mat{L}\mat{L}\T = (n + \lambda)\mat{P}$ works, and the
standard choice is the \textbf{Cholesky factor}\index{Cholesky factorisation}:
the unique lower-triangular $\mat{L}$ with positive diagonal, computed in
$\bigO{n^3}$ by \code{numpy.linalg.cholesky}. For a diagonal $\mat{P}$ the
Cholesky factor is simply the diagonal matrix of standard deviations
scaled by $\sqrt{n + \lambda}$, which is what makes the worked example
below easy to check by hand. The factorisation requires $\mat{P}$ to be
symmetric positive definite; \cref{sec:ch19-implementation} discusses what
to do when rounding makes it fail.

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
\caption{Sigma points and the unscented transform}
\label{alg:ch19-ut}
\KwIn{mean $\bar{\state} \in \R^n$, covariance $\mat{P}$, parameters $\alpha, \beta, \kappa$; function $g$; index sets $\mathcal{A}_x$, $\mathcal{A}_y$ of the angular components of $\state$ and of $g(\state)$}
\KwOut{sigma points and weights; transformed mean, covariance and cross-covariance}
\Fn{\NlfSigmaPoints{$\bar{\state}$, $\mat{P}$, $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\kappa$}}{
  $\lambda \gets \alpha^2(n + \kappa) - n$\;
  $\mat{L} \gets$ Cholesky factor of $(n + \lambda)\mat{P}$\label{alg:ch19-ut:chol}\;
  $\mathcal{X}_0 \gets \bar{\state}$; $\mathcal{X}_i \gets \bar{\state} + \vect{l}_i$; $\mathcal{X}_{n+i} \gets \bar{\state} - \vect{l}_i$ for $i = 1, \dots, n$\label{alg:ch19-ut:points}\;
  $W_0^{(m)} \gets \lambda/(n+\lambda)$; $W_0^{(c)} \gets W_0^{(m)} + 1 - \alpha^2 + \beta$; $W_i^{(m)} = W_i^{(c)} \gets 1/(2(n+\lambda))$ for $i = 1, \dots, 2n$\label{alg:ch19-ut:weights}\;
  \KwRet $(\mathcal{X}, W^{(m)}, W^{(c)})$\;
}
\Fn{\NlfUnscentedTransform{$\mathcal{X}$, $W^{(m)}$, $W^{(c)}$, $g$}}{
  $\mathcal{Y}_i \gets g(\mathcal{X}_i)$ for $i = 0, \dots, 2n$\label{alg:ch19-ut:map}\;
  $\bar{\vect{y}} \gets \sum_i W_i^{(m)} \mathcal{Y}_i$; for $j \in \mathcal{A}_y$ use $\bar{y}_j \gets \mathcal{Y}_{0,j} + \sum_i W_i^{(m)}\,$\NlfWrap{$\mathcal{Y}_{i,j} - \mathcal{Y}_{0,j}$}\label{alg:ch19-ut:mean}\;
  $\vect{e}_i \gets \mathcal{Y}_i \ominus \bar{\vect{y}}$; $\vect{d}_i \gets \mathcal{X}_i \ominus \bar{\state}$ (wrapped in the components $\mathcal{A}_y$, $\mathcal{A}_x$)\label{alg:ch19-ut:residuals}\;
  $\mat{P}_{yy} \gets \sum_i W_i^{(c)} \vect{e}_i \vect{e}_i\T$; $\mat{P}_{xy} \gets \sum_i W_i^{(c)} \vect{d}_i \vect{e}_i\T$\label{alg:ch19-ut:cov}\;
  \KwRet $(\bar{\vect{y}}, \mat{P}_{yy}, \mat{P}_{xy})$\;
}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Walkthrough.}
Line~\ref{alg:ch19-ut:chol} is the only expensive step, and it is the
reason the UKF costs about as much as the EKF. Line~\ref{alg:ch19-ut:points}
builds the symmetric set of points and line~\ref{alg:ch19-ut:weights} the
two weight vectors; note that the covariance weights do not sum to one,
only the mean weights do. The transform itself is three lines. The mean on
line~\ref{alg:ch19-ut:mean} needs care for angles: the ordinary weighted
mean of the bearings $3.1$ and $-3.1$ is $0$, which points in the opposite
direction. Taking the residuals relative to the central image
$\mathcal{Y}_0$, wrapping them, averaging and adding the result back to
$\mathcal{Y}_0$ gives the correct answer as long as the images spread over
less than half a turn. The residuals on line~\ref{alg:ch19-ut:residuals}
are wrapped for the same reason, and line~\ref{alg:ch19-ut:cov} forms the
two covariances. The cross-covariance $\mat{P}_{xy}$ is what makes the UKF
possible: it plays the role that $\mat{P}\mat{H}\T$ plays in the EKF, without
an $\mat{H}$.

\subsection{The filter}
\label{sec:ch19-ukf-step}

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
\caption{One step of the unscented Kalman filter (additive noise)}
\label{alg:ch19-ukf}
\KwIn{previous estimate $\hat{\state}_{k-1}$, $\mat{P}_{k-1}$; measurement $\meas_k$ or \KwNil; models $f$, $h$, noise $\mat{Q}_k$, $\mat{R}_k$; parameters $\alpha, \beta, \kappa$}
\KwOut{updated estimate $\hat{\state}_k$, $\mat{P}_k$}
\Fn{\NlfUkfStep{$\hat{\state}_{k-1}$, $\mat{P}_{k-1}$, $\meas_k$}}{
  $(\mathcal{X}, W^{(m)}, W^{(c)}) \gets$ \NlfSigmaPoints{$\hat{\state}_{k-1}$, $\mat{P}_{k-1}$, $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\kappa$}\label{alg:ch19-ukf:sp1}\;
  $(\hat{\state}_k^-, \mat{P}_k^-, \cdot) \gets$ \NlfUnscentedTransform{$\mathcal{X}$, $W^{(m)}$, $W^{(c)}$, $f$}; $\mat{P}_k^- \gets \mat{P}_k^- + \mat{Q}_k$\label{alg:ch19-ukf:predict}\;
  \If{$\meas_k = $ \KwNil}{\KwRet $(\hat{\state}_k^-, \mat{P}_k^-)$\;}
  $(\mathcal{X}^-, W^{(m)}, W^{(c)}) \gets$ \NlfSigmaPoints{$\hat{\state}_k^-$, $\mat{P}_k^-$, $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\kappa$}\tcp*{redraw around the prediction}\label{alg:ch19-ukf:sp2}
  $(\hat{\meas}_k, \mat{S}_k, \mat{C}_k) \gets$ \NlfUnscentedTransform{$\mathcal{X}^-$, $W^{(m)}$, $W^{(c)}$, $h$}; $\mat{S}_k \gets \mat{S}_k + \mat{R}_k$\label{alg:ch19-ukf:measure}\;
  $\Kgain_k \gets \mat{C}_k \mat{S}_k\inv$\label{alg:ch19-ukf:gain}\;
  $\vect{y}_k \gets \meas_k \ominus \hat{\meas}_k$\label{alg:ch19-ukf:innovation}\;
  $\hat{\state}_k \gets \hat{\state}_k^- + \Kgain_k \vect{y}_k$; wrap the angular components\label{alg:ch19-ukf:update}\;
  $\mat{P}_k \gets \mat{P}_k^- - \Kgain_k \mat{S}_k \Kgain_k\T$\label{alg:ch19-ukf:cov}\;
  \KwRet $(\hat{\state}_k, \mat{P}_k)$\;
}
\end{algorithm}

\Cref{alg:ch19-ukf} is the EKF with both linearisations replaced by
unscented transforms \cite{wan2000unscented}. Line~\ref{alg:ch19-ukf:sp1}
draws sigma points around the previous estimate and
line~\ref{alg:ch19-ukf:predict} pushes them through the motion model; the
process noise is additive, so $\mat{Q}_k$ is simply added to the transformed
covariance. Line~\ref{alg:ch19-ukf:sp2} draws a fresh set of sigma points
around the prediction. Reusing the propagated points $f(\mathcal{X}_i)$ is
also common and saves one factorisation, but those points no longer
represent $\mat{P}_k^-$ once $\mat{Q}_k$ has been added; redrawing is the safer
choice. Line~\ref{alg:ch19-ukf:measure} maps the new points through $h$ and
returns the predicted measurement, the innovation covariance and the
cross-covariance $\mat{C}_k$ between state and measurement. The gain on
line~\ref{alg:ch19-ukf:gain} is $\mat{C}_k\mat{S}_k\inv$; in the EKF,
$\mat{C}_k = \mat{P}_k^-\mat{H}_k\T$ and this is \cref{eq:ch19-ekf-update}. The
covariance update on line~\ref{alg:ch19-ukf:cov} is the form
$\mat{P}_k^- - \Kgain_k\mat{S}_k\Kgain_k\T$, which equals
$(\mat{I} - \Kgain_k\mat{H}_k)\mat{P}_k^-$ in the linear case and needs no
$\mat{H}_k$. Every evaluation of $f$ and $h$ is of the true nonlinear
function; the only approximation is the Gaussian fitted to $2n+1$ images.
\index{unscented Kalman filter!equations}

displacement between them (the speed clipped to at most $30\,\mathrm{m/s}$,

reflects the measurement noise at that range. The raw measurement,
converted to Cartesian coordinates, is the yardstick, as in \cref{ch:ch18}:
a filter that cannot beat it is useless for the planner. Errors are
averaged after a settling period of $5\,\mathrm{s}$, so that the
initialisation does not dominate the comparison.

surprise for anyone who expects the EKF to be the fragile one. Started

steps (the EKF a little more slowly, $3.1$ against $2.9\,\mathrm{m}$),
because the measurements pin the position down at every step and the
process noise re-inflates the covariance until the gain takes the
correction. The particle filter suffers more: its initial cloud contains no
particle with the right heading, the likelihood favours the least wrong
ones, and recovery depends on the process noise carrying some of them to
the truth before the cloud collapses; its mean RMSE is $5.7\,\mathrm{m}$,
its median $3.1\,\mathrm{m}$, and one run is lost. A particle filter is
only as good as its samples, and an overconfident initial covariance is
more dangerous for it than for a Gaussian filter.

\section{The particle filter}
\label{sec:ch19-pf}

\subsection{Particles and weights}

Both filters so far describe the belief by a mean and a covariance. The
particle filter describes it by samples.

\begin{definition}[Weighted particle set]
\label{def:ch19-particles}
A \textbf{weighted particle set}\index{particle} of size $N$ is a collection
$\{(\state^{(i)}, w^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N$ of states $\state^{(i)} \in \R^n$, the
\textbf{particles}, and non-negative \textbf{weights}\index{importance weight}
$w^{(i)}$ with $\sum_i w^{(i)} = 1$. It represents the density
\begin{equation}
  p(\state) \approx \sum_{i=1}^N w^{(i)}\,\delta(\state - \state^{(i)}),
  \qquad\text{so that}\qquad
  \E[\phi(\state)] \approx \sum_{i=1}^N w^{(i)}\,\phi(\state^{(i)})
  \label{eq:ch19-particle-approx}
\end{equation}
for any function $\phi$; the mean is the case $\phi(\state) = \state$ and
the covariance the case $\phi(\state) = (\state - \hat{\state})
(\state - \hat{\state})\T$.
\end{definition}

The representation makes no assumption about the shape of $p$. A set
with half of its weight around one state and half around another
represents a belief with two modes, something no single Gaussian can do.

Where do the particles and weights come from? The idea is
\textbf{importance sampling}\index{importance sampling}: we want samples
from the posterior $p(\state_k \mid \meas_{1:k})$ but cannot draw them
directly; we \emph{can} draw from the motion model, so we draw each new
particle from $p(\state_k \mid \state_{k-1}^{(i)})$ and correct for the
difference between where we sampled and where we wanted to sample by a
weight, the ratio of the target density to the proposal density. Writing
out that ratio for the recursion \cref{eq:ch19-bayes-predict,eq:ch19-bayes-update}
with the motion model as the proposal, everything cancels except the
likelihood \cite{arulampalam2002tutorial}:
\begin{equation}
  \state_k^{(i)} \sim p(\state_k \mid \state_{k-1}^{(i)}), \qquad
  w_k^{(i)} \propto w_{k-1}^{(i)}\, p(\meas_k \mid \state_k^{(i)}).
  \label{eq:ch19-sis}
\end{equation}
This choice of proposal is the \textbf{bootstrap filter}\index{bootstrap filter}
of Gordon, Salmond and Smith~\cite{gordon1993novel}, also called
sequential importance resampling (SIR)\index{sequential importance resampling}.
It requires only what \cref{def:ch19-model} promised: the ability to
sample the motion model and to evaluate the likelihood. For the
range--bearing sensor the likelihood is the Gaussian density of the
measurement around $h(\state^{(i)})$ with the bearing residual wrapped,
\begin{equation}
  \log p(\meas_k \mid \state^{(i)}) = -\frac12\,\vect{y}^{(i)\mathsf{T}}\mat{R}\inv\vect{y}^{(i)} + \text{const},
  \qquad \vect{y}^{(i)} = \meas_k \ominus h(\state^{(i)}),
  \label{eq:ch19-loglik}
\end{equation}
and the constant cancels when the weights are normalised. Other likelihoods
are equally welcome: a map that says ``no intruder inside a building''
is the likelihood that is $0$ inside and $1$ outside, which
\cref{sec:ch19-pf-example} uses.

\subsection{Degeneracy, the effective sample size and resampling}
\label{sec:ch19-resampling}

Applied naively, \cref{eq:ch19-sis} fails within a few steps. Every
particle is a random guess of the whole trajectory, most guesses drift
away from the truth, their likelihoods shrink, and after normalisation a
single particle carries almost all the weight. The set then represents the
belief by one point, and the remaining $N - 1$ particles are wasted. This
is \textbf{weight degeneracy}\index{degeneracy (particle filter)}, and it is
unavoidable: the variance of the weights can only grow over time
\cite{doucet2001sequential}. The standard diagnostic is the effective
sample size.

\begin{definition}[Effective sample size]
\label{def:ch19-ess}
The \textbf{effective sample size}\index{effective sample size} of a
normalised weight vector is
\begin{equation}
  N_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^N \bigl(w^{(i)}\bigr)^2}.
  \label{eq:ch19-ess}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

$N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ lies between $1$ (one particle has all the weight) and
$N$ (all weights equal), see \cref{thm:ch19-ess}, and it is roughly the
number of equally weighted particles that would give an estimate of the
same variance. The cure for degeneracy is \textbf{resampling}
\index{resampling}: draw $N$ particles from the current set with
probabilities equal to the weights, so that heavy particles are copied
several times and light ones disappear, and reset all weights to $1/N$.
The new set represents the same belief with the same $N$ particles, now
all of them useful. Resampling is applied when $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ falls
below a threshold $N_{\mathrm{thr}}$, typically $N/2$; the original
bootstrap filter resampled at every step, which is simpler but wasteful
(\cref{sec:ch19-implementation}).

Resampling has a cost of its own. Copies of the same particle are
identical until the process noise of the next step separates them, and if
the noise is small, or if measurements are missing for a while, the set
collapses onto a few distinct states: \textbf{sample impoverishment}
\index{sample impoverishment}. Resampling only when needed limits it;
\cref{sec:ch19-variants} lists stronger remedies.

How the $N$ draws are made matters. Drawing them independently
(multinomial resampling) adds unnecessary randomness. \textbf{Systematic
resampling}\index{resampling!systematic} \cite{kitagawa1996monte,
arulampalam2002tutorial} draws a single uniform number $u \in [0, 1/N)$,
places the $N$ points $u, u + 1/N, \dots, u + (N-1)/N$ on the unit interval,
and selects particle $i$ once for every point that falls into its slot
$(c_{i-1}, c_i]$ of the cumulative weights. Each particle is then copied
either $\lfloor N w^{(i)} \rfloor$ or $\lceil N w^{(i)} \rceil$ times
(\cref{thm:ch19-systematic}), the expected count is exactly $N w^{(i)}$,
and the whole procedure is a single pass, \cref{alg:ch19-resample}.

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
\caption{Systematic resampling}
\label{alg:ch19-resample}
\KwIn{normalised weights $w^{(1)}, \dots, w^{(N)}$}
\KwOut{indices $\mathit{idx}[1..N]$ of the particles to keep (with repetitions)}
\Fn{\NlfSystematicResample{$w$}}{
  $u \gets$ a uniform random number in $[0, 1/N)$\label{alg:ch19-resample:u}\;
  $c \gets$ cumulative sums of $w$, so $c_i = \sum_{l \le i} w^{(l)}$ and $c_N = 1$\label{alg:ch19-resample:cumsum}\;
  $i \gets 1$\;
  \For{$j \gets 1$ \KwTo $N$}{
    $u_j \gets u + (j - 1)/N$\label{alg:ch19-resample:grid}\;
    \While{$c_i < u_j$}{$i \gets i + 1$\label{alg:ch19-resample:advance}\;}
    $\mathit{idx}[j] \gets i$\label{alg:ch19-resample:select}\;
  }
  \KwRet $\mathit{idx}$\;
}
\end{algorithm}

Line~\ref{alg:ch19-resample:u} draws the only random number.
Line~\ref{alg:ch19-resample:grid} generates the evenly spaced points in
increasing order, so the pointer $i$ on line~\ref{alg:ch19-resample:advance}
only ever moves forward, and the loop is $\bigO{N}$. A particle whose slot
is wider than $1/N$ is selected on line~\ref{alg:ch19-resample:select}
several times in a row; one whose slot contains no grid point is dropped.
Rounding can leave $c_N$ a hair below $1$; clamp the last cumulative sum
to $1$ so that the loop cannot run past the end.

\subsection{The bootstrap filter}
\label{sec:ch19-pf-step}

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
\caption{One step of the bootstrap particle filter}
\label{alg:ch19-pf}
\KwIn{particles $\state_{k-1}^{(i)}$ with log-weights $\ell^{(i)}$, $i = 1, \dots, N$; measurement $\meas_k$ or \KwNil; motion model $f$ with noise sampler; likelihood $p(\meas \mid \state)$; threshold $N_{\mathrm{thr}}$}
\KwOut{particles $\state_k^{(i)}$, log-weights $\ell^{(i)}$, estimate $\hat{\state}_k$, $\mat{P}_k$}
\Fn{\NlfPfStep{$\{\state_{k-1}^{(i)}, \ell^{(i)}\}$, $\meas_k$}}{
  \ForEach{$i$}{
    draw $\vect{w}^{(i)} \sim \Normal(\vect{0}, \mat{Q}(\state_{k-1}^{(i)}))$; $\state_k^{(i)} \gets f(\state_{k-1}^{(i)}) + \vect{w}^{(i)}$\tcp*{propagate}\label{alg:ch19-pf:propagate}
  }
  \If{$\meas_k \ne$ \KwNil}{
    $\ell^{(i)} \gets \ell^{(i)} + \log p(\meas_k \mid \state_k^{(i)})$ for all $i$\tcp*{weight}\label{alg:ch19-pf:weight}
  }
  $w^{(i)} \gets \exp\bigl(\ell^{(i)} - \max_j \ell^{(j)}\bigr)$; $w^{(i)} \gets w^{(i)} / \sum_j w^{(j)}$; $\ell^{(i)} \gets \log w^{(i)}$\tcp*{normalise}\label{alg:ch19-pf:normalise}
  $\hat{\state}_k \gets \sum_i w^{(i)}\state_k^{(i)}$ (circular mean for angular components); $\mat{P}_k \gets \sum_i w^{(i)}(\state_k^{(i)} \ominus \hat{\state}_k)(\state_k^{(i)} \ominus \hat{\state}_k)\T$\label{alg:ch19-pf:estimate}\;
  \If{$1 / \sum_i (w^{(i)})^2 < N_{\mathrm{thr}}$}{
    $\mathit{idx} \gets$ \NlfSystematicResample{$w$}; $\state_k^{(i)} \gets \state_k^{(\mathit{idx}[i])}$; $\ell^{(i)} \gets -\log N$ for all $i$\tcp*{resample}\label{alg:ch19-pf:resample}
  }
  \KwRet $(\{\state_k^{(i)}, \ell^{(i)}\}, \hat{\state}_k, \mat{P}_k)$\;
}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Walkthrough.}
Line~\ref{alg:ch19-pf:propagate} is the predict step: every particle
follows the true motion model with its own random noise, so the cloud
spreads exactly as the model says, curvature and all. Line~\ref{alg:ch19-pf:weight}
is the update: the log-likelihood of the measurement is added to each
particle's log-weight. A missing measurement skips it, and the cloud
simply keeps spreading. Line~\ref{alg:ch19-pf:normalise} normalises in the
log domain, subtracting the largest log-weight first so that the
exponentials cannot underflow; with $2\,000$ particles and a sharp
likelihood, raw likelihood values of $10^{-300}$ are routine and would
otherwise become zero. Line~\ref{alg:ch19-pf:estimate} extracts a point
estimate and covariance for consumers that need one; the heading is
averaged as an angle, via the weighted mean of $(\cos\psi, \sin\psi)$.
When the cloud is bimodal, the mean lies between the modes and is not a
plausible state; \cref{sec:ch19-drone} argues that the planner should then
be given the cloud, not the mean. Line~\ref{alg:ch19-pf:resample} resamples
only when the effective sample size has dropped below the threshold.
\index{particle filter!bootstrap}\index{log-sum-exp}

\subsection{A worked example: an intruder behind a building}
\label{sec:ch19-pf-example}

\begin{example}[A multimodal posterior]
\label{ex:ch19-occlusion}
An intruder flies north at $8\,\mathrm{m/s}$ towards a building that
occupies the block $[-10, 10] \times [50, 70]$ metres; our sensor at the
origin measures range and bearing every $\dt = 0.5\,\mathrm{s}$ with
$\sigma_r = 2\,\mathrm{m}$ and $\sigma_\varphi = 0.05\,\mathrm{rad}$, but it
loses the intruder inside the street canyon $35 \le y \le 75$, where no
measurement arrives. The map is known, so a state inside the building has
likelihood $0$. Unseen by the sensor, the intruder turns left at
$1\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ for one second when it is $13\,\mathrm{m}$ short of the
building, flies north-west for two seconds, turns back to north and passes
the building on the west side. A bootstrap filter with $N = 2\,000$
particles, $\sigma_a = 0.5\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$,
$\sigma_\gamma = 0.3\,\mathrm{rad/s^2}$ and $N_{\mathrm{thr}} = N/2$ tracks
it; \code{occlusion\_example()} in the chapter code runs the scenario
and \code{gen\_ch19\_particles.py} draws \cref{fig:ch19-particles}.
\end{example}

$N_{\mathrm{eff}} = 514$, below the threshold, and the filter resamples.
From $k = 8$ on no measurement arrives. Line~\ref{alg:ch19-pf:propagate} of
\cref{alg:ch19-pf} is all that runs: every particle follows its own
acceleration and turn-rate noise, and the cloud fans out northwards.
Between $k = 10$ and $k = 12$ the leading particles reach $y = 50$, the
south face of the building; those inside receive weight zero, so
$N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ falls from $1\,911$ to $861$ and the filter resamples,
copying the survivors on both sides of the building. At $k = 15$
($t = 7.5\,\mathrm{s}$, orange) the belief has two lobes: $68\,\%$ of the
particles are west of the building and $28\,\%$ east of it, the remaining
$4\,\%$ lag behind south of it. The filter does not know which lobe holds
the intruder, and it says so. A Gaussian filter cannot. The UKF run on the
same measurements, with predict-only steps in the canyon, has its mean at
$(-3.0, 61.4)$ at $k = 15$, inside the building (the purple square in the
figure), and keeps it inside from $k = 11$ to $k = 17$; a planner that
inflates that mean by its covariance would guard a region the intruder
cannot be in and leave the two streets uncovered.

$(-20.8, 75.1)$ and the first measurement arrives. Only particles of the
west lobe are compatible with it, and only a few dozen of those:
$N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ collapses to $17$, the filter resamples from these
survivors, and $99.9\,\%$ of the new particles lie within $15\,\mathrm{m}$
of the truth (green); the east lobe is gone. The collapse is sample
impoverishment in action: after this resampling the $2\,000$ particles are
copies of a few dozen ancestors, and the process noise needs several steps
to spread them again ($N_{\mathrm{eff}} = 432$ at $k = 21$, above $1\,000$
at $k = 22$). Over the whole run the filter resamples $13$ times in $29$
steps. \Cref{exr:ch19-occlusion} explores what roughening, a different
resampling rule and a right turn do to this picture.

\section{Properties, costs and a decision guide}
\label{sec:ch19-properties}

None of the three filters is optimal in the sense in which the Kalman
filter is optimal for linear-Gaussian models (\cref{ch:ch18}); each is an
approximation of the Bayes filter, and what can be proved is how good the
approximation is and what it costs.

\subsection{The extended Kalman filter}

\begin{proposition}[The EKF contains the Kalman filter]
\label{thm:ch19-ekf-linear}
If $f(\state) = \mat{F}\state + \vect{b}$ and $h(\state) = \mat{H}\state +
\vect{c}$ are affine, \cref{alg:ch19-ekf} computes exactly the Kalman
filter of \cref{ch:ch18}, and its output is the exact posterior mean and
covariance.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The Jacobians of affine maps are the constant matrices $\mat{F}$ and
$\mat{H}$, the linearisations \cref{eq:ch19-ekf-linearise-f,eq:ch19-ekf-linearise-h}
are equalities, and \cref{eq:ch19-ekf-predict,eq:ch19-ekf-innovation,eq:ch19-ekf-update}
are the Kalman equations, whose exactness for linear-Gaussian models is
the main theorem of \cref{ch:ch18}.
\end{proof}

For nonlinear models the EKF has no such guarantee. Its predicted mean
$f(\hat{\state}_{k-1})$ differs from the true mean of $f(\state_{k-1})$ by the
second-order Taylor term $\tfrac12\sum_{jk} P_{jk}\,\partial^2 f/\partial
x_j\partial x_k$ and higher, and its covariance ignores the same terms; the
error is small when the functions are nearly linear across the
$1\sigma$ to $3\sigma$ region of the belief and grows with curvature times
covariance. This is why the EKF works well on the mild example of
\cref{sec:ch19-ekf-example} and badly on the wide bearing belief of
\cref{fig:ch19-linearisation}. The EKF is not guaranteed to converge, and
a poor initialisation can make it diverge with a covariance that claims
confidence. Convergence results exist under bounds on the nonlinearity and
the initial error \cite{sarkka2013bayesian}, but in practice the
covariance must be validated by looking at the innovations, as in
\cref{ch:ch18}.

\subsection{The unscented transform}

\begin{proposition}[Properties of the sigma points]
\label{thm:ch19-ut}
For every choice of $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\kappa$ with $n + \lambda > 0$, the
sigma points and weights of \cref{def:ch19-ut} satisfy:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item $\sum_{i=0}^{2n} W_i^{(m)} = 1$;
  \item $\sum_{i} W_i^{(m)}\mathcal{X}_i = \bar{\state}$ and
        $\sum_{i} W_i^{(c)}(\mathcal{X}_i - \bar{\state})(\mathcal{X}_i - \bar{\state})\T = \mat{P}$;
  \item if $g(\state) = \mat{A}\state + \vect{b}$ is affine, the unscented
        transform returns $\bar{\vect{y}} = \mat{A}\bar{\state} + \vect{b}$,
        $\mat{P}_{yy} = \mat{A}\mat{P}\mat{A}\T$ and $\mat{P}_{xy} = \mat{P}\mat{A}\T$,
        the exact mean, covariance and cross-covariance; consequently the UKF
        reduces to the Kalman filter for linear models.
\end{enumerate}
\index{unscented transform!properties}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(i) $W_0^{(m)} + 2n \cdot \frac{1}{2(n+\lambda)} = \frac{\lambda}{n+\lambda} +
\frac{n}{n+\lambda} = 1$.
(ii) The points come in pairs $\bar{\state} \pm \vect{l}_i$ with equal
weights, so $\sum_i W_i^{(m)}\mathcal{X}_i = \bar{\state}\sum_i W_i^{(m)} +
\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{2(n+\lambda)}(\vect{l}_i - \vect{l}_i) = \bar{\state}$.
In the covariance sum the term $i = 0$ vanishes because
$\mathcal{X}_0 = \bar{\state}$, and each pair contributes
$2 \cdot \frac{1}{2(n+\lambda)}\vect{l}_i\vect{l}_i\T$, so the sum is
$\frac{1}{n+\lambda}\mat{L}\mat{L}\T = \mat{P}$.
(iii) $\mathcal{Y}_i = \mat{A}\mathcal{X}_i + \vect{b}$, hence by (i) and (ii)
$\bar{\vect{y}} = \mat{A}\sum_i W_i^{(m)}\mathcal{X}_i + \vect{b}\sum_i W_i^{(m)} =
\mat{A}\bar{\state} + \vect{b}$, and $\mathcal{Y}_i - \bar{\vect{y}} =
\mat{A}(\mathcal{X}_i - \bar{\state})$ gives $\mat{P}_{yy} = \mat{A}\bigl(\sum_i
W_i^{(c)}(\mathcal{X}_i - \bar{\state})(\mathcal{X}_i - \bar{\state})\T\bigr)\mat{A}\T
= \mat{A}\mat{P}\mat{A}\T$ and likewise $\mat{P}_{xy} = \mat{P}\mat{A}\T$. With
these values, lines~\ref{alg:ch19-ukf:predict}--\ref{alg:ch19-ukf:cov} of
\cref{alg:ch19-ukf} are the Kalman equations. The self-test of the chapter
code checks (i) and (iii) numerically.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Second-order accuracy of the unscented mean]
\label{thm:ch19-ut-second-order}
Let $g$ be smooth and let $\state$ have mean $\bar{\state}$ and covariance
$\mat{P}$. The unscented mean agrees with the Taylor expansion of
$\E[g(\state)]$ about $\bar{\state}$ up to and including the second-order
terms, for every choice of the parameters; the EKF mean $g(\bar{\state})$
agrees only up to the first order. For a Gaussian $\state$ and
$\beta = 2$, the unscented covariance additionally matches some of the
fourth-order terms of the true covariance.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Write $\state = \bar{\state} + \vect{d}$ and expand
$g(\bar{\state} + \vect{d}) = g(\bar{\state}) + \mat{G}\vect{d} +
\tfrac12\sum_{jk} d_j d_k\,\partial_{jk}g + \bigO{\norm{\vect{d}}^3}$ with
$\mat{G}$ the Jacobian. Taking expectations, $\E[\vect{d}] = 0$ kills the
linear term and $\E[d_j d_k] = P_{jk}$ gives $\E[g(\state)] =
g(\bar{\state}) + \tfrac12\sum_{jk} P_{jk}\,\partial_{jk}g + \dots$. For
the sigma points, $\vect{d}_i = \pm\vect{l}_i$: the linear terms cancel in
pairs by symmetry, and the quadratic terms give $\tfrac12\sum_i W_i^{(m)}
\sum_{jk} d_{i,j}d_{i,k}\,\partial_{jk}g = \tfrac12\sum_{jk}\partial_{jk}g
\cdot \frac{1}{n+\lambda}\sum_{i=1}^n l_{i,j}l_{i,k} = \tfrac12\sum_{jk}
P_{jk}\,\partial_{jk}g$, the same second-order term. The fourth-order
statement is the content of \textcite{julier2004unscented} and
\textcite{wan2000unscented}; the linearisation example of
\cref{fig:ch19-linearisation} shows the second-order term at work: the
true mean of $r\sin\varphi$ is $100\,e^{-\sigma_\varphi^2/2} = 95.6$, the
EKF gives $100$, the UT gives $95.6$.
\end{proof}

The UT is not exact beyond second order, and nothing prevents a badly
scaled set of sigma points from sampling the function where it does not
represent the belief: with a spread of $\sqrt{n+\lambda}$ standard
deviations in $n = 5$ dimensions the points sit $2.2\sigma$ out, and for a
bearing belief with $\sigma_\varphi = 1\,\mathrm{rad}$ they straddle more
than half the circle. Negative weights ($\lambda < 0$) can produce a
$\mat{P}_{yy}$ that is not positive definite. In both cases the fix is a
smaller $\alpha$ or a different $\kappa$.

\subsection{The particle filter}

\begin{proposition}[Bounds on the effective sample size]
\label{thm:ch19-ess}
For normalised weights, $1 \le N_{\mathrm{eff}} \le N$. The upper bound is
attained if and only if all weights equal $1/N$, the lower bound if and
only if one weight equals $1$. After resampling, $N_{\mathrm{eff}} = N$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality, $1 = \bigl(\sum_i w^{(i)}\bigr)^2 \le
N\sum_i (w^{(i)})^2$, with equality if and only if all $w^{(i)}$ are equal;
hence $\sum_i (w^{(i)})^2 \ge 1/N$ and $N_{\mathrm{eff}} \le N$. For the
lower bound, $\sum_i (w^{(i)})^2 \le \max_i w^{(i)} \sum_i w^{(i)} =
\max_i w^{(i)} \le 1$, with equality if and only if a single weight is $1$.
Resampling sets every weight to $1/N$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Systematic resampling is unbiased]
\label{thm:ch19-systematic}
Let $n_i$ be the number of copies of particle $i$ produced by
\cref{alg:ch19-resample}. Then $n_i \in \{\lfloor N w^{(i)}\rfloor,
\lfloor N w^{(i)}\rfloor + 1\}$ and $\E[n_i] = N w^{(i)}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The grid $u_j = u + (j-1)/N$ has spacing $1/N$, and $n_i$ is the number of
grid points in the slot $(c_{i-1}, c_i]$ of length $w^{(i)}$. An interval
of length $\ell$ contains either $\lfloor N\ell\rfloor$ or
$\lfloor N\ell\rfloor + 1$ points of a grid of spacing $1/N$, and, since
$u$ is uniform on $[0, 1/N)$, the second case has probability equal to the
fractional part of $N\ell$. Hence $\E[n_i] = \lfloor N w^{(i)}\rfloor +
\bigl(N w^{(i)} - \lfloor N w^{(i)}\rfloor\bigr) = N w^{(i)}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Convergence of the bootstrap filter]
\label{thm:ch19-pf-convergence}
Under mild conditions on the model (a bounded, continuous likelihood and
a motion model that can be sampled), for every bounded function $\phi$
the particle estimate $\sum_i w_k^{(i)}\phi(\state_k^{(i)})$ of
\cref{alg:ch19-pf} converges to $\E[\phi(\state_k) \mid \meas_{1:k}]$ as
$N \to \infty$, and the mean-square error is bounded by $c_k \norm{\phi}_\infty^2 / N$
for a constant $c_k$ that depends on $k$ and on the model but not on $N$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
By induction over $k$: at each step, propagation, weighting and
resampling are each a Monte Carlo operation whose error is $\bigO{1/\sqrt{N}}$
in mean square for bounded test functions, and the errors add up over the
three operations and over time. Crisan and Doucet~\cite{crisan2002survey}
give the full argument and the conditions under which $c_k$ stays bounded
in $k$; see also \textcite{doucet2001sequential}. The constant grows with
the dimension of the state, which is the practical limit of the method.
\end{proof}

Unlike the EKF and the UKF, the particle filter is therefore consistent:
with enough particles it computes the Bayes filter for any model, Gaussian
or not, unimodal or not. The catch is in ``enough''. The number of
particles needed for a given accuracy grows exponentially with the state
dimension, because the likelihood concentrates in a region whose volume
fraction shrinks with every dimension; five or six dimensions are
comfortable with a few thousand particles, a dozen dimensions are not.

\subsection{Costs and a decision guide}
\label{sec:ch19-decision}

\Cref{tab:ch19-properties} summarises the three filters. Per step, the EKF
evaluates $f$, $h$ and two Jacobians once and performs $\bigO{n^3 + m^3}$
matrix arithmetic; the UKF performs two Cholesky factorisations and
$2n+1$ evaluations of $f$ and of $h$, again $\bigO{n^3}$; the particle
filter evaluates $f$ and the likelihood $N$ times and resamples in
$\bigO{N}$, so its cost is $\bigO{N(n + m)}$, linear in $N$ and, with
NumPy, dominated by memory traffic. In the experiment of
\cref{sec:ch19-ukf-compare} one step of the EKF takes about
$0.3$\,ms, one UKF step $0.5$\,ms and one step of the particle
filter with $N = 2\,000$ about $1.3$\,ms in pure \python with NumPy;
all three are far below the $50$ to $100\,\mathrm{ms}$ of a sensor cycle,
and the ratio between them is what matters when a drone tracks a dozen
intruders on an embedded processor.

\section{Variants and extensions}
\label{sec:ch19-variants}

\paragraph{Relatives of the EKF.}
The \textbf{iterated EKF}\index{iterated EKF} repeats the update a few times,
re-linearising $h$ at the updated estimate instead of the prediction; it
helps when the measurement is very informative and strongly nonlinear, as
for a bearing at short range. The second-order EKF adds the Hessian terms
that \cref{thm:ch19-ut-second-order} identifies, at the price of computing
them; the UKF gets the same benefit for free. Error-state formulations,
standard in inertial navigation, run the EKF on the small error between a
nominal trajectory and the truth, which keeps the linearisation accurate.

\paragraph{Relatives of the UKF.}
The \textbf{square-root UKF} propagates the Cholesky factor of $\mat{P}$
directly and never forms a covariance that could lose positive
definiteness. The \textbf{cubature Kalman filter}\index{cubature Kalman filter}
of Arasaratnam and Haykin~\cite{arasaratnam2009cubature} uses $2n$ points at
$\sqrt{n}$ standard deviations with equal weights $1/(2n)$, which is the UT
with $\alpha = 1$, $\beta = 0$, $\kappa = 0$ without the centre point; it
has no parameters to tune and no negative weights. The augmented UKF
appends the noise variables to the state so that non-additive process
noise, such as $\mat{G}(\state)\vect{\eta}_k$ in \cref{eq:ch19-ct-noise},
passes through the sigma points instead of being added afterwards.
All of these belong to the family of sigma-point filters that
\textcite{sarkka2013bayesian} treats uniformly, together with the
smoothers that estimate past states from later measurements.

\paragraph{Relatives of the particle filter.}
The \textbf{regularised particle filter} adds a small random jitter
(``roughening'') to the copies produced by resampling, which counters
impoverishment; the auxiliary particle filter looks one measurement ahead
before it resamples; and the \textbf{Rao--Blackwellised particle filter}
\index{Rao--Blackwellised particle filter} samples only the nonlinear part of
the state and runs a Kalman filter per particle for the linear part, which
is how simultaneous localisation and mapping (SLAM) systems keep thousands
of landmarks tractable. Better proposal distributions, informed by the
current measurement, reduce the number of particles needed;
\textcite{arulampalam2002tutorial} and \textcite{doucet2001sequential}
survey them. For an intruder whose behaviour switches between a few
regimes (straight, turning left, turning right), the \textbf{interacting
multiple model}\index{interacting multiple model} (IMM) filter of Blom and
Bar-Shalom~\cite{blom1988imm} runs one EKF or UKF per regime and mixes
them according to transition probabilities; it captures the ``left or
right'' hypothesis with two Gaussians instead of two thousand particles, as
long as the hypotheses do not interact with a map. Finally, all of these
filters have three-dimensional versions: add an altitude and a vertical
speed to the state and an elevation angle to the sensor, and the
Jacobians and sigma points grow accordingly (\cref{exr:ch19-coding}).

\section{Implementation notes}
\label{sec:ch19-implementation}

The chapter code, \code{code/ch19\_nonlinear\_filters.py}, contains the
coordinated-turn model with its Jacobian and noise, the range--bearing
sensor with its Jacobian and log-likelihood, the classes
\code{ExtendedKalmanFilter}, \code{UnscentedKalmanFilter} and
\code{ParticleFilter}, systematic resampling, a simulator for the
turning target and the two worked examples.
\Cref{lst:ch19-ekf,lst:ch19-ut,lst:ch19-pf} are excerpts.

\paragraph{Numerical issues with the covariance.}
\index{Cholesky factorisation!failure}
Every filter here keeps a covariance that must stay symmetric and positive
definite. Floating-point arithmetic erodes both properties: the product
$\mat{F}\mat{P}\mat{F}\T$ is symmetric only up to rounding, and the update
$(\mat{I} - \Kgain\mat{H})\mat{P}^-$ subtracts two nearly equal matrices. For the
EKF the Joseph form of line~\ref{alg:ch19-ekf:joseph} avoids the
subtraction; for the UKF, whose covariance update
$\mat{P}^- - \Kgain\mat{S}\Kgain\T$ has no Joseph form, the code symmetrises,
$\mat{P} \gets \tfrac12(\mat{P} + \mat{P}\T)$, after every step. When
\code{numpy.linalg.cholesky} nevertheless raises \code{LinAlgError}, the
usual reasons are a state component that is perfectly known (a zero
variance, common when a filter is initialised from exact values), a
$\lambda < 0$ that produced a negative weight, or a genuinely diverged
filter. Adding a small jitter $\epsilon\mat{I}$ with $\epsilon \approx
10^{-9}$ before the factorisation cures the first; the other two must be
fixed at the source. \Cref{lst:ch19-ut} shows the jitter and the
symmetrisation. A related detail hides in the motion model: for
$\abs{\omega} < 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ the code evaluates $f$ and
$\mat{F}$ by their series in $\omega$ to second order rather than by
\cref{eq:ch19-ct,eq:ch19-ct-jacobian}, whose divisions by $\omega$ and
$\omega^2$ would otherwise turn rounding noise into metres; the self-test
checks that both branches agree at the switch.

\paragraph{Angles.}
\index{angle wrapping!implementation}
The bearing innovation, the heading component of every state difference,
the mean of the sigma points and the covariance residuals are all wrapped
with \code{wrap\_angle}, which implements \cref{eq:ch19-wrap} for scalars
and arrays alike. The circular mean of the particles' headings is computed
from the weighted sums of $\cos\psi$ and $\sin\psi$. If the heading is
represented by $(\cos\psi, \sin\psi)$ instead of $\psi$, most of these
special cases disappear at the cost of a six-dimensional state and a
constraint that the filter does not know about; the wrapped angle is the
lesser evil.

\paragraph{Particles in NumPy.}
\index{vectorisation}
The particle filter is a loop over particles in the pseudocode and a set of
array operations in the code: the particles are an $N \times n$ array, the
motion model and the measurement model accept such arrays and return
$N \times n$ and $N \times m$ arrays, and the log-likelihood is a single
vectorised expression. A \python loop over $2\,000$ particles would be a
hundred times slower. Log-weights are kept throughout and normalised with
the log-sum-exp trick of line~\ref{alg:ch19-pf:normalise}; systematic
resampling is \code{numpy.searchsorted} of the grid points in the
cumulative weights, which is the pointer walk of \cref{alg:ch19-resample}
in one call. The number of particles is a budget: the error of the
estimate falls like $1/\sqrt{N}$ (\cref{thm:ch19-pf-convergence}), and for
the five-dimensional intruder $1\,000$ to $5\,000$ particles are a
reasonable range. Measure the effect of $N$ on your own scenario
(\cref{exr:ch19-coding}) rather than trusting a rule of thumb.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={The EKF step (from \code{code/ch19\_nonlinear\_filters.py}). The Jacobians are evaluated at the previous estimate (predict) and at the prediction (update); the innovation and the heading are wrapped; the covariance uses the Joseph form.},label={lst:ch19-ekf}]
def predict(self):
    """Linearise f at the previous estimate, then move mean and covariance."""
    F = self.motion.jacobian(self.x)              # Jacobian at x_{k-1}
    Q = self.motion.Q(self.x)
    self.x = self.motion.f(self.x)                 # true f for the mean
    self.P = F @ self.P @ F.T + Q
    return self.x, self.P

def update(self, z):
    """Linearise h at the prediction and apply the Kalman update."""
    H = self.sensor.jacobian(self.x)              # Jacobian at x_k^-
    z_pred = self.sensor.h(self.x)                 # true h for the prediction
    y = self.sensor.residual(z, z_pred)            # innovation, bearing wrapped
    S = H @ self.P @ H.T + self.sensor.R
    K = np.linalg.solve(S.T, (self.P @ H.T).T).T   # K = P H^T S^{-1}
    self.x = self.x + K @ y
    for j in self.motion.angle_index:
        self.x[j] = wrap_angle(self.x[j])
    IKH = np.eye(len(self.x)) - K @ H
    self.P = IKH @ self.P @ IKH.T + K @ self.sensor.R @ K.T   # Joseph form
    self.last = dict(H=H, z_pred=z_pred, y=y, S=S, K=K)
    return self.x, self.P
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Sigma points and the unscented transform (from \code{code/ch19\_nonlinear\_filters.py}). The angular components are averaged through wrapped residuals relative to the central point.},label={lst:ch19-ut}]
def sigma_points(mean, cov, alpha=1.0, beta=2.0, kappa=0.0, jitter=1e-9):
    """The 2n+1 sigma points (rows) and the weight vectors W_m, W_c."""
    mean = np.asarray(mean, dtype=float)
    n = mean.size
    lam = alpha ** 2 * (n + kappa) - n
    P = 0.5 * (cov + cov.T) + jitter * np.eye(n)     # symmetrise, guard the factorisation
    L = np.linalg.cholesky((n + lam) * P)            # columns l_i: L L^T = (n+lam) P
    X = np.empty((2 * n + 1, n))
    X[0] = mean
    X[1:n + 1] = mean + L.T                          # row i of L.T is column i of L
    X[n + 1:] = mean - L.T
    W_m = np.full(2 * n + 1, 1.0 / (2.0 * (n + lam)))
    W_c = W_m.copy()
    W_m[0] = lam / (n + lam)
    W_c[0] = lam / (n + lam) + 1.0 - alpha ** 2 + beta
    return X, W_m, W_c


def weighted_mean(Y, W_m, angle_index=()):
    """Weighted mean of the rows of Y; angular columns via wrapped residuals."""
    mean = W_m @ Y
    for j in angle_index:
        mean[j] = wrap_angle(Y[0, j] + W_m @ wrap_angle(Y[:, j] - Y[0, j]))
    return mean


def residuals(Y, mean, angle_index=()):
    """Rows of Y minus mean, wrapped in the angular columns."""
    E = Y - mean
    for j in angle_index:
        E[:, j] = wrap_angle(E[:, j])
    return E


def unscented_transform(X, W_m, W_c, g, x_mean=None, angle_in=(), angle_out=()):
    """Push sigma points through g; return mean, covariance and cross-covariance."""
    Y = g(X)                                         # g must accept an (2n+1, n) array
    y_mean = weighted_mean(Y, W_m, angle_out)
    E = residuals(Y, y_mean, angle_out)
    if x_mean is None:
        x_mean = weighted_mean(X, W_m, angle_in)
    D = residuals(X, x_mean, angle_in)
    P_yy = (E.T * W_c) @ E                           # sum_i W_c[i] e_i e_i^T
    P_xy = (D.T * W_c) @ E
    return y_mean, P_yy, P_xy
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Systematic resampling and the bootstrap step of the particle filter (from \code{code/ch19\_nonlinear\_filters.py}), vectorised over the $N \times 5$ particle array with log-weights.},label={lst:ch19-pf}]
def systematic_resample(weights, rng):
    """Indices of the particles to keep: one uniform draw, N evenly spaced points."""
    n = len(weights)
    positions = (rng.random() + np.arange(n)) / n
    cumulative = np.cumsum(weights)
    cumulative[-1] = 1.0                             # guard against rounding
    return np.searchsorted(cumulative, positions)

def predict(self):
    """Propagate every particle through f with its own noise sample."""
    self.X = self.motion.f(self.X) + self.motion.sample_noise(self.X, self.rng)
    self.X[:, 3] = wrap_angle(self.X[:, 3])
    return self.X

def update(self, z=None):
    """Weight by the likelihood (if any), normalise, estimate, resample if needed."""
    if z is not None:
        self.logw += self.sensor.log_likelihood(z, self.X)
    if self.constraint is not None:
        self.logw[~self.constraint(self.X)] = -np.inf
    top = np.max(self.logw)
    if not np.isfinite(top):                     # every particle died: restart flat
        self.logw[:] = -math.log(self.n)
        top = self.logw[0]
    w = np.exp(self.logw - top)                  # log-sum-exp normalisation
    w /= np.sum(w)
    with np.errstate(divide="ignore"):
        self.logw = np.log(w)
    self.n_eff = effective_sample_size(w)
    self.x, self.P = self.estimate(w)
    if self.n_eff < self.threshold:
        idx = systematic_resample(w, self.rng)
        self.X = self.X[idx]
        if self.roughening is not None:              # optional jitter of the copies
            self.X = self.X + self.rng.normal(size=self.X.shape) * self.roughening
        self.logw = np.full(self.n, -math.log(self.n))
        self.resample_count += 1
    return self.x, self.P
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{pitfall}[Jacobians evaluated at the wrong point]
The EKF linearises $f$ at the \emph{previous} estimate
$\hat{\state}_{k-1}$ and $h$ at the \emph{predicted} estimate
$\hat{\state}_k^-$. Evaluating $\mat{H}_k$ at $\hat{\state}_{k-1}$, which is a
natural slip when both Jacobians are computed at the top of the step, uses
the geometry of where the intruder \emph{was}; at $12\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and
$50\,\mathrm{m}$ range that is a $7^\circ$ error in the direction of the
bearing row of $\mat{H}$, and the filter's covariance quietly becomes wrong.
The same slip in the other direction, computing $\mat{F}_k$ at the
prediction, is a smaller error but still an error. A finite-difference
check of both Jacobians at random states, as in the self-test, catches
sign and index mistakes in the formulas of \cref{sec:ch19-ekf-jacobians};
it does not catch the wrong evaluation point, which only a test against
a simulated track reveals.
\end{pitfall}

\begin{pitfall}[Forgetting to wrap angles]
An unwrapped bearing innovation near the $\pm\pi$ seam is about $2\pi$
instead of nearly zero. In \cref{ex:ch19-ekf} the EKF would multiply
$6.21\,\mathrm{rad}$ by the gain and move the estimate by tens of metres in
a single step; the innovation of the next step is then huge as well, the
filter chases its own error, and the track is lost. The bug is invisible as
long as the intruder stays away from the seam, which is why it survives
testing: place the sensor so that the seam crosses the test trajectory, and
wrap every angle difference, not only the innovation. The particle filter
has the same bug in the likelihood, where it quietly assigns weight zero to
every particle on the far side of the seam.
\end{pitfall}

\begin{pitfall}[Resampling at every step]
Resampling replaces a set of distinct particles by copies, and the copies
stay identical until the process noise separates them. Resampling when
$N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is still close to $N$ throws away diversity for nothing;
over a long gap without measurements, as in \cref{ex:ch19-occlusion}, a
filter that resamples every step ends with a cloud made of a handful of
distinct ancestors and can no longer represent the two lobes. Resample
when $N_{\mathrm{eff}} < N/2$, add roughening if the process noise is
small, and monitor the number of distinct particles.
\end{pitfall}

\begin{pitfall}[Too few particles in six dimensions]
A particle filter that works with $500$ particles on a planar
coordinated turn will not work with $500$ particles in three dimensions
with a seven-dimensional state. The likelihood of a precise sensor
concentrates in a region whose volume fraction shrinks with every
dimension, so most particles receive negligible weight, $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$
collapses after every measurement, and the estimate jumps between a few
survivors. The symptoms are an $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ that is small after every
update and an error that does not improve with more measurements. Increase
$N$ until the error curve flattens, or move the linear part of the state
into a Kalman filter per particle (\cref{sec:ch19-variants}).
\end{pitfall}

\section{Where this fits in the drone system}
\label{sec:ch19-drone}
\begin{dronebox}
\index{particle filter!in the drone system}\index{prediction layer!nonlinear filters}%
In the hybrid architecture of \cref{ch:ch24} the filters of this chapter are
the prediction layer's trackers for \emph{manoeuvring} intruders, taking
over from the Kalman filter of \cref{ch:ch18} whenever the intruder turns or
the sensor reports range and bearing. They receive the raw measurements of
the on-board sensor, possibly with gaps, and deliver at every cycle an
estimate of position, speed, heading and turn rate with its covariance.
That output feeds three consumers: the local avoidance layer
(\cref{ch:ch13,ch:ch14}), which needs the intruder's velocity now; the
trajectory predictor of \cref{ch:ch20}, which extrapolates the estimate
over the safety horizon, with the coordinated-turn model as a stronger
baseline than constant velocity; and the prediction-aware constraints of
\cref{ch:ch24}, which inflate the intruder's footprint by its uncertainty.
The particle filter adds something the Gaussian filters cannot: when the
belief is multimodal, the cloud itself is the right thing to hand over.
Propagating every particle forward over the horizon yields, at each
future time, an empirical distribution of where the intruder may be, from
which the planner can compute the probability that a candidate path
collides, or block the cells that more than a chosen fraction of the
particles visit, without ever pretending that the intruder is in one
place. In the study plan (\cref{ch:appA}) this chapter completes Week~9.
\end{dronebox}

\section{Summary}
\begin{summary}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Turning intruders (the coordinated-turn model with turn rate
  $\omega$ in the state) and range--bearing sensors make the motion and
  measurement models nonlinear, and the linear Kalman filter no longer
  applies. All three filters of this chapter approximate the same Bayes
  filter: predict through $f$, update with the likelihood of $\meas_k$.
  \item The EKF linearises $f$ at the previous estimate and $h$ at the
  prediction with the Jacobians $\mat{F}_k$, $\mat{H}_k$ and runs the Kalman
  equations. It is exact for linear models, cheap, and biased when the
  functions curve across the belief; a poor start with weak measurements
  can make it diverge.
  \item The unscented transform represents a Gaussian by $2n+1$ sigma points
  $\bar{\state} \pm \vect{l}_i$ from the Cholesky factor of $(n+\lambda)\mat{P}$
  with weights $W^{(m)}$, $W^{(c)}$; it reproduces mean and covariance
  exactly for linear functions and the mean to second order in general.
  The UKF replaces both linearisations by unscented transforms and needs no
  Jacobians.
  \item The bootstrap particle filter propagates $N$ samples through the
  motion model with sampled noise, weights them by the measurement
  likelihood, normalises in the log domain and resamples systematically
  when $N_{\mathrm{eff}} = 1/\sum_i (w^{(i)})^2$ drops below $N/2$. It
  represents multimodal beliefs and arbitrary likelihoods, including maps,
  at a cost linear in $N$, with $N$ growing quickly with the state dimension.
  \item Wrap every angle difference; evaluate Jacobians at the right point;
  keep covariances symmetric and, if necessary, jittered; vectorise
  particles. Use the EKF for mild nonlinearity, the UKF for range--bearing
  tracking without Jacobians, the particle filter for occlusions, maps and
  multiple hypotheses, and hand the planner the particle cloud when the
  belief is not a single blob.
\end{itemize}
\end{summary}

\paragraph{Further reading.}
\textcite{thrun2005probabilistic} present the EKF, the UKF and the particle
filter as three instances of the Bayes filter and are the reference for
the derivations of this chapter; \textcite{sarkka2013bayesian} gives the
same material with proofs, the sigma-point family and the corresponding
smoothers. The unscented transform is due to
\textcite{julier1997new}, with the definitive account in
\textcite{julier2004unscented}; \textcite{wan2000unscented} introduced
the parameters $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\kappa$ used here. The bootstrap filter
was introduced by \textcite{gordon1993novel} and, independently, by
\textcite{kitagawa1996monte}; \textcite{arulampalam2002tutorial} is the
standard tutorial, \textcite{doucet2001sequential} the standard collection,
and \textcite{crisan2002survey} the survey of convergence results. The
coordinated-turn model and the tracking practice around it are treated
by \textcite{barshalom2001estimation}, who also cover the interacting
multiple-model filter of \textcite{blom1988imm}. The cubature Kalman
filter is from \textcite{arasaratnam2009cubature}.
