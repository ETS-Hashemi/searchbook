\section{Why this matters for a drone swarm}
\label{sec:ch19-motivation}

The Kalman filter of \cref{ch:ch18} gives the prediction layer of the hybrid
architecture (\cref{ch:ch24}) a stable estimate of an intruder's state and a
covariance that says how much to trust it. It rests on two assumptions: the
intruder moves according to a \emph{linear} model, and the sensor reports a
\emph{linear} function of the state. Both fail in the situations that matter
most. An unknown quadrotor that enters a survey site flies a straight leg,
banks into a turn around a crane and straightens out; a constant-velocity
model lags in every turn, and the natural model for a banked drone, the
\textbf{coordinated-turn model}\index{coordinated-turn model} with speed,
heading and turn rate in the state, contains the sine and cosine of the
heading. The sensor our drone carries, a radar, a lidar or a camera with a
rangefinder, reports a range and a bearing, the square root and the
arctangent of the position. The Kalman filter has no place for either.

This chapter presents the three standard answers in increasing order of
generality and cost. The \textbf{extended Kalman filter}\index{extended Kalman filter}
(EKF) linearises the models around the current estimate and runs the Kalman
equations on the linearisation. The \textbf{unscented Kalman filter}
\index{unscented Kalman filter} (UKF) avoids derivatives: it pushes a handful
of chosen points through the true functions and fits a Gaussian to what comes
out. The \textbf{particle filter}\index{particle filter} (PF) drops the
Gaussian assumption and represents the belief by thousands of weighted
samples, which lets it keep ``went left around the building'' and ``went
right'' alive at once. All three live in the prediction layer of
\cref{ch:ch24}, turning raw measurements into a state estimate with its
uncertainty for the avoidance layer (\cref{ch:ch13,ch:ch14}), the trajectory
predictor (\cref{ch:ch20}) and the planner's prediction-aware constraints;
in the study plan (\cref{ch:appA}) they complete Week~9.
\Cref{sec:ch19-intuition,sec:ch19-problem} give the idea and the
models, \cref{sec:ch19-ekf,sec:ch19-ukf,sec:ch19-pf} develop the three
filters and compare them on a turning target, and the remaining sections
cover properties, variants, implementation and the place in the drone system.

\section{The idea in plain words}
\label{sec:ch19-intuition}

Every filter step pushes a Gaussian belief through a function and describes
the result by a mean and a covariance. For an affine function the result
is Gaussian (\cref{ch:ch02}) and the Kalman filter is exact. As soon as the function bends, the output is
no longer Gaussian: push a Gaussian in range and bearing through the
polar-to-Cartesian map and you get a \emph{banana}, a curved region that no
ellipse describes well (\cref{fig:ch19-idea}). The three filters are three
approximations of that banana. The EKF replaces the bent function by its
tangent at the current estimate: one Jacobian per step, and an error that
grows with the curvature of the function within the width of the belief. The
UKF keeps the true function and changes the representation of the input:
$2n+1$ \emph{sigma points} whose mean and covariance are exactly those of the
belief are mapped through the function, and mean and covariance are read off
the images; no derivative is needed, and the mean is correct up to the
second-order Taylor term, precisely the term the EKF drops. The particle
filter represents the belief by many weighted random samples, moves each
through the true function with its own noise and re-weights it by how well
it explains the measurement; the belief can be curved, skewed or split in
two, and the price is the number of samples.\index{linearisation
error}\index{banana distribution}

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \inputfigure{ch19/idea}
  \caption{Three ways of pushing a Gaussian belief (left) through a
  nonlinear function $f$ into the curved true output distribution (shaded
  banana). (a) The EKF moves the ellipse with the tangent at the mean; the
  ellipse sits on the image of the mean, not on the mean of the banana, and
  cannot bend. (b) The UKF pushes $2n+1$ sigma points through the true $f$
  and fits an ellipse to the images; the fitted mean lies inside the banana.
  (c) The particle filter pushes a cloud of random samples through $f$ and
  keeps the cloud as the belief, curvature included.}
  \label{fig:ch19-idea}
\end{figure}

\begin{keyidea}
A nonlinear filter has to push a belief through a bent function. The EKF
bends the belief with the tangent (one Jacobian), the UKF pushes $2n+1$
representative points through the real function (no Jacobian, second-order
accurate mean), and the particle filter pushes thousands of random samples
through it (no Gaussian at all, but $\bigO{N}$ work and the need to
resample). Choose the cheapest one whose assumptions your intruder and your
sensor satisfy.
\end{keyidea}

\section{Problem statement and notation}
\label{sec:ch19-problem}

\begin{definition}[Nonlinear state-space model]
\label{def:ch19-model}
A \textbf{nonlinear state-space model}\index{state-space model!nonlinear} has
a state $\state_k \in \R^n$ at times $k = 0, 1, \dots$ spaced by $\dt$,
measurements $\meas_k \in \R^m$, a motion model $f$ and a measurement model
$h$ with
\begin{equation}
  \state_k = f(\state_{k-1}) + \vect{w}_k, \qquad
  \meas_k = h(\state_k) + \vect{v}_k,
  \label{eq:ch19-model}
\end{equation}
where $\vect{w}_k \sim \Normal(\vect{0}, \mat{Q}_k)$ and $\vect{v}_k \sim
\Normal(\vect{0}, \mat{R}_k)$ are independent of each other and over time, and
$\state_0 \sim \Normal(\hat{\state}_0, \mat{P}_0)$. The \textbf{filtering
problem}\index{filtering problem} is to compute, at every $k$, the posterior
$p(\state_k \mid \meas_{1:k})$ or at least its mean $\hat{\state}_k$ and
covariance $\mat{P}_k$.
\end{definition}

For $f(\state) = \mat{F}\state$ and $h(\state) = \mat{H}\state$ this is the
linear-Gaussian model of \cref{ch:ch18}, whose notation we keep:
$\hat{\state}_k^-$, $\mat{P}_k^-$ are the \emph{predicted} mean and covariance
before $\meas_k$ is used, $\hat{\state}_k$, $\mat{P}_k$ the \emph{updated}
ones, $\vect{y}_k$ the innovation, $\mat{S}_k$ its covariance and $\Kgain_k$
the gain. All three filters approximate the \textbf{Bayes filter}
\index{Bayes filter} \cite{thrun2005probabilistic},
\begin{equation}
  p(\state_k \mid \meas_{1:k-1}) = \!\int\! p(\state_k \mid \state_{k-1})\,
    p(\state_{k-1} \mid \meas_{1:k-1})\, \mathrm{d}\state_{k-1},
  \qquad
  p(\state_k \mid \meas_{1:k}) \propto p(\meas_k \mid \state_k)\,
    p(\state_k \mid \meas_{1:k-1}),
  \label{eq:ch19-bayes}
\end{equation}
which pushes yesterday's posterior through the motion model and then applies
Bayes' rule (\cref{ch:ch02}) with the likelihood $p(\meas_k \mid \state_k) =
\Normal(\meas_k; h(\state_k), \mat{R}_k)$; for the models below neither
step has a closed form. We write $\partial g/\partial\state$ for the \textbf{Jacobian}\index{Jacobian}
of $g$, the matrix of partial derivatives $\partial g_i/\partial x_j$, which
gives the best affine approximation $g(\state + \vect{d}) \approx g(\state) +
\frac{\partial g}{\partial\state}(\state)\,\vect{d}$.

\subsection{The coordinated-turn model}
\label{sec:ch19-ct}

A drone flying at constant speed with a constant bank angle follows a
circular arc. The \textbf{coordinated-turn model}
\index{coordinated-turn model!definition} has the state
(\cref{fig:ch19-models})
\begin{equation}
  \state = (x, y, v, \psi, \omega)\T,
  \label{eq:ch19-ct-state}
\end{equation}
with position $(x, y)$, speed $v \ge 0$, heading $\psi$ (counter-clockwise
from the $x$ axis) and turn rate $\omega = \dot{\psi}$; $\omega > 0$ turns
left, $\omega = 0$ is straight flight. Integrating $\dot{x} = v\cos\psi$,
$\dot{y} = v\sin\psi$, $\dot{\psi} = \omega$ over one step gives the exact
discrete model
\begin{equation}
  f(\state) =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    x + \dfrac{v}{\omega}\bigl(\sin(\psi + \omega\dt) - \sin\psi\bigr)\\[1.2ex]
    y - \dfrac{v}{\omega}\bigl(\cos(\psi + \omega\dt) - \cos\psi\bigr)\\[1.2ex]
    v\\
    \psi + \omega\dt\\
    \omega
  \end{pmatrix}
  \ (\omega \ne 0), \qquad
  f(\state) =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    x + v\dt\cos\psi\\
    y + v\dt\sin\psi\\
    v\\
    \psi\\
    0
  \end{pmatrix}
  \ (\omega = 0),
  \label{eq:ch19-ct}
\end{equation}
an arc of radius $v/\abs{\omega}$; the second form is the limit of the first,
used below a small threshold on $\abs{\omega}$. The model is nonlinear
through $v\sin\psi$, $v\cos\psi$ and the division by $\omega$ (Bar-Shalom,
Li and Kirubarajan~\cite{barshalom2001estimation} write it with Cartesian
velocities; the polar form has the more compact Jacobian and delivers the
speed and heading the avoidance layer wants). Real intruders change speed
and turn rate. We model a random longitudinal acceleration $a_k \sim
\Normal(0, \sigma_a^2)$ and a random turn acceleration $\gamma_k \sim
\Normal(0, \sigma_\gamma^2)$, constant during the step, so that
\begin{equation}
  \vect{w}_k = \mat{G}(\state)\begin{pmatrix} a_k\\ \gamma_k\end{pmatrix},
  \quad
  \mat{G}(\state) =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \tfrac12\dt^2\cos\psi & 0\\
    \tfrac12\dt^2\sin\psi & 0\\
    \dt & 0\\
    0 & \tfrac12\dt^2\\
    0 & \dt
  \end{pmatrix},
  \quad
  \mat{Q}(\state) = \mat{G}(\state)
  \begin{pmatrix}\sigma_a^2 & 0\\ 0 & \sigma_\gamma^2\end{pmatrix}
  \mat{G}(\state)\T,
  \label{eq:ch19-ct-noise}
\end{equation}
the white-noise-acceleration construction of \cref{ch:ch18} applied along
the heading and to the turn rate. $\mat{Q}$ depends on $\psi$, so the filters
evaluate it at the current estimate; $\sigma_a$ and $\sigma_\gamma$ say how
abruptly the intruder may manoeuvre.\index{process noise!coordinated turn}

  \centering
  \inputfigure{ch19/models}
  \caption{The two models of the chapter. The intruder at $(x, y)$ flies at
  speed $v$ along the heading $\psi$ and turns left at the rate $\omega > 0$
  on a circle of radius $v/\abs{\omega}$; the sensor at $\vect{s}$ measures
  the range $r$ and the bearing $\varphi$ of the intruder, not its position.}

\subsection{The range--bearing sensor and angles}
\label{sec:ch19-rb}

\begin{definition}[Range--bearing measurement]
\label{def:ch19-rb}
A sensor at the known position $\vect{s} = (s_x, s_y)$ measures the
\textbf{range}\index{range--bearing measurement} $r$ and the \textbf{bearing}
$\varphi \in (-\pi, \pi]$ of the intruder,
\begin{equation}
  h(\state) =
  \begin{pmatrix} r \\ \varphi \end{pmatrix} =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \sqrt{(x - s_x)^2 + (y - s_y)^2}\\
    \operatorname{atan2}(y - s_y,\, x - s_x)
  \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad
  \vect{v}_k \sim \Normal\!\bigl(\vect{0}, \diag(\sigma_r^2, \sigma_\varphi^2)\bigr).
  \label{eq:ch19-rb}
\end{equation}
$h$ depends only on the position; the filter learns $v$, $\psi$ and $\omega$
from how the position changes over time.
\end{definition}

A bearing error of $\sigma_\varphi$ radians at range $r$ displaces the
position by about $r\sigma_\varphi$ across the line of sight; the error
region is a thin arc, the banana of \cref{fig:ch19-idea}. Headings and
bearings live on a circle: the
difference between the bearings $3.10$ and $-3.10$ is not $6.20$ but
$-0.083$. We write
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{wrap}(\theta) = \theta - 2\pi\Bigl\lceil \frac{\theta - \pi}{2\pi} \Bigr\rceil
  \in (-\pi, \pi]
  \label{eq:ch19-wrap}
\end{equation}
for the representative of $\theta$ on the circle; every difference of two
angles in this chapter, in an innovation, a residual or a likelihood, is
$\operatorname{wrap}(\theta_1 - \theta_2)$, and $\meas \ominus \hat{\meas}$
denotes a componentwise difference whose angular components are wrapped.
The heading of an estimate is wrapped after every update.\index{angle wrapping}

\section{The extended Kalman filter}
\label{sec:ch19-ekf}

\subsection{Linearise, then run the Kalman filter}

The EKF replaces $f$ by its tangent at the current estimate and $h$ by its
tangent at the prediction,
\begin{equation}
  f(\state) \approx f(\hat{\state}_{k-1}) + \mat{F}_k(\state - \hat{\state}_{k-1}),
  \quad
  h(\state) \approx h(\hat{\state}_k^-) + \mat{H}_k(\state - \hat{\state}_k^-),
  \quad
  \mat{F}_k = \frac{\partial f}{\partial \state}(\hat{\state}_{k-1}),\
  \mat{H}_k = \frac{\partial h}{\partial \state}(\hat{\state}_k^-),
  \label{eq:ch19-ekf-linearise}
\end{equation}
and applies the Kalman equations of \cref{ch:ch18} to the tangents, with the
true $f$ and $h$ for the means:
\begin{align}
  \hat{\state}_k^- &= f(\hat{\state}_{k-1}),
  & \mat{P}_k^- &= \mat{F}_k \mat{P}_{k-1} \mat{F}_k\T + \mat{Q}_k,
  \label{eq:ch19-ekf-predict}\\
  \vect{y}_k &= \meas_k \ominus h(\hat{\state}_k^-),
  & \mat{S}_k &= \mat{H}_k \mat{P}_k^- \mat{H}_k\T + \mat{R}_k,
  \label{eq:ch19-ekf-innovation}\\
  \Kgain_k &= \mat{P}_k^- \mat{H}_k\T \mat{S}_k\inv,
  & \hat{\state}_k &= \hat{\state}_k^- + \Kgain_k \vect{y}_k,
  \qquad
  \mat{P}_k = (\mat{I} - \Kgain_k \mat{H}_k)\,\mat{P}_k^-.
  \label{eq:ch19-ekf-update}
\end{align}
Only the covariances and the gain use the linearisation. The Jacobians
depend on the estimate, hence on the data: the covariance cannot be computed
in advance, and a wrong estimate produces a wrong gain
(\cref{sec:ch19-ekf-failure}).\index{extended Kalman filter!equations}

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
\caption{One step of the extended Kalman filter}
\label{alg:ch19-ekf}
\KwIn{previous estimate $\hat{\state}_{k-1}$, $\mat{P}_{k-1}$; measurement $\meas_k$ or \KwNil; models $f$, $h$ with Jacobians; $\mat{Q}_k$, $\mat{R}_k$}
\KwOut{updated estimate $\hat{\state}_k$, $\mat{P}_k$}
\Fn{\NlfEkfStep{$\hat{\state}_{k-1}$, $\mat{P}_{k-1}$, $\meas_k$}}{
  $\mat{F}_k \gets \partial f / \partial\state$ at $\hat{\state}_{k-1}$; $\hat{\state}_k^- \gets f(\hat{\state}_{k-1})$, heading wrapped; $\mat{P}_k^- \gets \mat{F}_k \mat{P}_{k-1} \mat{F}_k\T + \mat{Q}_k$\label{alg:ch19-ekf:predict}\;
  \If{$\meas_k = $ \KwNil}{\KwRet $(\hat{\state}_k^-, \mat{P}_k^-)$\tcp*{missing measurement: predict only}\label{alg:ch19-ekf:missing}}
  $\mat{H}_k \gets \partial h / \partial\state$ at $\hat{\state}_k^-$\label{alg:ch19-ekf:H}\;
  $\vect{y}_k \gets \meas_k - h(\hat{\state}_k^-)$; $\vect{y}_k[j] \gets$ \NlfWrap{$\vect{y}_k[j]$} for every angular component $j$\label{alg:ch19-ekf:innovation}\;
  $\mat{S}_k \gets \mat{H}_k \mat{P}_k^- \mat{H}_k\T + \mat{R}_k$; $\Kgain_k \gets \mat{P}_k^- \mat{H}_k\T \mat{S}_k\inv$\label{alg:ch19-ekf:gain}\;
  $\hat{\state}_k \gets \hat{\state}_k^- + \Kgain_k \vect{y}_k$, heading wrapped\label{alg:ch19-ekf:update}\;
  $\mat{P}_k \gets (\mat{I} - \Kgain_k \mat{H}_k)\,\mat{P}_k^- (\mat{I} - \Kgain_k \mat{H}_k)\T + \Kgain_k \mat{R}_k \Kgain_k\T$\tcp*{Joseph form}\label{alg:ch19-ekf:joseph}
  \KwRet $(\hat{\state}_k, \mat{P}_k)$\;
}
\end{algorithm}

Line~\ref{alg:ch19-ekf:predict} of \cref{alg:ch19-ekf} is the predict step,
with $\mat{F}_k$ at the \emph{previous} estimate; line~\ref{alg:ch19-ekf:missing}
handles a step without measurement, as when an obstacle hides the intruder,
by taking the prediction as the estimate. The update linearises $h$ at the
\emph{predicted} estimate (line~\ref{alg:ch19-ekf:H}); the innovation on
line~\ref{alg:ch19-ekf:innovation} has its bearing wrapped
(\cref{sec:ch19-ekf-example}). Line~\ref{alg:ch19-ekf:joseph} uses the
Joseph form of the covariance update, algebraically equal to $(\mat{I} -
\Kgain_k\mat{H}_k)\mat{P}_k^-$ but symmetric and positive definite under
rounding.

\subsection{The Jacobians of the two models}
\label{sec:ch19-ekf-jacobians}

\begin{proposition}[Jacobian of the coordinated-turn model]
\label{thm:ch19-ct-jacobian}
Let $\psi' = \psi + \omega\dt$. For $\omega \ne 0$ the Jacobian of
\cref{eq:ch19-ct} is
\begin{equation}
  \mat{F} =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    1 & 0 & \dfrac{\sin\psi' - \sin\psi}{\omega}
      & \dfrac{v}{\omega}\bigl(\cos\psi' - \cos\psi\bigr)
      & \dfrac{v\dt\cos\psi'}{\omega} - \dfrac{v\,(\sin\psi' - \sin\psi)}{\omega^2}\\[1.6ex]
    0 & 1 & -\dfrac{\cos\psi' - \cos\psi}{\omega}
      & \dfrac{v}{\omega}\bigl(\sin\psi' - \sin\psi\bigr)
      & \dfrac{v\dt\sin\psi'}{\omega} + \dfrac{v\,(\cos\psi' - \cos\psi)}{\omega^2}\\[1.6ex]
    0 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0\\
    0 & 0 & 0 & 1 & \dt\\
    0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 1
  \end{pmatrix},
  \label{eq:ch19-ct-jacobian}
\end{equation}
and for $\omega = 0$ the first two rows become
$F_{13} = \dt\cos\psi$, $F_{14} = -v\dt\sin\psi$,
$F_{15} = -\tfrac12 v\dt^2\sin\psi$, $F_{23} = \dt\sin\psi$,
$F_{24} = v\dt\cos\psi$, $F_{25} = \tfrac12 v\dt^2\cos\psi$.
\index{Jacobian!coordinated turn}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Rows three to five are the derivatives of $v$, $\psi + \omega\dt$ and
$\omega$. In the first row, $v$ enters linearly with the factor
$(\sin\psi' - \sin\psi)/\omega$; differentiating with respect to $\psi$ uses
$\partial\psi'/\partial\psi = 1$, and with respect to $\omega$ the quotient
rule on $1/\omega$ together with $\partial\psi'/\partial\omega = \dt$; the
second row is the same computation for $-\frac{v}{\omega}(\cos\psi' -
\cos\psi)$. The $\omega = 0$ entries are limits: $\sin\psi' = \sin\psi +
\omega\dt\cos\psi - \tfrac12\omega^2\dt^2\sin\psi + \bigO{\omega^3}$ gives
$x' = x + v\dt\cos\psi - \tfrac12 v\omega\dt^2\sin\psi + \bigO{\omega^2}$,
whose $\omega$-derivative at $0$ is $-\tfrac12 v\dt^2\sin\psi$.
\Cref{exr:ch19-ct-jacobian} completes the limits; the self-test of the
chapter code checks every entry against finite differences.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Jacobian of the range--bearing model]
\label{thm:ch19-rb-jacobian}
With $d_x = x - s_x$, $d_y = y - s_y$ and $r = \sqrt{d_x^2 + d_y^2} > 0$,
\begin{equation}
  \mat{H} = \frac{\partial h}{\partial \state} =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    d_x/r & d_y/r & 0 & 0 & 0\\[0.6ex]
    -d_y/r^2 & d_x/r^2 & 0 & 0 & 0
  \end{pmatrix}.
  \label{eq:ch19-rb-jacobian}
\end{equation}
\index{Jacobian!range--bearing}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The chain rule on the square root gives the first row; for the bearing,
$\varphi = \operatorname{atan2}(d_y, d_x)$ has $\partial\varphi/\partial x =
-d_y/(d_x^2 + d_y^2)$ and $\partial\varphi/\partial y = d_x/(d_x^2 + d_y^2)$
wherever $r > 0$, on every branch of the arctangent. The last three columns
vanish because $h$ ignores $v$, $\psi$, $\omega$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A worked example: one EKF step by hand}
\label{sec:ch19-ekf-example}

\begin{example}[One EKF step with a range--bearing measurement]
\label{ex:ch19-ekf}
Our drone hovers at $\vect{s} = (0, 0)$ and observes an intruder once per
second ($\dt = 1\,\mathrm{s}$). After step $k-1$,
\[
  \hat{\state}_{k-1} = (-100,\ -4,\ 10,\ \pi,\ 0.3)\T, \qquad
  \mat{P}_{k-1} = \diag(4,\ 4,\ 1,\ 0.04,\ 0.01),
\]
in metres, metres per second, radians and radians per second: the intruder
is $100\,\mathrm{m}$ to the west, flies west at $10\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and turns
left at $0.3\,\mathrm{rad/s}$, so its path curves towards the south. The
process noise has $\sigma_a = 1\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ and $\sigma_\gamma =
0.1\,\mathrm{rad/s^2}$, the sensor $\sigma_r = 2\,\mathrm{m}$ and
$\sigma_\varphi = 0.03\,\mathrm{rad}$, and the new measurement is $\meas_k =
(111.5\,\mathrm{m},\ 3.12\,\mathrm{rad})$. The instance is
\code{worked\_example()} in \code{ch19\_nonlinear\_filters.py}; every number
below is printed by its self-test.
\end{example}

\paragraph{Predict.}
With $v/\omega = 33.33$ and $\psi' = \pi + 0.3$, \cref{eq:ch19-ct} gives
$x^- = -100 + 33.33\sin\psi' = -109.85$ and $y^- = -4 - 33.33\,(\cos\psi' + 1)
= -5.49$: $9.85\,\mathrm{m}$ west and $1.49\,\mathrm{m}$ south. The heading
$\pi + 0.3$ wraps to $-2.8416$; speed and turn rate are unchanged. The
Jacobian \cref{eq:ch19-ct-jacobian} at $\hat{\state}_{k-1}$ has $F_{13} =
-0.985$, $F_{14} = 1.489$, $F_{15} = 0.991$, $F_{23} = -0.149$, $F_{24} =
-9.851$ and $F_{25} = -4.888$. The predicted covariance $\mat{F}_k\mat{P}_{k-1}\mat{F}_k\T +
\mat{Q}_k$ has the position block $\begin{psmallmatrix} 5.319 & -0.488\\
-0.488 & 8.143\end{psmallmatrix}$ (standard deviations $2.31$ and
$2.85\,\mathrm{m}$, larger across the westward flight) and now correlates
position with heading ($-0.443$ between $y$ and $\psi$) and speed, which the
update will use to correct both from a position measurement.

\paragraph{Update.}
The predicted measurement is $h(\hat{\state}_k^-) = (109.99, -3.0917)$: the
intruder is expected just south of the negative $x$ axis, so its bearing is
just above $-\pi$, while the measured bearing $3.12$ is just below $+\pi$.
Subtracting naively gives $6.2117$, an innovation of $356^\circ$ that would
send the estimate to the other side of the sensor; wrapping gives
$-0.0715\,\mathrm{rad}$, so $\vect{y}_k = (1.51,\ -0.0715)$: $1.5\,\mathrm{m}$
further away and $4.1^\circ$ clockwise of the prediction. With

\subsection{Where the EKF fails}
\label{sec:ch19-ekf-failure}

\paragraph{Strong nonlinearity.}
\Cref{fig:ch19-linearisation} makes the linearisation error visible. A
Gaussian belief in range and bearing, $r \sim \Normal(100, 5^2)$ and
$\varphi \sim \Normal(\pi/2, 0.3^2)$, is pushed through $(r, \varphi) \mapsto
(r\cos\varphi, r\sin\varphi)$, as when a track is initialised from one
measurement. The true output, estimated from
$2\,000$ samples, has its mean at $(-0.2, 95.6)$; the exact mean is
$100\,e^{-0.3^2/2} = 95.6$, because the samples lie on an arc of radius $100$
and the average of points on an arc is \emph{inside} the arc. The linearised
map puts the mean at the image of the input mean, $(0, 100)$,
$4.4\,\mathrm{m}$ too far out, and its ellipse is a straight bar that cannot
follow the curved tails: its standard deviation along $y$ is $5.0\,\mathrm{m}$
where the samples spread by $7.7\,\mathrm{m}$, and its $2\sigma$ ellipse
contains $74.6\,\%$ of the samples instead of the nominal $86.5\,\%$. Nothing
in the EKF signals the error: it reports a confident covariance around a
biased mean (\cref{thm:ch19-ekf-order} quantifies the bias). The
unscented transform of \cref{sec:ch19-ukf} puts the mean at $(0.0, 95.6)$
with five function evaluations and no derivative, and its ellipse contains
$91.6\,\%$ of the samples.

\paragraph{A poor initial estimate.}
If the estimate is far from the truth, $\mat{F}_k$ and $\mat{H}_k$ describe
the wrong tangent, the gain is computed for a fictitious geometry, and an
update can push the estimate further away while the covariance keeps
shrinking: the filter has
\textbf{diverged}\index{divergence (EKF)}. A range--bearing sensor measures
the position directly, and in the experiment of \cref{sec:ch19-ukf-compare}
the EKF recovers from a $90^\circ$ heading error in every run; divergence is
the real danger with weak or bearing-only measurements, long gaps, and an
initial covariance small enough to make the filter ignore its innovations.
The cures are an honest $\mat{P}_0$, a filter that does not depend on a
tangent, or a particle filter that keeps several hypotheses alive.

\section{The unscented Kalman filter}
\label{sec:ch19-ukf}

\subsection{The unscented transform}
\label{sec:ch19-ut}

Julier and Uhlmann~\cite{julier1997new,julier2004unscented} start from one
observation: it is easier to approximate a probability distribution than an
arbitrary nonlinear function. Instead of replacing $f$ by its tangent, keep
$f$ and replace the Gaussian by a small set of points that carry its mean and
covariance exactly, push the \emph{points} through the true $f$, and read
mean and covariance off the images.

\begin{definition}[Sigma points and weights]
\label{def:ch19-sigma}
Let $\bar{\state} \in \R^n$ and $\mat{P}$ be a mean and a covariance, let
$\alpha$, $\beta$, $\kappa$ be parameters and $\lambda = \alpha^2(n + \kappa)
- n$. Let $\mat{L}$ be a \textbf{matrix square root}\index{matrix square root}
of $(n + \lambda)\mat{P}$, that is $\mat{L}\mat{L}\T = (n + \lambda)\mat{P}$,
with columns $\vect{l}_1, \dots, \vect{l}_n$. The $2n + 1$ \textbf{sigma
points}\index{sigma points} and their weights are
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \mathcal{X}_0 &= \bar{\state}, &
    W_0^{(m)} &= \frac{\lambda}{n + \lambda}, &
    W_0^{(c)} &= \frac{\lambda}{n + \lambda} + 1 - \alpha^2 + \beta,\\
    \mathcal{X}_i &= \bar{\state} + \vect{l}_i, \quad
    \mathcal{X}_{n+i} = \bar{\state} - \vect{l}_i, &
    W_i^{(m)} &= W_i^{(c)} = \frac{1}{2(n + \lambda)}, &&
    i = 1, \dots, n,
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:ch19-sigma}
\end{equation}
where $W^{(m)}$ weights the mean and $W^{(c)}$ the covariance; the weights
$W^{(m)}$ sum to one.
\end{definition}

The points sit on the principal axes of the ellipse, $\sqrt{n + \lambda} =
\alpha\sqrt{n + \kappa}$ standard deviations from the mean. $\alpha$ sets
that spread, $\kappa$ is a secondary scaling, and $\beta$ enters only
$W_0^{(c)}$ to inject knowledge of the shape of the distribution, $\beta = 2$
being optimal for a Gaussian~\cite{wan2000unscented}. The standard choices
are $\alpha \in [10^{-3}, 1]$, $\beta = 2$ and $\kappa = 0$ or $\kappa = 3 -
n$. With $\alpha = 1$ and $\kappa = 0$ (the chapter code), $\lambda = 0$,
$W_0^{(m)} = 0$ and the points sit at $\sqrt{n}$ standard deviations;
$\kappa = 3 - n$ puts them at $\sqrt{3}$, which matches the fourth moment of
a Gaussian~\cite{julier1997new}, at the price of a negative $W_0^{(m)}$ when
$n > 3$. A small $\alpha$ keeps the points close to the mean, but makes
$W_0^{(m)}$ a large negative number and the recombination a difference of
large terms (\cref{exr:ch19-weights}).\index{unscented transform!parameters}
The square root is computed by the \textbf{Cholesky
factorisation}\index{Cholesky factorisation} $(n + \lambda)\mat{P} =
\mat{L}\mat{L}\T$, $\mat{L}$ lower triangular, in $\bigO{n^3}$ operations;
it exists when $\mat{P}$ is symmetric and positive definite. Any other
square root gives a different set of points with the same mean and
covariance and a slightly different result.

\begin{definition}[Unscented transform]
\label{def:ch19-ut}
For a function $g$, the \textbf{unscented transform}\index{unscented
transform} (UT) of $\Normal(\bar{\state}, \mat{P})$ maps every sigma point,
$\mathcal{Y}_i = g(\mathcal{X}_i)$, and returns
\begin{equation}
  \bar{\vect{y}} = \sum_{i=0}^{2n} W_i^{(m)} \mathcal{Y}_i, \quad
  \mat{P}_{yy} = \sum_{i=0}^{2n} W_i^{(c)}
    (\mathcal{Y}_i \ominus \bar{\vect{y}})(\mathcal{Y}_i \ominus \bar{\vect{y}})\T, \quad
  \mat{P}_{xy} = \sum_{i=0}^{2n} W_i^{(c)}
    (\mathcal{X}_i \ominus \bar{\state})(\mathcal{Y}_i \ominus \bar{\vect{y}})\T,
  \label{eq:ch19-ut}
\end{equation}
the approximate mean, covariance and \textbf{cross-covariance}
\index{cross-covariance} of $\state$ and $g(\state)$.
\end{definition}

Angular components are handled as in \cref{sec:ch19-rb}: the differences in
\cref{eq:ch19-ut} are wrapped, and the mean of an angular component is
$\mathcal{Y}_{0,j} + \sum_i W_i^{(m)} \operatorname{wrap}(\mathcal{Y}_{i,j} -
\mathcal{Y}_{0,j})$, wrapped again, so that images on both sides of the
$\pm\pi$ seam average correctly. \Cref{fig:ch19-linearisation}(b) shows the
transform at work: five evaluations of the polar-to-Cartesian map, and the
mean lands inside the banana; \cref{thm:ch19-ut} proves why.

\subsection{The filter}

The UKF replaces the two linearisations of the EKF by two unscented
transforms and the product $\mat{P}_k^-\mat{H}_k\T$ by the cross-covariance
$\mat{C}_k = \mat{P}_{xz}$ between state and predicted measurement (equal
to $\mat{P}_k^-\mat{H}_k\T$ in the linear case), so that $\Kgain_k =
\mat{C}_k\mat{S}_k\inv$ and $\mat{P}_k = \mat{P}_k^- -
\Kgain_k\mat{S}_k\Kgain_k\T$ need no $\mat{H}_k$ at all. \Cref{alg:ch19-ukf}
gives the version for additive noise, the one that fits \cref{eq:ch19-model}.

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
\caption{One step of the unscented Kalman filter (additive noise)}
\label{alg:ch19-ukf}
\KwIn{previous estimate $\hat{\state}_{k-1}$, $\mat{P}_{k-1}$; measurement $\meas_k$ or \KwNil; models $f$, $h$; $\mat{Q}_k$, $\mat{R}_k$; parameters $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\kappa$}
\KwOut{updated estimate $\hat{\state}_k$, $\mat{P}_k$}
\Fn{\NlfSigmaPoints{$\bar{\state}$, $\mat{P}$}}{
  $\lambda \gets \alpha^2(n + \kappa) - n$; $\mat{L} \gets \operatorname{chol}\bigl((n + \lambda)\mat{P}\bigr)$ with columns $\vect{l}_1, \dots, \vect{l}_n$\label{alg:ch19-ukf:chol}\;
  $\mathcal{X}_0 \gets \bar{\state}$; $\mathcal{X}_i \gets \bar{\state} + \vect{l}_i$ and $\mathcal{X}_{n+i} \gets \bar{\state} - \vect{l}_i$ for $i = 1, \dots, n$\label{alg:ch19-ukf:points}\;
  $W_0^{(m)} \gets \lambda/(n + \lambda)$; $W_0^{(c)} \gets W_0^{(m)} + 1 - \alpha^2 + \beta$; $W_i^{(m)} = W_i^{(c)} \gets 1/(2(n + \lambda))$ for $i \ge 1$\label{alg:ch19-ukf:weights}\;
  \KwRet $(\mathcal{X}, W^{(m)}, W^{(c)})$\;
}
\Fn{\NlfUnscentedTransform{$\mathcal{X}$, $W^{(m)}$, $W^{(c)}$, $g$}}{
  $\mathcal{Y}_i \gets g(\mathcal{X}_i)$ for $i = 0, \dots, 2n$\label{alg:ch19-ukf:push}\;
  $\bar{\vect{y}} \gets \sum_i W_i^{(m)}\mathcal{Y}_i$; $\mat{P}_{yy} \gets \sum_i W_i^{(c)}(\mathcal{Y}_i \ominus \bar{\vect{y}})(\mathcal{Y}_i \ominus \bar{\vect{y}})\T$; $\mat{P}_{xy} \gets \sum_i W_i^{(c)}(\mathcal{X}_i \ominus \mathcal{X}_0)(\mathcal{Y}_i \ominus \bar{\vect{y}})\T$\label{alg:ch19-ukf:recombine}\;
  \KwRet $(\bar{\vect{y}}, \mat{P}_{yy}, \mat{P}_{xy})$\;
}
\Fn{\NlfUkfStep{$\hat{\state}_{k-1}$, $\mat{P}_{k-1}$, $\meas_k$}}{
  $(\mathcal{X}, W^{(m)}, W^{(c)}) \gets$ \NlfSigmaPoints{$\hat{\state}_{k-1}$, $\mat{P}_{k-1}$}\;
  $(\hat{\state}_k^-, \mat{P}_{xx}, \cdot) \gets$ \NlfUnscentedTransform{$\mathcal{X}$, $W^{(m)}$, $W^{(c)}$, $f$}; $\mat{P}_k^- \gets \mat{P}_{xx} + \mat{Q}_k$\label{alg:ch19-ukf:predict}\;
  \If{$\meas_k = $ \KwNil}{\KwRet $(\hat{\state}_k^-, \mat{P}_k^-)$\;}
  $(\mathcal{X}, W^{(m)}, W^{(c)}) \gets$ \NlfSigmaPoints{$\hat{\state}_k^-$, $\mat{P}_k^-$}\tcp*{redraw around the prediction}\label{alg:ch19-ukf:redraw}
  $(\hat{\meas}_k, \mat{P}_{zz}, \mat{C}_k) \gets$ \NlfUnscentedTransform{$\mathcal{X}$, $W^{(m)}$, $W^{(c)}$, $h$}; $\mat{S}_k \gets \mat{P}_{zz} + \mat{R}_k$\label{alg:ch19-ukf:measure}\;
  $\Kgain_k \gets \mat{C}_k\mat{S}_k\inv$; $\hat{\state}_k \gets \hat{\state}_k^- + \Kgain_k(\meas_k \ominus \hat{\meas}_k)$, heading wrapped; $\mat{P}_k \gets \mat{P}_k^- - \Kgain_k\mat{S}_k\Kgain_k\T$\label{alg:ch19-ukf:update}\;
  \KwRet $(\hat{\state}_k, \mat{P}_k)$\;
}
\end{algorithm}

Lines~\ref{alg:ch19-ukf:chol}--\ref{alg:ch19-ukf:weights} build the points
of \cref{def:ch19-sigma}; line~\ref{alg:ch19-ukf:push} is the only place
where the model is called, so the UKF works with any $f$ and $h$ you can
evaluate, a learned model included. The prediction
(line~\ref{alg:ch19-ukf:predict}) adds $\mat{Q}_k$ after the transform,
because additive noise does not pass through $f$. The update redraws the
sigma points around the prediction (line~\ref{alg:ch19-ukf:redraw}):
reusing the propagated points would ignore $\mat{Q}_k$, which for a
manoeuvring intruder is the larger part of $\mat{P}_k^-$.
Line~\ref{alg:ch19-ukf:measure} produces the predicted measurement, its
covariance and the cross-covariance in one call, and
line~\ref{alg:ch19-ukf:update} is the Kalman update with $\mat{C}_k$ in place
of $\mat{P}_k^-\mat{H}_k\T$.\index{unscented Kalman filter!algorithm}

\subsection{Worked example: the same step with sigma points}
\label{sec:ch19-ukf-example}

Run \cref{alg:ch19-ukf} on \cref{ex:ch19-ekf}. With $n = 5$, $\alpha = 1$,
$\kappa = 0$ we get $\lambda = 0$, $W_0^{(m)} = 0$, $W_0^{(c)} = 2$ and
$W_i^{(m)} = W_i^{(c)} = 0.1$ for $i \ge 1$. Since $\mat{P}_{k-1}$ is
diagonal, each sigma point differs from the mean in one component by
$\sqrt{5} \approx 2.236$ standard deviations ($4.472\,\mathrm{m}$ in $x$ or
$y$, $2.236\,\mathrm{m/s}$ in $v$, $0.447\,\mathrm{rad}$ in $\psi$,
$0.224\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ in $\omega$). The centre image is the EKF
prediction, $(-109.851, -5.489)$. The two heading points map to
$(-108.238, -9.602)$ and $(-109.526, -1.082)$: they straddle the arc, and
their average $x$ is $-108.882$, almost a metre east of the centre image,
because the arc bends and the chord lies inside it. The weighted mean of
all eleven images, $\hat{\state}_k^- = (-109.641, -5.456, 10, -2.8416,
0.3)$, is $0.21\,\mathrm{m}$ east of the EKF prediction: the second-order
term $\tfrac12 P_{\psi\psi}\,\partial^2 x'/\partial\psi^2 = \tfrac12 \cdot
0.04 \cdot 9.85 = 0.197\,\mathrm{m}$, plus a little from the $\omega$
points. The covariance also changes shape slightly ($\sigma_x = 2.355$
against $2.306$, $\sigma_y = 2.810$ against $2.854$). The update redraws
eleven points around $\hat{\state}_k^-$, maps them through $h$ and obtains
the predicted measurement $(109.81, -3.0918)$, the innovation $(1.69,
-0.0713)$ after wrapping, and the gain from the cross-covariance. The result
(\cref{tab:ch19-ekf-trace}) lies within $0.11\,\mathrm{m}$ of the EKF's: on
this step the two filters agree because the heading uncertainty
($0.2\,\mathrm{rad}$) is small compared with the curvature of the arc. The
next section shows what happens when the belief is wide compared with the
bend.

\subsection{EKF, UKF and particle filter on a turning target}
\label{sec:ch19-ukf-compare}

\Cref{fig:ch19-compare} and \cref{tab:ch19-rmse} compare the three filters
on a target that starts at $(120, -100)$ heading north at $12\,\mathrm{m/s}$
and flies $4\,\mathrm{s}$ straight, $7\,\mathrm{s}$ at $\omega =

-0.4\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ and $8\,\mathrm{s}$ straight, sampled at $\dt =
0.5\,\mathrm{s}$. The sensor at the origin has $\sigma_r = 3\,\mathrm{m}$

\section{The particle filter}
\label{sec:ch19-pf}

\subsection{Particles, weights and the bootstrap filter}
\label{sec:ch19-pf-idea}

When the intruder disappears behind a building and may come out on either
side, no single ellipse describes where it is: the belief has two lobes,
and their mean is a point inside the building where the intruder cannot
be. The particle filter drops the Gaussian.

\begin{definition}[Particle set]
\label{def:ch19-particles}
A \textbf{particle set}\index{particle filter!particle set} is a collection
$\{(\state^{(i)}, w^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N$ of states, the \textbf{particles},
with non-negative weights summing to one. It represents the distribution
$\sum_i w^{(i)}\,\delta(\state - \state^{(i)})$, so that $\E[g(\state)]
\approx \sum_i w^{(i)} g(\state^{(i)})$ for any $g$; the estimate is the
weighted mean $\hat{\state} = \sum_i w^{(i)}\state^{(i)}$ and the weighted
covariance.
\end{definition}

The weights come from \textbf{importance sampling}\index{importance
sampling}: if we cannot draw from the posterior $p$ but can draw from a
proposal $q$, then samples $\state^{(i)} \sim q$ with weights $w^{(i)}
\propto p(\state^{(i)})/q(\state^{(i)})$ represent $p$. Done sequentially
with the motion model as the proposal, this is the \textbf{bootstrap
filter}\index{particle filter!bootstrap} of Gordon, Salmond and
Smith~\cite{gordon1993novel}, also called \textbf{sequential importance
resampling}\index{sequential importance resampling} (SIR)
\cite{arulampalam2002tutorial}: each particle is moved through $f$ with its
own draw of the process noise, which samples $p(\state_k \mid
\state_{k-1}^{(i)})$, and its weight is multiplied by the likelihood of the
new measurement,
\begin{equation}
  \state_k^{(i)} = f(\state_{k-1}^{(i)}) + \vect{w}_k^{(i)},\quad
  \vect{w}_k^{(i)} \sim \Normal(\vect{0}, \mat{Q}_k), \qquad
  w_k^{(i)} \propto w_{k-1}^{(i)}\, p(\meas_k \mid \state_k^{(i)}),
  \label{eq:ch19-sir}
\end{equation}
followed by normalisation. This is the Bayes filter \cref{eq:ch19-bayes}
with the integral replaced by a sample and the multiplication by the
likelihood applied to each sample. For our models the noise sample is
$\mat{G}(\state^{(i)})(a^{(i)}, \gamma^{(i)})\T$ from \cref{eq:ch19-ct-noise}
and the log-likelihood is
\begin{equation}
  \log p(\meas_k \mid \state) = -\frac{(r_k - \hat{r})^2}{2\sigma_r^2}
  - \frac{\operatorname{wrap}(\varphi_k - \hat{\varphi})^2}{2\sigma_\varphi^2}
  + \text{const}, \qquad (\hat{r}, \hat{\varphi}) = h(\state),
  \label{eq:ch19-loglik}
\end{equation}
with the bearing difference wrapped. A step without a measurement skips the
weighting, and any other knowledge that can be written as a likelihood joins
in the same way: a map, for instance, gives weight zero to a particle inside
a building.

\subsection{Degeneracy, effective sample size and resampling}
\label{sec:ch19-resampling}

Multiplying weights step after step makes their variance grow until one
particle carries almost all the weight and the others contribute nothing,
a state called
\textbf{degeneracy}\index{degeneracy}\index{particle filter!degeneracy}. The
standard diagnostic is the \textbf{effective sample size}\index{effective
sample size}
\begin{equation}
  N_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^N (w^{(i)})^2} \in [1, N],
  \label{eq:ch19-ess}
\end{equation}
which equals $N$ when all weights are equal and $1$ when one particle has
all the weight (\cref{exr:ch19-ess}). When $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ falls below a
threshold $N_{\mathrm{thr}}$, typically $N/2$, the filter
\textbf{resamples}\index{resampling}: it draws $N$ particles from the
current set with probabilities $w^{(i)}$, with replacement, and gives the
copies equal weights $1/N$: heavy particles are duplicated, light ones
disappear. Drawing $N$ times independently (multinomial resampling)
costs $\bigO{N\log N}$ and adds unnecessary randomness;
\textbf{systematic resampling}\index{systematic resampling}
\cite{kitagawa1996monte,arulampalam2002tutorial}, \cref{alg:ch19-resample},
uses one uniform draw and a grid of $N$ evenly spaced points walked along
the cumulative weights, in $\bigO{N}$ time and with the smallest variance
of the standard schemes.

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
\caption{Systematic resampling}
\label{alg:ch19-resample}
\KwIn{normalised weights $w^{(1)}, \dots, w^{(N)}$}
\KwOut{indices $j_1, \dots, j_N$ of the particles to copy}
\Fn{\NlfSystematicResample{$w$}}{
  draw $u \sim \mathcal{U}[0, 1/N)$; $c \gets w^{(1)}$; $i \gets 1$\label{alg:ch19-resample:draw}\;
  \For{$m = 1, \dots, N$}{
    $u_m \gets u + (m - 1)/N$\label{alg:ch19-resample:grid}\;
    \While{$u_m > c$}{$i \gets i + 1$; $c \gets c + w^{(i)}$\label{alg:ch19-resample:walk}\;}
    $j_m \gets i$\;
  }
  \KwRet $(j_1, \dots, j_N)$\;
}
\end{algorithm}

Resampling cures degeneracy and creates a second problem. The survivors are
copies of a few ancestors; if the process noise is small, the copies stay
together and the cloud collapses onto a handful of distinct states, which is
\textbf{sample impoverishment}\index{sample impoverishment}\index{particle
filter!impoverishment}. Resampling at every step makes it worse, because
each round discards diversity the noise has not yet restored. The remedies
are to resample only when $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ demands it, to keep the
process noise honest, and to jitter the copies after resampling
(\emph{roughening}\index{roughening}~\cite{gordon1993novel}) or draw them
from a kernel around each particle (the regularised particle
filter~\cite{arulampalam2002tutorial}).

\subsection{The algorithm}
\label{sec:ch19-pf-algorithm}

\Cref{alg:ch19-pf} collects the pieces. It keeps \textbf{log-weights}
\index{log-weights} $\ell^{(i)} = \log w^{(i)}$: the likelihood of a
particle ten standard deviations off is $e^{-50}$, and a product of such
factors underflows double precision within a few steps, whereas sums of
logarithms do not.

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
\caption{One step of the bootstrap particle filter with systematic resampling}
\label{alg:ch19-pf}
\KwIn{particles $\state_{k-1}^{(i)}$ with log-weights $\ell^{(i)}$, $i = 1, \dots, N$; measurement $\meas_k$ or \KwNil; motion model $f$ with process noise $\mat{Q}_k$; likelihood $p(\meas \mid \state)$; threshold $N_{\mathrm{thr}}$}
\KwOut{particles and log-weights at time $k$; estimate $\hat{\state}_k$, $\mat{P}_k$; $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$}
\Fn{\NlfPfStep{$\{\state_{k-1}^{(i)}, \ell^{(i)}\}$, $\meas_k$}}{
  \For{$i = 1, \dots, N$}{
    draw $\vect{w}^{(i)} \sim \Normal(\vect{0}, \mat{Q}_k)$; $\state_k^{(i)} \gets f(\state_{k-1}^{(i)}) + \vect{w}^{(i)}$, heading wrapped\label{alg:ch19-pf:predict}\;
    \If{$\meas_k \ne$ \KwNil}{$\ell^{(i)} \gets \ell^{(i)} + \log p(\meas_k \mid \state_k^{(i)})$\label{alg:ch19-pf:weight}\;}
  }
  $w^{(i)} \gets \exp\bigl(\ell^{(i)} - \max_j \ell^{(j)}\bigr)$; $w^{(i)} \gets w^{(i)}/\sum_j w^{(j)}$; $\ell^{(i)} \gets \log w^{(i)}$\label{alg:ch19-pf:normalise}\;
  $\hat{\state}_k \gets \sum_i w^{(i)}\state_k^{(i)}$ (circular mean for the heading); $\mat{P}_k \gets \sum_i w^{(i)}(\state_k^{(i)} \ominus \hat{\state}_k)(\state_k^{(i)} \ominus \hat{\state}_k)\T$\label{alg:ch19-pf:estimate}\;
  $N_{\mathrm{eff}} \gets 1/\sum_i (w^{(i)})^2$\label{alg:ch19-pf:ess}\;
  \If{$N_{\mathrm{eff}} < N_{\mathrm{thr}}$}{
    $(j_1, \dots, j_N) \gets$ \NlfSystematicResample{$w$}; $\state_k^{(i)} \gets \state_k^{(j_i)}$ and $\ell^{(i)} \gets -\log N$ for all $i$\label{alg:ch19-pf:resample}\;
  }
  \KwRet $\{\state_k^{(i)}, \ell^{(i)}\}$, $\hat{\state}_k$, $\mat{P}_k$, $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$\;
}
\end{algorithm}

Line~\ref{alg:ch19-pf:predict} is the prediction: no Jacobian, no sigma
points, just the model and a noise draw per particle, so the filter accepts
any model you can simulate, even one with discrete decisions such as ``turn
left or right with equal probability''. Line~\ref{alg:ch19-pf:weight} is
Bayes' rule, particle by particle; line~\ref{alg:ch19-pf:normalise}
normalises in the log domain by subtracting the largest log-weight before
exponentiating. The estimate on line~\ref{alg:ch19-pf:estimate} is computed
\emph{before} resampling, from the weighted set; it serves consumers that
want a Gaussian, but the particle set itself is the belief
(\cref{sec:ch19-drone}). Lines~\ref{alg:ch19-pf:ess}--\ref{alg:ch19-pf:resample}
resample only when $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ has dropped below the
threshold.\index{particle filter!algorithm}

\subsection{Worked example: an intruder hidden behind a building}
\label{sec:ch19-pf-example}

\begin{example}[A multimodal posterior]
\label{ex:ch19-occlusion}
An intruder starts at $(0, 5)$ flying north at $8\,\mathrm{m/s}$ towards a
building that occupies $x \in [-10, 10]$, $y \in [50, 70]$. Our sensor at
the origin ($\sigma_r = 2\,\mathrm{m}$, $\sigma_\varphi = 0.05\,\mathrm{rad}$,
$\dt = 0.5\,\mathrm{s}$) loses sight of it while $y \in [35, 75]$, a band
of urban canyon in which the intruder, unknown to us, turns left for one
second at $1\,\mathrm{rad/s}$, flies straight for two, turns back and
continues north on the west side of the building. The particle filter uses
$N = 2\,000$ particles, $N_{\mathrm{thr}} = N/2$, the coordinated-turn model
with $\sigma_a = 0.5\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ and $\sigma_\gamma =
0.3\,\mathrm{rad/s^2}$, and the map as a likelihood: a particle inside the
building gets weight zero. A UKF with the same models runs alongside. The
instance is \code{occlusion\_example()} in the chapter code.
\end{example}

\section{Properties: accuracy, convergence and cost}
\label{sec:ch19-properties}

None of the three filters is optimal for a nonlinear model; each
approximates the Bayes filter, and what can be proved is how good the
approximation is and what it costs.

\begin{proposition}[The EKF and the second-order term]
\label{thm:ch19-ekf-order}
If $f$ and $h$ are affine, the EKF is the Kalman filter and is exact. For a
twice differentiable $g$ and $\state \sim \Normal(\bar{\state}, \mat{P})$,
\begin{equation}
  \E[g(\state)] = g(\bar{\state}) + \frac12 \sum_{i,j} P_{ij}\,
  \frac{\partial^2 g}{\partial x_i \partial x_j}(\bar{\state}) + \text{(fourth and higher order)},
  \label{eq:ch19-second-order}
\end{equation}
and the EKF keeps only the first term: its mean error is of second order in
the spread of the belief, proportional to curvature times covariance.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For affine $g$ the Jacobian is the constant matrix of the model, so the
equations of \cref{alg:ch19-ekf} are those of the Kalman filter. Write
$\state = \bar{\state} + \vect{d}$ with $\E[\vect{d}] = \vect{0}$,
$\E[\vect{d}\vect{d}\T] = \mat{P}$, and expand $g$ to second order; the
linear term has zero mean, the quadratic term has mean $\frac12
\sum_{ij}P_{ij}\,\partial^2 g/\partial x_i\partial x_j$, and the odd terms
of a symmetric distribution vanish. The EKF mean is $g(\bar{\state})$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Properties of the unscented transform]
\label{thm:ch19-ut}
The sigma points of \cref{def:ch19-sigma} satisfy $\sum_i W_i^{(m)}
\mathcal{X}_i = \bar{\state}$ and $\sum_i W_i^{(c)}(\mathcal{X}_i -
\bar{\state})(\mathcal{X}_i - \bar{\state})\T = \mat{P}$. For affine $g$ the
unscented transform returns the exact mean, covariance and cross-covariance.
For any three times differentiable $g$ it returns the mean of
\cref{eq:ch19-second-order} correctly through second order.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The weights $W^{(m)}$ sum to $\lambda/(n+\lambda) + 2n/(2(n+\lambda)) = 1$,
and the points come in symmetric pairs, so $\sum_i W_i^{(m)}\mathcal{X}_i =
\bar{\state}$. The covariance sum has no contribution from $\mathcal{X}_0$
and gives $\frac{1}{2(n+\lambda)}\sum_{i=1}^n 2\,\vect{l}_i\vect{l}_i\T =
\frac{1}{n+\lambda}\mat{L}\mat{L}\T = \mat{P}$. For $g(\state) = \mat{B}
\state + \vect{b}$ every image is $g(\bar{\state}) \pm \mat{B}\vect{l}_i$,
and the three sums of \cref{eq:ch19-ut} reduce to $\mat{B}\bar{\state} +
\vect{b}$, $\mat{B}\mat{P}\mat{B}\T$ and $\mat{P}\mat{B}\T$ by the same
computation. For general $g$, expand each image about $\bar{\state}$: the
odd terms cancel between $\bar{\state} + \vect{l}_i$ and $\bar{\state} -
\vect{l}_i$, the constant term is weighted by $\sum_i W_i^{(m)} = 1$, and the
quadratic terms give $\frac{1}{2(n+\lambda)}\sum_{i=1}^n \vect{l}_i\T
\nabla^2 g\,\vect{l}_i = \frac{1}{2(n+\lambda)}\tr\bigl(\nabla^2 g\,
\mat{L}\mat{L}\T\bigr) = \frac12\tr(\nabla^2 g\,\mat{P})$, the second-order
term of \cref{eq:ch19-second-order} (for each component of $g$).
\end{proof}

The covariance is also correct through second order, with $\beta$ tuning
part of the fourth-order term~\cite{julier2004unscented}; the chapter's
self-test checks the affine exactness numerically. What the proposition does
not say matters as much: the UKF still fits one Gaussian, and its error
grows with the curvature within the belief just as the EKF's does, only more
slowly.\index{unscented transform!second-order accuracy}

\begin{proposition}[Systematic resampling]
\label{thm:ch19-systematic}
Let $N_i$ be the number of copies of particle $i$ produced by
\cref{alg:ch19-resample}. Then $N_i \in \{\lfloor N w^{(i)}\rfloor,
\lceil N w^{(i)}\rceil\}$, $\E[N_i] = N w^{(i)}$, and the algorithm runs in
$\bigO{N}$ time.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Particle $i$ owns the interval $(c_{i-1}, c_i]$ of the cumulative weights,
of length $w^{(i)}$, and $N_i$ is the number of grid points $u + (m-1)/N$
in it; a half-open interval of length $w$ contains $\lfloor Nw \rfloor$ or
$\lceil Nw \rceil$ points of a grid with spacing $1/N$. Each $u_m$ is
uniform on $[(m-1)/N, m/N)$, so it lands in the interval with probability
$N$ times the overlap, and summing over $m$ gives $N w^{(i)}$. The inner
loop advances $i$ at most $N$ times in total.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Convergence of the bootstrap filter; Crisan and Doucet 2002]
\label{thm:ch19-pf-convergence}
Under mild conditions on the model (a bounded, continuous likelihood), for
every bounded function $g$ and every time $k$ there is a constant $c_k$,
independent of $N$, such that
\[
  \E\Bigl[\bigl(\textstyle\sum_i w_k^{(i)} g(\state_k^{(i)}) -
  \E[g(\state_k) \mid \meas_{1:k}]\bigr)^2\Bigr] \le \frac{c_k\,\norm{g}_\infty^2}{N}.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Each sampling step (prediction, resampling) contributes a mean-square error
of order $1/N$ that the next step propagates, and the constants compound
over time; see Crisan and Doucet~\cite{crisan2002survey}.
\end{proof}

The theorem is reassuring and misleading at once: the rate $1/\sqrt{N}$
does not depend on the dimension, but $c_k$ does, and badly, because the
cloud must cover a region whose volume grows exponentially with the state
dimension (\cref{sec:ch19-variants}).\index{particle filter!convergence}

\paragraph{Cost per step.}
With $n$ states and $m$ measurements, the EKF evaluates one Jacobian pair and
does $\bigO{n^3 + n^2 m}$ matrix work; the UKF adds two Cholesky
factorisations and $2(2n+1)$ model evaluations; the particle filter costs
$\bigO{Nn}$ for propagation and likelihoods and $\bigO{N}$ for resampling,
with $N$ in the thousands: $0.27$, $0.47$ and $1.2\,\mathrm{ms}$ per step
for $n = 5$ (\cref{sec:ch19-ukf-compare}). \Cref{tab:ch19-comparison}
summarises.\index{particle filter!cost}

\paragraph{Which filter for which intruder model.}
\index{filter selection}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Linear motion and sensor (constant velocity, position
  measurements): the Kalman filter of \cref{ch:ch18}.
  \item Mild nonlinearity, unimodal belief (coordinated turn at long range,
  small bearing noise, a good initialisation): the EKF, within $1\,\%$ of
  the UKF in \cref{tab:ch19-rmse} at the lowest cost.
  \item Strong nonlinearity, still unimodal (short range, wide bearing
  noise, a poor start, a model without closed-form derivatives): the UKF.
  \item Multimodal or non-Gaussian beliefs (occlusions, buildings, an
  unknown manoeuvre onset, outliers, hard constraints): the particle
  filter, with thousands of particles in five dimensions and more in 3D.
  \item An intruder that switches between straight flight and turns: a bank
  of EKFs or UKFs mixed by the IMM scheme of \cref{sec:ch19-variants}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Variants and extensions}
\label{sec:ch19-variants}

\paragraph{Better Gaussian filters.}
The \emph{iterated EKF} re-linearises $h$ at the updated estimate and
repeats the update until it stops moving; the \emph{second-order EKF} adds the
Hessian term of \cref{eq:ch19-second-order}
explicitly~\cite{barshalom2001estimation}. The \emph{augmented} UKF appends
the noise to the state so that non-additive noise passes through $f$; the
\emph{square-root UKF} propagates the Cholesky factor of $\mat{P}$ and
cannot lose positive definiteness~\cite{wan2000unscented}; the
\emph{cubature Kalman filter} uses $2n$ equally weighted points at
$\sqrt{n}$ standard deviations, a UKF without $\alpha$, $\beta$,
$\kappa$~\cite{arasaratnam2009cubature}.\index{cubature Kalman filter}
\index{iterated EKF}

\paragraph{Switching models.}
With $\omega$ in the state, the coordinated-turn model adapts to a turn only
as fast as $\sigma_\gamma$ allows, hence the error spikes at every turn
onset in \cref{fig:ch19-compare}. The \textbf{interacting multiple
model}\index{interacting multiple model (IMM)} (IMM) filter of Blom and
Bar-Shalom~\cite{blom1988imm,barshalom2001estimation} runs one filter per
manoeuvre mode (straight, left turn, right turn), mixes their estimates with
mode probabilities updated from the innovations, and switches within a step
or two.

\paragraph{Better particle filters, and three dimensions.}
The \emph{auxiliary} particle filter looks at $\meas_k$ before choosing
which particles to propagate; the \emph{regularised} filter resamples from a
smoothed distribution; the \emph{Rao--Blackwellised} filter runs a Kalman
filter for the linear part of the state inside every particle and samples
only the nonlinear part, which is what makes particle methods workable in
higher dimensions~\cite{doucet2001sequential,arulampalam2002tutorial}. For a
drone the state gains altitude and vertical speed, $\state = (x, y, z, v,
\psi, \omega, v_z)$ with $z' = z + v_z\dt$, and the sensor an elevation
angle; the Jacobians gain a row and two columns, the UKF two sigma points,
and the particle filter roughly an order of magnitude in $N$.
\index{particle filter!variants}

\section{Implementation notes}
\label{sec:ch19-implementation}

\paragraph{Keeping $\mat{P}$ a covariance.}
The Cholesky factorisation fails as soon as $\mat{P}$ loses symmetry or
positive definiteness by rounding. Symmetrise, $\mat{P} \gets
\frac12(\mat{P} + \mat{P}\T)$, after every update; use the Joseph form for
the EKF; add a small multiple of the identity before factorising; and if all
else fails clip the negative eigenvalues or move to the square-root UKF.
\index{Cholesky factorisation!numerical issues}

\paragraph{Angles.}
Wrap every difference of angles: bearing innovations, the residuals of
sigma-point images and of particles, and the differences from which a
sigma-point mean is computed; wrap the heading of every estimate and every
particle after prediction and update; and compute the heading of a particle
cloud as the circular mean $\operatorname{atan2}(\sum_i
w^{(i)}\sin\psi^{(i)}, \sum_i w^{(i)}\cos\psi^{(i)})$. The code centralises
this in \code{wrap\_angle}, \code{residual} and \code{weighted\_mean}.

\paragraph{Vectorising the particle filter.}
Store the particles as an $(N, n)$ array and write $f$, $h$ and the
log-likelihood for arrays of states, so that a step is a handful of NumPy
calls; systematic resampling is a cumulative sum and one
\code{searchsorted}. With $N = 2\,000$ this gives $1.2\,\mathrm{ms}$ per
step, a hundred times faster than a Python loop over particles. Keep
log-weights, normalise with the log-sum-exp trick, and detect a dead cloud
(all log-weights $-\infty$) explicitly, restarting the weights flat rather
than dividing by zero.\index{particle filter!vectorisation}
\Cref{lst:ch19-pf} shows the resampling and the update of the particle
filter; \code{sigma\_points} and \code{unscented\_transform} in the same
file transcribe \cref{alg:ch19-ukf} line by line.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Systematic resampling and the update of the bootstrap particle filter with log-weights (from \code{code/ch19\_nonlinear\_filters.py}).},label={lst:ch19-pf}]
def systematic_resample(weights, rng):
    """Indices of the particles to keep: one uniform draw, N evenly spaced points."""
    n = len(weights)
    positions = (rng.random() + np.arange(n)) / n
    cumulative = np.cumsum(weights)
    cumulative[-1] = 1.0                             # guard against rounding
    return np.searchsorted(cumulative, positions)

    def update(self, z=None):
        """Weight by the likelihood (if any), normalise, estimate, resample if needed."""
        if z is not None:
            self.logw += self.sensor.log_likelihood(z, self.X)
        if self.constraint is not None:
            self.logw[~self.constraint(self.X)] = -np.inf
        top = np.max(self.logw)
        if not np.isfinite(top):                     # every particle died: restart flat
            self.logw[:] = -math.log(self.n)
            top = self.logw[0]
        w = np.exp(self.logw - top)                  # log-sum-exp normalisation
        w /= np.sum(w)
        with np.errstate(divide="ignore"):
            self.logw = np.log(w)
        self.n_eff = effective_sample_size(w)
        self.x, self.P = self.estimate(w)
        if self.n_eff < self.threshold:
            idx = systematic_resample(w, self.rng)
            self.X = self.X[idx]
            if self.roughening is not None:              # optional jitter of the copies
                self.X = self.X + self.rng.normal(size=self.X.shape) * self.roughening
            self.logw = np.full(self.n, -math.log(self.n))
            self.resample_count += 1
        return self.x, self.P
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{The Jacobian at the wrong point, and the angle that is not wrapped.}
$\mat{F}_k$ is the derivative of $f$ at the \emph{previous updated}
estimate $\hat{\state}_{k-1}$, from which the prediction starts; $\mat{H}_k$
is the derivative of $h$ at the \emph{predicted} estimate $\hat{\state}_k^-$,
at which the innovation is formed. Swapping the two points costs a fraction
of a metre per step in \cref{ex:ch19-ekf} and, when the state moves fast, a
divergent filter; evaluating $\mat{Q}(\state)$ at the wrong point is the
same mistake in disguise. Just as silent: an intruder crossing the negative
$x$ axis produces bearings that jump from $+3.1$ to $-3.1$, and an unwrapped
innovation of $6.2\,\mathrm{rad}$ times a gain of $-47$ moves the estimate
$290\,\mathrm{m}$ (\cref{exr:ch19-wrapping}); the same happens to sigma-point
images that straddle the seam and to particle likelihoods. Wrap in one
function and call it everywhere.
\end{pitfall}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{Resampling every step, and too few particles.} Resampling discards
diversity; with a small process noise a filter that resamples every step
collapses onto a few distinct states within seconds
(\cref{sec:ch19-resampling}). Resample when $N_{\mathrm{eff}} < N/2$ and
watch the sawtooth of \cref{fig:ch19-particles}: a curve that stays near
$N$ means uninformative measurements, one that stays near $1$ a lost track.
And a cloud of $200$ particles that tracks a target in the plane fails in
the five-dimensional coordinated-turn state, because the fraction of
particles near the truth shrinks with every dimension
(\cref{thm:ch19-pf-convergence} hides this in $c_k$). Start with $N$ in the
low thousands for five or six states, check that the RMSE has stopped
improving with $N$ (\cref{exr:ch19-coding}), and use a Rao--Blackwellised
filter or an IMM when it has not.
\end{pitfall}

\section{Where this fits in the drone system}
\label{sec:ch19-drone}

\begin{dronebox}
The three filters are the prediction layer's answer to \emph{manoeuvring}
intruders in the hybrid architecture of \cref{ch:ch24}: one filter per
tracked drone turns its range--bearing or position measurements into an
estimate with speed, heading and turn rate for the local avoidance layer
(\cref{ch:ch13,ch:ch14}) and a covariance for the trajectory predictor of
\cref{ch:ch20}. The EKF is the default for a turning intruder at long
range; the UKF replaces it when the sensor is close or noisy or the motion
model has no Jacobians. The particle filter is the natural
input to the \emph{prediction-aware} constraints of \cref{ch:ch24}: its
cloud, pushed $H$ steps through the motion model, is a set of weighted
predicted trajectories, and ``stay clear of the intruder with probability
$1 - \delta$'' becomes ``stay clear of the $(1 - \delta)$ fraction of the
particles'', which handles two lobes behind a building without inflating
one ellipse over the whole block. In the study plan (\cref{ch:appA}) this
chapter completes Week~9; its coding exercise is \cref{exr:ch19-coding}.
\end{dronebox}

\section{Summary}
\begin{summary}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A turning intruder (coordinated-turn model, $\state = (x, y, v,
  \psi, \omega)$) and a range--bearing sensor make $f$ and $h$ nonlinear;
  a Gaussian pushed through a bent function is a banana, not an ellipse.
  \item The EKF linearises, $\mat{F}_k = \partial f/\partial\state$ at
  $\hat{\state}_{k-1}$ and $\mat{H}_k = \partial h/\partial\state$ at
  $\hat{\state}_k^-$, then runs the Kalman equations; it is cheap and
  accurate when the belief is narrow compared with the curvature, its mean
  error is the dropped second-order term, and a poor start can make it
  diverge.
  \item The UKF pushes $2n + 1$ sigma points through the true models and
  recombines them with the weights $W^{(m)}$, $W^{(c)}$: no Jacobians, exact
  for affine models, second-order accurate mean, less than twice the cost.
  \item The bootstrap particle filter moves $N$ samples through the motion
  model with sampled noise, weights them by the likelihood and resamples
  systematically when $N_{\mathrm{eff}} = 1/\sum_i (w^{(i)})^2 < N/2$; it
  represents multimodal beliefs and constraints at a cost linear in $N$ and
  fails by degeneracy, impoverishment and too few particles.
  \item Wrap every angle difference, evaluate Jacobians and $\mat{Q}$ at the
  right point, keep $\mat{P}$ symmetric positive definite, keep log-weights,
  vectorise; and choose the cheapest filter whose assumptions hold: KF for
  linear models, EKF for mild nonlinearity, UKF for strong nonlinearity,
  particle filter for multimodality, IMM for switching manoeuvres.
\end{itemize}
\end{summary}

\paragraph{Further reading.}
Thrun, Burgard and Fox~\cite{thrun2005probabilistic} develop all three
filters in the notation of this chapter; S\"arkk\"a~\cite{sarkka2013bayesian}
is the concise mathematical treatment. The unscented transform is due to Julier and
Uhlmann~\cite{julier1997new,julier2004unscented}, the parameters $\alpha$,
$\beta$, $\kappa$ and the square-root form to Wan and van der
Merwe~\cite{wan2000unscented}. The bootstrap filter is Gordon, Salmond and
Smith~\cite{gordon1993novel}; Arulampalam et
al.~\cite{arulampalam2002tutorial} is the standard tutorial with systematic
resampling and the variants; Doucet, de Freitas and
Gordon~\cite{doucet2001sequential} collect the theory. Bar-Shalom, Li and
Kirubarajan~\cite{barshalom2001estimation} cover the coordinated-turn model
and the IMM filter.

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[\difficulty{1}]
\label{exr:ch19-which}
For each combination say whether $f$, $h$, both or neither is nonlinear and
which filter of \cref{ch:ch18} or this chapter you would use, with one
reason: (a) constant-velocity motion, position measurements; (b)
constant-velocity motion, range--bearing measurements; (c) coordinated-turn
motion, position measurements; (d) coordinated-turn motion, range--bearing
measurements; (e) a turn with a \emph{known, fixed} rate $\omega$ and state
$(x, \dot{x}, y, \dot{y})$, position measurements.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[\difficulty{2}]
\label{exr:ch19-ct-jacobian}
Derive the Jacobian \cref{eq:ch19-ct-jacobian} of the coordinated-turn
model (\cref{thm:ch19-ct-jacobian}) from \cref{eq:ch19-ct}, including the six entries for $\omega \to 0$
as limits of the general expressions. Then check your result numerically:
implement the model, compute a central finite difference with step
$10^{-5}$ at $\state = (10, 20, 12, 0.7, \omega)$ for $\omega =
0.3\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ and for $\omega = 0$, and compare with
\code{CoordinatedTurnModel.jacobian}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[\difficulty{1}]
\label{exr:ch19-wrapping}
In \cref{ex:ch19-ekf} the unwrapped bearing innovation is $6.2117\,\mathrm{rad}$.
With the gain printed by \code{worked\_example()} ($K_{12} = 3.21$, $K_{22}
= -47.05$, $K_{42} = 2.56$), compute where the updated position and heading
would have landed, and explain in one sentence why $K_{22}$ is negative.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[\difficulty{2}]
\label{exr:ch19-weights}
For $n = 5$ compute $\lambda$, the distance of the sigma points from the
mean in standard deviations, and the weights $W_0^{(m)}$, $W_0^{(c)}$ and
$W_i^{(m)}$ for $(\alpha, \beta, \kappa) = (1, 2, 0)$, $(1, 2, -2)$ and
$(10^{-3}, 2, 0)$. Which choices give a negative $W_0^{(m)}$? Verify that
the weights $W^{(m)}$ sum to one in every case, and explain why the third
choice is numerically dangerous when the images are computed in floating
point.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[\difficulty{2}]
\label{exr:ch19-banana}
Let $r \sim \Normal(\mu_r, \sigma_r^2)$ and $\varphi \sim \Normal(\pi/2,
\sigma_\varphi^2)$ be independent. Show that the exact mean of $(r\cos
\varphi, r\sin\varphi)$ is $(0, \mu_r e^{-\sigma_\varphi^2/2})$ and evaluate
it for the numbers of \cref{fig:ch19-linearisation}. Then compute the
unscented-transform mean by hand with $n = 2$, $\alpha = 1$, $\beta = 2$,
$\kappa = 1$ (five points, $\lambda = 1$) and compare it with the EKF mean
$(0, \mu_r)$ and with the exact value.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[\difficulty{2}]
\label{exr:ch19-ess}

and check the result against \cref{thm:ch19-systematic}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[\difficulty{3} Coding]
\label{exr:ch19-coding}
Track a turning drone with all three filters: simulate an intruder that
flies the legs of \cref{sec:ch19-ukf-compare} (or your own), observe it

\{100, 300, 1\,000, 3\,000, 10\,000\}$ and plot the particle filter's RMSE
and time per step against $N$. (d) Add a five-second occlusion during the
second turn and report which filters recover. Optional: extend the state to
three dimensions (\cref{sec:ch19-variants}) and repeat (c).
\end{exercise}
