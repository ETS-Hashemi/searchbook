\section{Why this matters for a drone swarm}
\label{sec:ch20-motivation}

The tracker of \cref{ch:ch18} hands the planner a clean estimate of where an
unknown drone \emph{is}. That is not enough. A drone flying at $3$~m/s covers
$7.5$~m in the $2.5$~s that a swarm member needs to brake, turn and
re-establish its formation, and the collision that matters is the one at the
end of those $2.5$~s. The local safety layer of \cref{ch:ch24} therefore asks
a different question: where will the intruder be at $t + 0.2$~s, $t + 0.4$~s,
\dots, $t + 2.4$~s, and how sure are we? This is \emph{trajectory
prediction}, the second half of the prediction layer of the hybrid
architecture.

The simplest answer, ``where it is now plus its velocity times the elapsed
time'', is the constant-velocity extrapolation that the Kalman filter of
\cref{ch:ch18} produces when its prediction step is run several times in a
row. For a drone in steady flight it is exact, and at short horizons it is
remarkably hard to beat: a widely discussed
study~\cite{schoeller2020constant} found that a carefully tuned
constant-velocity model matches or beats many published neural predictors on
the standard pedestrian benchmarks, and the survey of Rudenko et
al.~\cite{rudenko2020human} places that result in context. The reason is not
that learning is useless but that most of a trajectory \emph{is} straight
flight, and that learned models were often compared against a badly tuned
baseline. The interesting cases are the others: the intruder banks into a
turn, begins an evasive maneuver, or is one of several drones whose paths
interact. There the last second of history reveals what happens next, and a
model that has learned the typical shapes of maneuvers predicts the turn
while the straight-line model flies off along the tangent.

The Week~10 milestone of the study plan (\cref{ch:appA}) therefore needs a
strong baseline and an honest measurement: you should be able to
\emph{quantify} whether learning improves prediction, per horizon and per
kind of maneuver, before you let a learned predictor set the safety margins
of a swarm. The chapter builds both, and it prepares what \cref{ch:ch24}
needs: not a single predicted path but a distribution over paths, turned
into a growing ellipse per time step, so that \orca (\cref{ch:ch13}) can
inflate the intruder's radius, \dstarlite (\cref{ch:ch05}) can raise the
cost of the cells it may occupy, and the MPC of \cref{ch:ch21} can impose a
chance constraint. \Cref{sec:ch20-example} is the experiment, run with the
book's own NumPy code, and \cref{sec:ch20-properties} proves the few results
that make it interpretable.

\section{The idea in plain words}
\label{sec:ch20-intuition}

\Cref{fig:ch20-idea} shows the situation. A drone has been observed at eight
time steps, $0.2$~s apart; we want the next twelve positions. Three things
are visible in the picture, and they organize the chapter.

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \inputfigure{ch20/idea}
  \caption{The prediction problem. The last $T_{\mathrm{obs}} = 8$ positions of
  an intruder are observed; the next $T_{\mathrm{pred}} = 12$ must be
  predicted. Blue is a prediction, green the future that actually happens, and
  the red segments are the displacement errors $e_h$ whose average is the ADE
  and whose last value is the FDE. The ellipses grow with the horizon, and the
  dashed orange line is a second future that was just as plausible given the
  history: predictions are distributions, not lines.}
  \label{fig:ch20-idea}
\end{figure}

The past constrains the future --- the drone will not jump, so its next
position and velocity are near its last, which is all the constant-velocity
model uses and for the first few steps enough. The past also reveals the
maneuver: steadily rotating displacements mean a turn that will continue, a
rotation that began two steps ago may be an evasive burst that lasts about a
second, and which patterns occur and how they end is a property of the
\emph{population} of trajectories, learnable from recorded data by a
recurrent network or by a Transformer. And the future is genuinely uncertain,
part noise and part a choice the intruder has not made yet, so a single
predicted line is wrong twice: it lies between the branches and it does not
say how unsure it is (\cref{sec:ch20-multimodal}).

\begin{keyidea}
Predict the next $T_{\mathrm{pred}}$ positions from the last $T_{\mathrm{obs}}$,
in the intruder's own frame, and measure the error at every horizon.
Constant velocity is the baseline that must be beaten; learned sequence
models beat it where the history reveals a maneuver; nothing beats it on
straight flight at the long horizons that set the safety margin, or on a
maneuver that has not started yet. Always predict a
distribution, because the planner needs the spread as much as the mean.
\end{keyidea}

\section{Problem statement, metrics and evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:ch20-problem}

\begin{definition}[Trajectory prediction]
\label{def:ch20-problem}
A \textbf{trajectory}\index{trajectory prediction} is a sequence of positions
$\pos_t \in \R^2$ (or $\R^3$) sampled every $\dt$ seconds. At the current time
$T$ the \textbf{observation window} of length $T_{\mathrm{obs}}$ contains the
positions $\pos_{T - T_{\mathrm{obs}} + 1}, \dots, \pos_T$ (in practice noisy
measurements of them). A \textbf{predictor} maps the observation window, and
possibly the windows of other agents and a map, to predicted positions
$\hat{\pos}_{T+1}, \dots, \hat{\pos}_{T+T_{\mathrm{pred}}}$ over the
\textbf{prediction horizon}\index{prediction horizon} $T_{\mathrm{pred}}$. The
integer $h \in \set{1, \dots, T_{\mathrm{pred}}}$ is the \textbf{horizon step};
$h\,\dt$ is the look-ahead time.
\end{definition}

Three distinctions matter. A \textbf{single-agent} predictor sees one history
at a time; an \textbf{interaction-aware}\index{interaction-aware prediction}
predictor also sees the neighbors, because drones and people avoid each
other. A \textbf{deterministic} predictor outputs one trajectory; a
\textbf{probabilistic} one outputs a distribution, as a mean and covariance
per step or as $K$ sampled trajectories, and a
\textbf{multimodal}\index{multimodal prediction} one can put mass on several
distinct futures. Throughout the chapter $\dt = 0.2$~s, $T_{\mathrm{obs}} = 8$
($1.6$~s) and $T_{\mathrm{pred}} = 12$ ($2.4$~s), the $8/12$-step convention of
the pedestrian benchmarks ETH~\cite{pellegrini2009you} and
UCY~\cite{lerner2007crowds} at a sampling rate suited to drones.

\begin{definition}[Displacement errors]
\label{def:ch20-metrics}
For one trajectory with true future $\pos_{T+h}$ and prediction
$\hat{\pos}_{T+h}$, the \textbf{displacement error} at step $h$ is
$e_h = \norm{\hat{\pos}_{T+h} - \pos_{T+h}}$. The \textbf{average displacement
error}\index{ADE (average displacement error)} and the \textbf{final
displacement error}\index{FDE (final displacement error)} are
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{ADE} = \frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{pred}}} \sum_{h=1}^{T_{\mathrm{pred}}} e_h,
  \qquad
  \mathrm{FDE} = e_{T_{\mathrm{pred}}} ,
  \label{eq:ch20-ade-fde}
\end{equation}
averaged over the trajectories of a test set. The \textbf{per-horizon}
versions are $\mathrm{ADE}_h = \frac{1}{h}\sum_{j \le h} e_j$ and
$\mathrm{FDE}_h = e_h$. For a predictor that outputs $K$ sampled trajectories
with errors $e_h^{(1)}, \dots, e_h^{(K)}$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{minADE}_K = \min_{k} \frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{pred}}} \sum_{h} e_h^{(k)},
  \qquad
  \mathrm{minFDE}_K = \min_{k} e_{T_{\mathrm{pred}}}^{(k)},
  \label{eq:ch20-minade}
\end{equation}
\index{minADE@$\mathrm{minADE}_K$}%
again averaged over the test set. The \textbf{miss rate} is the fraction of
trajectories with $\mathrm{FDE} > \tau_{\mathrm{miss}}$ for a threshold such as
$\tau_{\mathrm{miss}} = 1$~m.
\end{definition}

The ADE rewards a prediction that is close on average; the FDE isolates the
end of the horizon, where errors are largest and the planner's safety margin
is decided. $\mathrm{minADE}_K$ is generous by construction: it scores a
sampler by its \emph{best} of $K$ guesses, which measures whether the samples
cover the true future but not how a planner should choose among them, so a
$\mathrm{minADE}_{20}$ next to the ADE of a deterministic baseline compares
two different things. Displacement errors also ignore physics: a prediction
that passes through a wall has a small ADE if it ends near the truth.
\textbf{Collision-based metrics}\index{collision rate!of predictions} count
the fraction of predicted trajectories that come closer than $d_{\min}$ to a
static obstacle or to the true trajectory of another agent at the same time
step:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{CR}_{d_{\min}} = \frac{1}{\abs{\mathcal{D}}} \sum_{n \in \mathcal{D}}
  \mathbb{1}\bigl[\exists\, h \le T_{\mathrm{pred}},\ \exists\, j \ne i :\
  \norm{\hat{\pos}^{\,i}_{T+h} - \pos^{\,j}_{T+h}} < d_{\min}\bigr],
  \label{eq:ch20-collision-rate}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{D}$ is the test set and $i$ the agent predicted in sample
$n$. Scored against the true $\pos^{\,j}$ the metric measures the predictor;
scored against the other agents' \emph{predictions} it measures a joint
sampler's self-consistency instead. \Cref{ch:ch25} measures the collision
rate of the \emph{planner} that consumes the predictions, the metric that
finally matters.

\paragraph{An honest evaluation protocol.}
\label{sec:ch20-protocol}
Prediction results are easy to inflate, mostly by accident. The protocol of
this chapter has five rules.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Split the \emph{scenes} (recordings) into training, validation and
  test sets, then cut every trajectory into windows of $T_{\mathrm{obs}} +
  T_{\mathrm{pred}}$ samples. Cutting first and splitting the shuffled
  windows puts the future of a training window into the past of a test
  window: the model has seen the answers. This is \textbf{data
  leakage}\index{data leakage}; split by scene, or by time for one long
  recording, and keep scenes with the maneuvers you care about in the test
  set.
  \item Compute normalization statistics on the training set only: whole-data
  statistics leak the test set into the model, and the error then surfaces
  only when the drone flies.
  \item Tune the baselines (the velocity window $k$, the process noise $q$)
  on the validation set with the same care as the learning rate; both change
  the ADE by a large factor (\cref{sec:ch20-example}).
  \item Select epochs and hyperparameters of the learned models on the
  validation set, never on the test set.
  \item Report $\mathrm{ADE}_h$ and $\mathrm{FDE}_h$ as curves over $h$, per
  subset of the test set (maneuver type, density), with the miss rate and,
  for samplers, $\mathrm{minADE}_K$ with $K$ stated; repeat over seeds and
  report the spread. An ADE at one unstated horizon hides whether a model is
  good at $0.4$~s or at $2.4$~s.
\end{enumerate}

\section[The physics baselines]{The baselines: constant velocity, constant acceleration, Kalman extrapolation}
\label{sec:ch20-baselines}

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
\caption{Physics baselines for a horizon of $H$ steps}
\label{alg:ch20-baselines}
\KwIn{observed positions $\meas_1, \dots, \meas_{T_{\mathrm{obs}}}$ (noisy), step $\dt$, horizon $H$, window $k$, filter matrices $\mat{F}, \mat{Q}$}
\KwOut{predicted positions $\hat{\pos}_1, \dots, \hat{\pos}_H$ (and covariances $\mat{\Sigma}_1, \dots, \mat{\Sigma}_H$ for the filter)}
\Fn{\PredCV{$\meas$, $H$, $k$}}{
  $\hat{\vel} \gets (\meas_{T_{\mathrm{obs}}} - \meas_{T_{\mathrm{obs}} - k}) / (k\,\dt)$\label{alg:ch20-baselines:vel}\tcp*{mean velocity over the last $k$ intervals}
  \KwRet $\hat{\pos}_h = \meas_{T_{\mathrm{obs}}} + h\,\dt\,\hat{\vel}$ for $h = 1, \dots, H$\;
}
\Fn{\PredCA{$\meas$, $H$, $k$}}{
  $\vel_1 \gets (\meas_{T_{\mathrm{obs}}} - \meas_{T_{\mathrm{obs}} - k}) / (k\,\dt)$; \quad $\vel_0 \gets (\meas_{T_{\mathrm{obs}} - k} - \meas_{T_{\mathrm{obs}} - 2k}) / (k\,\dt)$\;
  $\hat{\acc} \gets (\vel_1 - \vel_0)/(k\,\dt)$; \quad $\hat{\vel} \gets \vel_1 + \tfrac{1}{2}\hat{\acc}\,k\,\dt$\label{alg:ch20-baselines:acc}\tcp*{$\vel_1$ is the velocity at the midpoint}
  \KwRet $\hat{\pos}_h = \meas_{T_{\mathrm{obs}}} + h\,\dt\,\hat{\vel} + \tfrac{1}{2}(h\,\dt)^2\hat{\acc}$ for $h = 1, \dots, H$\;
}
\Fn{\PredKF{$\meas$, $H$, $\mat{F}$, $\mat{Q}$}}{
  initialize $\hat{\state} \gets (\meas_2,\, (\meas_2 - \meas_1)/\dt)$ and $\mat{P}$ from the measurement noise $\sigma^2\mat{I}$\;
  \For{$t = 3, \dots, T_{\mathrm{obs}}$}{run one predict and one update step of the constant-velocity Kalman filter of \cref{ch:ch18} with $\mat{F}, \mat{Q}$ of \cref{eq:ch20-q}, $\mat{H} = (\mat{I}\;\mat{0})$, $\mat{R} = \sigma^2\mat{I}$ and measurement $\meas_t$\label{alg:ch20-baselines:filter}\;}
  \For{$h = 1, \dots, H$}{
    $\hat{\state} \gets \mat{F}\hat{\state}$; \quad $\mat{P} \gets \mat{F}\mat{P}\mat{F}\T + \mat{Q}$\label{alg:ch20-baselines:extrapolate}\tcp*{predict step only: no measurement}
    $\hat{\pos}_h \gets \mat{H}\hat{\state}$; \quad $\mat{\Sigma}_h \gets \mat{H}\mat{P}\mat{H}\T$\;
  }
  \KwRet $(\hat{\pos}_h, \mat{\Sigma}_h)_{h=1}^{H}$\;
}
\end{algorithm}

\Cref{alg:ch20-baselines} lists the three predictors that every learned model
must beat; \cref{fig:ch20-baselines} shows them on a gently turning test
trajectory of the experiment in \cref{sec:ch20-example}.

\paragraph{Constant velocity (CV).}
\index{constant-velocity model!as prediction baseline}%
The velocity is estimated from the last $k$ intervals
(line~\ref{alg:ch20-baselines:vel}) and held constant. With $k = 1$ the
estimate uses the last two measurements only and is very noisy: the velocity
estimate inherits an error of standard deviation $\sigma\sqrt{2}/(k\dt)$ per
axis, which the look-ahead $h\dt$ turns into a position error growing like
$h\sigma/k$ --- the step $\dt$ cancels (\cref{thm:ch20-cv-noise}). A larger $k$ averages the noise but lags behind
a turn. The window $k$ is the tuning parameter of the baseline and must be
chosen on the validation set; in the experiment below the naive $k = 1$ is
$88\,\%$ worse than the tuned $k = 2$ on straight flight. This is the
\emph{poorly tuned baseline} that many papers beat.

\paragraph{Constant acceleration (CA).}
\index{constant-acceleration model}%
Two successive velocity estimates give an acceleration
(line~\ref{alg:ch20-baselines:acc}). On a circular turn the acceleration
points to the center and rotates with the drone, so extrapolating it as a
constant is right for a step or two and then overshoots: the CA prediction
in \cref{fig:ch20-baselines} cuts inside the turn and ends $4$~m from the
truth, further than either CV prediction. The second difference also
amplifies the measurement noise: its stencil $(1, -2, 1)$ has squared norm
$6$ against the first difference's $2$, and extrapolated to $h = 12$ this
becomes a root-mean-square position error $4.7$ times that of constant
velocity at the same window $k = 3$, so \cref{thm:ch20-cv-noise} predicts a
mean final error of $\sqrt{\pi}/2 \cdot 4.7 \cdot 0.45 = 1.88$~m on straight
flight against the $1.869$~m measured in \cref{tab:ch20-results}, and CA
loses there ($0.830$ against $0.335$~m ADE). ``More physics'' in the
extrapolation is not automatically better.

\paragraph{Kalman extrapolation (KF).}
\index{Kalman filter!extrapolation}%
The constant-velocity Kalman filter of \cref{ch:ch18}, with state
$\state = (\pos, \vel) \in \R^4$, transition
$\mat{F} = \begin{psmallmatrix} \mat{I} & \dt\,\mat{I} \\ \mat{0} & \mat{I}
\end{psmallmatrix}$, measurement $\mat{H} = (\mat{I}\;\;\mat{0})$, measurement
noise $\mat{R} = \sigma^2\mat{I}$ and the white-noise-acceleration process
noise
\begin{equation}
  \mat{Q} = q \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac{1}{3}\dt^3\,\mat{I} & \tfrac{1}{2}\dt^2\,\mat{I} \\[2pt]
                              \tfrac{1}{2}\dt^2\,\mat{I} & \dt\,\mat{I} \end{pmatrix},
  \label{eq:ch20-q}
\end{equation}
is run over the observed window and then \emph{extrapolated}: the predict
step is repeated $H$ times without an update
(line~\ref{alg:ch20-baselines:extrapolate}). The mean is a constant-velocity
extrapolation of the filtered state, so the filter is a CV model whose
velocity estimate weighs the whole window optimally under its noise model;
under that model the filtered mean is the conditional mean of the state given
the measurements (\cref{ch:ch18}), hence the minimum-mean-square-error
predictor (\cref{thm:ch20-conditional-mean}), and its covariance
$\mat{\Sigma}_h$, which grows with $h$, is the first prediction in this
chapter that comes with an uncertainty. Small $q$ trusts the straight-line
model and smooths the noise, large $q$ follows the last measurements and
reacts to turns. None of the three baselines knows that turns end, that
evasive maneuvers have a typical duration, or that two drones on a collision
course will both deviate. That knowledge has to come from data.

  rate $0.17$~rad/s, speed $2.9$~m/s), plotted in the agent frame of

\section[LSTM and sequence-to-sequence prediction]{Sequence models: the LSTM and sequence-to-sequence prediction}
\label{sec:ch20-lstm}

\subsection{The LSTM cell}
\label{sec:ch20-cell}

A \textbf{recurrent neural network}\index{recurrent neural network} reads a
sequence one element at a time and keeps a state vector that summarizes what
it has read. The simplest form updates a hidden state $\vect{h}_t \in \R^D$ as
$\vect{h}_t = \tanh(\mat{W}[\vect{x}_t; \vect{h}_{t-1}] + \vect{b})$, where
$[\vect{x}_t; \vect{h}_{t-1}]$ is the concatenation of the current input and
the previous state. Training it means propagating the loss gradient
backward through every step (\emph{backpropagation through
time}\index{backpropagation through time}, BPTT~\cite{werbos1990bptt}), and
each step multiplies the gradient by the Jacobian of the update, a matrix
whose norm is typically far from~$1$. After twenty steps the product has
either vanished or exploded, and the network cannot learn that something it
saw twenty steps ago matters now. Hochreiter and
Schmidhuber~\cite{hochreiter1997lstm} solved this with the \textbf{long
short-term memory}\index{LSTM (long short-term memory)} (LSTM) cell of
\cref{fig:ch20-lstm-cell}: a second state, the \textbf{cell
state}\index{LSTM (long short-term memory)!cell state} $\vect{c}_t$, that is
changed only by \emph{gated} additions, so that the gradient can flow
through it unchanged. The forget gate, used throughout, was added by Gers,
Schmidhuber and Cummins~\cite{gers2000learning}.

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \inputfigure{ch20/lstm-cell}
  \caption{One step of the LSTM cell. The cell state $\vect{c}$ runs along the
  top and is changed only by a multiplication with the forget gate and an
  addition of the gated candidate; the hidden state $\vect{h}$ is a gated,
  squashed read-out of it. All four gate vectors are computed from the same
  concatenated input $[\vect{x}_t; \vect{h}_{t-1}]$ with their own weights.}
  \label{fig:ch20-lstm-cell}
\end{figure}

\begin{definition}[LSTM cell]
\label{def:ch20-lstm}
An LSTM cell with input size $M$ and $D$ hidden units has weights
$\mat{W}_f, \mat{W}_i, \mat{W}_o, \mat{W}_c \in \R^{D \times (M + D)}$ and
biases $\vect{b}_f, \vect{b}_i, \vect{b}_o, \vect{b}_c \in \R^D$. Given the
input $\vect{x}_t \in \R^M$ and the previous states $\vect{h}_{t-1}, \vect{c}_{t-1}
\in \R^D$, one step computes, with $\vect{z}_t = [\vect{x}_t; \vect{h}_{t-1}]$,
the logistic function $\sigma(a) = 1/(1 + e^{-a})$ and $\odot$ the
element-wise product,
\begin{align}
  \vect{f}_t &= \sigma(\mat{W}_f \vect{z}_t + \vect{b}_f) && \text{forget gate,}\index{LSTM (long short-term memory)!gates} \label{eq:ch20-forget}\\
  \vect{i}_t &= \sigma(\mat{W}_i \vect{z}_t + \vect{b}_i) && \text{input gate,} \label{eq:ch20-input}\\
  \vect{o}_t &= \sigma(\mat{W}_o \vect{z}_t + \vect{b}_o) && \text{output gate,} \label{eq:ch20-output}\\
  \tilde{\vect{c}}_t &= \tanh(\mat{W}_c \vect{z}_t + \vect{b}_c) && \text{candidate values,} \label{eq:ch20-candidate}\\
  \vect{c}_t &= \vect{f}_t \odot \vect{c}_{t-1} + \vect{i}_t \odot \tilde{\vect{c}}_t && \text{cell state,} \label{eq:ch20-cell}\\
  \vect{h}_t &= \vect{o}_t \odot \tanh(\vect{c}_t) && \text{hidden state.} \label{eq:ch20-hidden}
\end{align}
We write $(\vect{h}_t, \vect{c}_t) = \PredCell(\vect{x}_t, \vect{h}_{t-1}, \vect{c}_{t-1})$.
\end{definition}

In \crefrange{eq:ch20-forget}{eq:ch20-hidden}, and only there, $\sigma(\cdot)$
is this squashing function applied entry by entry; everywhere else in the
chapter $\sigma$ is a standard deviation, as in the measurement noise
$\sigma$ of \cref{sec:ch20-baselines} and the predicted $\vect{\sigma}_h$ of
\cref{eq:ch20-nll}.

The two nonlinearities have different jobs. The logistic function maps every
pre-activation into $(0, 1)$, so the gates are soft switches: a forget-gate
entry near $1$ keeps that component of the memory, near $0$ erases it; the
input gate decides how much of the candidate is written and the output gate
how much of the memory is exposed. The hyperbolic tangent maps into
$(-1, 1)$ and produces the \emph{values}: the candidate that may be written
and the squashed memory that may be read. Because \cref{eq:ch20-cell} is a
gated sum rather than a squashed product, the derivative of $\vect{c}_t$ with
respect to $\vect{c}_{t-1}$ along the direct path is exactly
$\diag(\vect{f}_t)$ (\cref{thm:ch20-carousel}); with forget gates near $1$ the
gradient neither vanishes nor explodes, which is why gating helps.

\begin{example}[One LSTM step by hand]
\label{ex:ch20-cell}
Take a cell with $M = 2$ inputs and $D = 2$ hidden units, the weights
\begin{align*}
  \mat{W}_f &= \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & -0.5 & 0.3 & 0.1 \\ 0.2 & 0.4 & -0.3 & 0.6 \end{pmatrix}, &
  \mat{W}_i &= \begin{pmatrix} 1.0 & 0.0 & 0.2 & -0.4 \\ -0.5 & 0.8 & 0.1 & 0.3 \end{pmatrix},\\
  \mat{W}_o &= \begin{pmatrix} 0.3 & 0.3 & -0.6 & 0.2 \\ 0.7 & -0.2 & 0.4 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix}, &
  \mat{W}_c &= \begin{pmatrix} 0.9 & -0.6 & 0.0 & 0.8 \\ -0.4 & 0.5 & 0.7 & -0.1 \end{pmatrix},
\end{align*}
$\vect{b}_f = (1, 1)$ (the usual initialization that starts by remembering)
and all other biases zero. The input is $\vect{x}_t = (1.0, -0.5)$, the
previous states are $\vect{h}_{t-1} = (0.2, -0.1)$ and $\vect{c}_{t-1} = (0.5,
-0.3)$, so $\vect{z}_t = (1.0, -0.5, 0.2, -0.1)$. \Cref{tab:ch20-cell} traces

\subsection{A sequence-to-sequence predictor}
\label{sec:ch20-seq2seq}

\Cref{fig:ch20-seq2seq} shows how LSTM cells become a trajectory predictor.
An \textbf{encoder}\index{sequence-to-sequence model} LSTM reads the observed
history one step at a time; its final states $(\vect{h}, \vect{c})$ summarize
the history. A \textbf{decoder} LSTM starts from those states and rolls the
future out one step at a time: at every step a linear read-out turns its
hidden state into the next displacement, which is fed back as the next
input. \Cref{alg:ch20-seq2seq} is the forward pass; four design decisions
make it work.

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \inputfigure{ch20/seq2seq}
  \caption{The sequence-to-sequence predictor unrolled in time. The encoder
  cells (blue) read the seven observed displacements in the agent frame;
  their final state is handed to the decoder cells (orange), which produce,
  through the read-out $\mat{W}_{\mathrm{out}}$, the mean and standard deviation
  of the next displacement. Each decoder step is fed the mean of the previous
  one (dashed); teacher forcing replaces this feedback by the true
  displacement during training.}
  \label{fig:ch20-seq2seq}
\end{figure}

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
\caption{Sequence-to-sequence prediction with an encoder and a decoder LSTM}
\label{alg:ch20-seq2seq}
\KwIn{normalized observed displacements $\vect{x}_1, \dots, \vect{x}_{T_{\mathrm{obs}}-1}$, horizon $H$, mode $\in \set{\text{free-running}, \text{teacher forcing}, \text{sampling}}$, true future $\vect{y}_{1:H}$ (teacher forcing only)}
\KwOut{means $\vect{\mu}_{1:H}$ and standard deviations $\vect{\sigma}_{1:H}$ of the future displacements}
\Fn{\PredPredict{$\vect{x}_{1:T_{\mathrm{obs}}-1}$, $H$, mode}}{
  $\vect{h}, \vect{c} \gets \vect{0}$; \quad $\bar{\vect{x}} \gets$ mean of the $\vect{x}_t$\tcp*{the constant-velocity displacement}
  \For{$t = 1, \dots, T_{\mathrm{obs}} - 1$}{$(\vect{h}, \vect{c}) \gets \PredCell_{\mathrm{enc}}(\vect{x}_t, \vect{h}, \vect{c})$\label{alg:ch20-seq2seq:encode}\;}
  $\vect{u} \gets \vect{x}_{T_{\mathrm{obs}}-1}$\tcp*{first decoder input: the last observed displacement}
  \For{$h = 1, \dots, H$}{
    $(\vect{h}, \vect{c}) \gets \PredCell_{\mathrm{dec}}(\vect{u}, \vect{h}, \vect{c})$\label{alg:ch20-seq2seq:decode}\;
    $(\vect{\mu}_h, \log\vect{\sigma}_h) \gets \mat{W}_{\mathrm{out}}\vect{h} + \vect{b}_{\mathrm{out}}$; \quad $\vect{\mu}_h \gets \vect{\mu}_h + \bar{\vect{x}}$\label{alg:ch20-seq2seq:readout}\tcp*{residual over constant velocity}
    \uIf{mode = teacher forcing}{$\vect{u} \gets \vect{y}_h$\;}
    \uElseIf{mode = sampling}{$\vect{u} \gets \vect{\mu}_h + \vect{\sigma}_h \odot \vect{\eps}$, $\vect{\eps} \sim \Normal(\vect{0}, \mat{I})$\;}
    \Else{$\vect{u} \gets \vect{\mu}_h$\label{alg:ch20-seq2seq:feedback}\;}
  }
  \KwRet $(\vect{\mu}_{1:H}, \vect{\sigma}_{1:H})$; positions are the cumulative sums of the means, mapped back to the world frame\;
}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Input normalization: the agent-centered frame.}
\index{agent-centered frame}%
The network should not have to learn that trajectories near $x = 40$~m
behave like those near $x = -10$~m, or that a drone heading north turns like
one heading east; positions are arbitrary and the physics is invariant to
translation and rotation. The inputs are therefore \emph{displacements} in a
frame whose origin is the last observed position and whose $x$-axis points
along the last observed heading $\varphi_T$, divided by a typical step length
$s$ so that they are of order one:
\begin{equation}
  \vect{x}_t = \frac{1}{s}\,\mat{R}(-\varphi_T)\,(\pos_t - \pos_{t-1}),
  \qquad t = T - T_{\mathrm{obs}} + 2, \dots, T,
  \label{eq:ch20-frame}
\end{equation}
with $\mat{R}(\cdot)$ a rotation matrix. The targets, the future
displacements, are transformed the same way; predicted displacements are
summed, rescaled and rotated back into world positions. Constant velocity is
then a fixed, simple pattern: the inputs are all $\approx (\bar{x}, 0)$ and
the answer is to repeat them. Line~\ref{alg:ch20-seq2seq:readout} makes this
explicit by adding the mean observed displacement $\bar{\vect{x}}$ to every
decoder output, so that a decoder that outputs zero reproduces the
constant-velocity prediction and the network only learns the
\emph{deviation} from it. In \cref{sec:ch20-example} the same network trained
on absolute positions is worse than the constant-velocity baseline.

\paragraph{Rolling the decoder out.}
The decoder's input at step $h$ is a displacement, and where it comes from
defines three modes of the same network. In \textbf{free-running} mode it is
the mean $\vect{\mu}_{h-1}$ the decoder produced one step earlier
(line~\ref{alg:ch20-seq2seq:feedback}); this is how the predictor is used at
run time. In \textbf{teacher forcing}\index{teacher forcing} mode, used during
training, it is the \emph{true} displacement $\vect{y}_{h-1}$: the decoder is
told the correct answer for the previous step and only has to predict the
next one, which makes early training fast and stable because errors do not
accumulate along the rollout. Its weakness is \textbf{exposure
bias}\index{exposure bias}: trained on perfect inputs and evaluated on its
own imperfect ones, the decoder can learn to copy its input, a shortcut that
works under teacher forcing and fails in free-running mode
(\cref{fig:ch20-training}). The remedies are to train in free-running mode
with the gradient flowing through the feedback, or to move from one to the
other with a schedule~\cite{bengio2015scheduled}. In \textbf{sampling} mode
the input is a sample from the predicted Gaussian: one plausible future per
rollout.

\paragraph{The loss: squared error or Gaussian likelihood.}
\index{loss function!mean squared error}\index{loss function!Gaussian negative log-likelihood}%
With a read-out of two numbers per step the natural loss is the \textbf{mean
squared error} of the predicted positions,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MSE}} = \frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H} \norm{\hat{\pos}_{T+h} - \pos_{T+h}}^2,
  \label{eq:ch20-mse}
\end{equation}
averaged over the batch. It trains a predictor of the \emph{conditional
mean} (\cref{thm:ch20-conditional-mean}) and says nothing about uncertainty.
With four numbers per step, a mean $\vect{\mu}_h$ and a log standard deviation
$\log\vect{\sigma}_h$ of the next displacement, the loss becomes the
\textbf{Gaussian negative log-likelihood} of the true displacements
$\vect{y}_h$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NLL}} = \frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{d \in \set{x, y}}
  \left[ \log\sigma_{h,d} + \frac{(y_{h,d} - \mu_{h,d})^2}{2\sigma_{h,d}^2} \right] + \text{const},
  \label{eq:ch20-nll}
\end{equation}
which is minimized by the conditional mean \emph{and} the conditional
standard deviation: the network learns to say ``I do not know'' where the
training data are unpredictable, for instance right after the onset of an
evasive maneuver. Predicting $\log\vect{\sigma}$ keeps $\vect{\sigma}$
positive without a constraint; a full covariance per step (a third number
for the correlation) is a small extension, and the original Social LSTM used
exactly such a bivariate Gaussian output~\cite{alahi2016social}. The position
covariance at horizon $h$ is the sum of the displacement covariances up to
$h$, which assumes independent step errors and, as \cref{sec:ch20-example}
shows, underestimates the true spread.

\paragraph{Training.}
\index{Adam optimizer}\index{early stopping}%
One epoch draws the training windows in random order, normalizes each
mini-batch to the agent frame of \cref{eq:ch20-frame}, runs the decoder in
free-running (or teacher-forcing) mode, and takes one optimizer step per
batch; the learning rate is interpolated geometrically from $\eta_0$ to
$\eta_E$ over the run. The gradient is computed by BPTT through the decoder,
the hand-over of the state and the encoder, and in free-running mode also
through the fed-back means. The weights are updated with
Adam~\cite{kingma2015adam}, which rescales every parameter's step by a running
estimate of its gradient's magnitude; the gradient is clipped to a maximum
norm because one badly conditioned batch can otherwise throw the weights far
away. \textbf{Early stopping} evaluates the free-running validation ADE after
every epoch, keeps the best epoch's weights and stops after $p$ epochs
without improvement: the one regularizer every trajectory predictor should
have, and where the validation split earns its keep. \code{train\_predictor}
in \code{ch20\_prediction.py} is the loop, line for line.

\subsection{Interaction: the Social LSTM}
\label{sec:ch20-social}

A drone in a swarm, like a pedestrian in a crowd, moves according to its
neighbors. Alahi et al.~\cite{alahi2016social} made the LSTM predictor
interaction-aware with \textbf{social pooling}\index{social pooling}\index{Social LSTM}
(\cref{fig:ch20-social-pooling}). Every agent $i$ has its own LSTM with
hidden state $\vect{h}_{i,t}$. Around agent $i$ lay a grid of $N_o \times N_o$
cells of side $2\rho/N_o$ and, for every neighbor $j$ inside it, add its
hidden state of the previous step to the cell in which it lies:
\begin{equation}
  \mat{S}_{i,t}[m, n, :] = \sum_{j \ne i} \mathbb{1}\bigl[\pos_{j,t-1} - \pos_{i,t-1} \in \text{cell}(m, n)\bigr]\,\vect{h}_{j,t-1} .
  \label{eq:ch20-pooling}
\end{equation}
The states of the \emph{previous} step are pooled, so every agent's cell can
be stepped once per time step in any order; pooling the current states would
make the recurrence circular. The tensor $\mat{S}_{i,t} \in \R^{N_o \times
N_o \times D}$ is flattened, embedded and concatenated with the embedding of
the agent's own displacement as the input of its cell. Because every
$\vect{h}_{j,t-1}$ summarizes the history of agent $j$, agent $i$ sees not
only where its neighbors are but where they are going; because the grid is
centered on $i$ the representation is translation invariant; because cells are
summed, any number of neighbors fits. Social pooling is coarse: a neighbor
on a cell boundary jumps between cells and the grid has a fixed range. Its
successors replaced the grid by a maximum over all neighbors (Social
GAN~\cite{gupta2018social}), by a graph whose edges carry the relative state
of each pair (Trajectron++~\cite{salzmann2020trajectron}) and, most
generally, by attention.

  \centering
  \inputfigure{ch20/social-pooling}
  \caption{Social pooling. Left: a grid centered on agent $i$; neighbors
  inside it ($j_1, j_2, j_3$) contribute their LSTM hidden states, a
  neighbor outside it ($j_4$) is ignored. Middle: the pooling tensor
  $\mat{S}_{i,t}$ holds, per cell, the sum of the previous hidden states
  $\vect{h}_{j,t-1}$ of the neighbors in it. Right: the embedded tensor is

\section{Transformers and attention over agents}
\label{sec:ch20-transformer}

An LSTM reads the history in order and compresses it into one state vector;
whatever the decoder needs from step $3$ of the history must survive five
updates. \textbf{Attention}\index{attention} removes the bottleneck: every
output looks directly at every input and decides how much each one matters.
Vaswani et al.~\cite{vaswani2017attention} showed that a network built from
attention alone, the \textbf{Transformer}\index{Transformer}, outperforms
recurrent networks on sequence transduction, and Giuliari et
al.~\cite{giuliari2021transformer} showed that the same architecture, applied
to one trajectory at a time, beats the LSTM predictors on the pedestrian
benchmarks.

\begin{definition}[Scaled dot-product attention]
\label{def:ch20-attention}
Let $\mat{Q} \in \R^{n_q \times d_k}$ hold $n_q$ \textbf{queries}, $\mat{K} \in
\R^{n_k \times d_k}$ hold $n_k$ \textbf{keys} and $\mat{V} \in \R^{n_k \times d_v}$
the \textbf{values} attached to the keys, one per row. Then
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Attention}(\mat{Q}, \mat{K}, \mat{V})
  = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mat{Q}\mat{K}\T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)\mat{V},
  \qquad
  \alpha_{ij} = \frac{\exp(\vect{q}_i\T\vect{k}_j/\sqrt{d_k})}{\sum_{j'}\exp(\vect{q}_i\T\vect{k}_{j'}/\sqrt{d_k})},
  \label{eq:ch20-attention}
\end{equation}
where the softmax acts on every row: the output for query $i$ is
$\sum_j \alpha_{ij}\vect{v}_j$, a convex combination of the values with the
\textbf{attention weights} $\alpha_{ij} \ge 0$, $\sum_j \alpha_{ij} = 1$.
\index{attention!scaled dot-product}
\end{definition}

\begin{pitfall}[Three letters that mean something else in Part~VI]
In this section, and nowhere else in the book, $\mat{Q}$, $\mat{K}$ and
$\mat{V}$ are the query, key and value matrices of attention: they have
nothing to do with the process-noise covariance $\mat{Q}$ of
\cref{eq:ch20-q} and \cref{ch:ch18,ch:ch19}, with the measurement-noise
covariance $\mat{R}$, with the Kalman gain $\Kgain$, or with the cost
weights $\mat{Q}$ and $\mat{R}$ of \cref{ch:ch21}. The three attention
matrices are data that the network builds from its own input; the
covariances are properties of a motion model and a sensor.
\end{pitfall}

The dot product $\vect{q}_i\T\vect{k}_j$ scores how relevant input $j$ is to
output $i$; the division by $\sqrt{d_k}$ keeps the scores of order one for
random vectors, so that the softmax does not saturate at initialization.
\textbf{Multi-head attention}\index{attention!multi-head} runs $n_h$ such
attentions in parallel on learned projections of the same inputs,
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{head}_m = \operatorname{Attention}(\mat{X}\mat{W}_m^{Q}, \mat{X}\mat{W}_m^{K}, \mat{X}\mat{W}_m^{V}),
  \qquad
  \operatorname{MultiHead}(\mat{X}) = [\operatorname{head}_1, \dots, \operatorname{head}_{n_h}]\,\mat{W}^{O},
  \label{eq:ch20-multihead}
\end{equation}
with $\mat{W}_m^{Q}, \mat{W}_m^{K}, \mat{W}_m^{V} \in \R^{d \times d/n_h}$ and
$\mat{W}^{O} \in \R^{d \times d}$, so that one head can attend to the last
step, another to the onset of the current maneuver, a third to the nearest
neighbor. A Transformer layer wraps the attention block in a position-wise
feed-forward network, residual connections and layer normalization; see
\textcite{vaswani2017attention} and \textcite{goodfellow2016deep}.

\paragraph{Positional encodings.}
\index{positional encoding}%
Attention is a set operation: permuting the inputs permutes the outputs, and
nothing in \cref{eq:ch20-attention} knows that step $7$ comes after step
$6$. The order is injected by adding to the embedding of the input at step
$t$ a \textbf{positional encoding}
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{PE}(t, 2i) = \sin\!\left(\frac{t}{10000^{2i/d}}\right), \qquad
  \mathrm{PE}(t, 2i + 1) = \cos\!\left(\frac{t}{10000^{2i/d}}\right), \qquad i = 0, \dots, d/2 - 1,
  \label{eq:ch20-pe}
\end{equation}
a vector of sinusoids of geometrically spaced frequencies. No two steps have
the same vector, and the encoding of step $t + \delta$ is a fixed linear
function of the encoding of step $t$ (\cref{exr:ch20-pe}), so a head can
learn to attend ``two steps back'' regardless of $t$.

\paragraph{An encoder-decoder Transformer for one trajectory.}
Giuliari et al.~\cite{giuliari2021transformer} use the original
encoder-decoder layout: a stack of encoder layers with self-attention over
the embedded, positionally encoded observed displacements, and an
autoregressive decoder that at step $k$ applies \emph{masked} self-attention
to the displacements predicted so far (a token attends only to earlier ones,
so that training processes all $H$ steps in one pass while staying causal),
then \emph{cross-attention} with the encoder outputs as keys and values, and
a read-out. There is no recurrence, so every observed step is one attention
hop away from every predicted step.

\paragraph{Attention across agents.}
Nothing in \cref{eq:ch20-attention} requires the keys to be time steps of the
\emph{same} agent. Let the tokens be the encoded histories of all $N$ agents
in the scene. The query of agent $i$ scored against the keys of all agents
gives weights $\alpha_{ij}$ that say how much agent $j$ matters for predicting
agent $i$, and $\sum_j \alpha_{ij}\vect{v}_j$ is a learned summary of the
neighborhood: social pooling with a continuous notion of ``nearby''. The
graph attention of Trajectron++~\cite{salzmann2020trajectron} and joint
attention over agents and time steps are the current state of the art for
interaction-aware prediction. \Cref{fig:ch20-attention} shows the mechanism on
four drones. To keep the numbers reproducible without training, the query and
key maps are set by hand in \code{toy\_agent\_attention}: with
$\vect{x} = \pos + \tau\vel$ the position extrapolated $\tau = 2$~s ahead, the
features $\vect{k}_j = (\vect{x}_j, \norm{\vect{x}_j}^2, 1)$ and
$\vect{q}_i = (\vect{x}_i, -\tfrac12, -\tfrac12\norm{\vect{x}_i}^2)\,\sqrt{d_k}/\ell^2$
make the scaled dot product equal to
$-\norm{\vect{x}_i - \vect{x}_j}^2 / (2\ell^2)$, so the weight is large for
agents whose extrapolated positions are close. Drone~A splits its attention
almost equally between itself and drone~B, which is on a collision course
($\alpha_{AB} = 0.48$), gives drone~C, near but diverging, $0.003$ and the
distant drone~D nothing. A trained model learns such maps from data, one per
head.

\paragraph{What attention buys.}
Attention helps at long horizons because the decoder can consult any observed
step directly instead of a compressed state, and it helps with many agents
because the same mechanism ranks neighbors by learned relevance rather than
by a grid. The next subsection builds the smallest predictor that uses both
ideas and measures what they are worth here.

\subsection{A Transformer predictor end to end}
\label{sec:ch20-transformer-model}

The primitives above are enough to build a predictor, and
\code{ch20\_prediction.py} contains one: an encoder-decoder Transformer in
just under two hundred lines of NumPy, its gradient written by hand and
checked against finite differences like the LSTM's. It is trained on the same
$1\,600$ trajectories of \cref{ex:ch20-dataset}, with the same Adam, batches
and budget, so that its row of \cref{tab:ch20-results} differs from the
LSTM's in the model and in nothing else.

\emph{The encoder} embeds each of the $T_{\mathrm{obs}} - 1 = 7$ observed
displacements linearly into $\R^{d}$ with $d = 24$, adds the positional
encoding of \cref{eq:ch20-pe}, and applies one multi-head self-attention layer
with $n_h = 2$ heads (\cref{eq:ch20-multihead}) and a residual connection,
followed by a residual position-wise block
$\tanh(\cdot\,\mat{W}_1 + \vect{b}_1)\mat{W}_2 + \vect{b}_2$ of width $2d$. It
returns one context vector per observed step.

\emph{The decoder} has neither recurrence nor autoregression. The $H = 12$
future steps are represented by their own positional encodings, mapped by a
learned matrix into one query per predicted step,
\begin{equation}
  \mat{Q}_{\mathrm{dec}} = \bigl[\mathrm{PE}(T_{\mathrm{obs}}), \dots, \mathrm{PE}(T_{\mathrm{obs}} + H - 1)\bigr]\T\,\mat{W}^{Q}_{\mathrm{dec}} \in \R^{H \times d};
  \label{eq:ch20-decoder-queries}
\end{equation}
one cross-attention layer reads the encoder outputs with these queries, and a
linear head maps each of the $H$ results to one displacement, added to the
constant-velocity displacement exactly as in \cref{sec:ch20-seq2seq}. All $H$
displacements leave in a single pass: this is a \textbf{non-autoregressive
decoder}\index{non-autoregressive decoder}\index{Transformer!non-autoregressive decoder},
which has no exposure bias to pay but also no way to let step $k$ see what
step $k-1$ predicted. Two things are left out on purpose: the layer is one
layer, not a stack, and there is no layer normalization, which a stack needs
and a single residual layer can do without.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={The forward pass of the Transformer predictor (from \code{code/ch20\_prediction.py}): embedding plus positional encoding, one encoder self-attention layer, the feed-forward block, the cross-attention decoder with one query per predicted step, and the linear head. \code{\_split\_heads} reshapes $(B, T, d)$ into $(B, n_h, T, d/n_h)$ and \code{\_merge\_heads} inverts it.},label={lst:ch20-transformer}]
Z = x @ self.W_emb + self.b_emb + self.pe[:T]     # embed + positional encoding
Q, K, V = Z @ self.Wq, Z @ self.Wk, Z @ self.Wv   # encoder self-attention
qh, kh, vh = _split_heads(Q, nh), _split_heads(K, nh), _split_heads(V, nh)
A = softmax(np.einsum("bhtd,bhsd->bhts", qh, kh) / math.sqrt(dh))
C = _merge_heads(np.einsum("bhts,bhsd->bhtd", A, vh))
Z1 = Z + C @ self.Wo                              # residual connection
Hff = np.tanh(Z1 @ self.Wf1 + self.bf1)
Z2 = Z1 + Hff @ self.Wf2 + self.bf2               # position-wise feed-forward
Q2 = self.pe[T_OBS:T_OBS + P] @ self.Wq2          # one query per predicted step
K2, V2 = Z2 @ self.Wk2, Z2 @ self.Wv2             # decoder cross-attention
q2h = Q2.reshape(P, nh, dh).transpose(1, 0, 2)
k2h, v2h = _split_heads(K2, nh), _split_heads(V2, nh)
A2 = softmax(np.einsum("hpd,bhtd->bhpt", q2h, k2h) / math.sqrt(dh))
C2 = _merge_heads(np.einsum("bhpt,bhtd->bhpd", A2, v2h))
Z3 = Q2 + C2 @ self.Wo2
out = Z3 @ self.W_out.T + self.b_out + self.baseline(x)[:, None]
\end{lstlisting}

The model has $7\,106$ weights against the LSTM's $9\,026$, and trains with
the same learning rate, batches, clipping and epoch budget; only the patience
of the early stopping is $15$ epochs rather than $10$, because without layer
normalization the validation curve is noisy enough that $10$ stopped this
initialization at epoch~$12$ while it was still improving. The run keeps
epoch~$24$ of $39$ and takes nine seconds.

The result is the last row of \cref{tab:ch20-results,tab:ch20-horizon} and
the pentagon curve of \cref{fig:ch20-results}: an ADE of $0.831$~m and an FDE
of $1.758$~m over the test set, $27\,\%$ below the tuned constant velocity,
one centimeter above LSTM-NLL's $0.821$~m and four above LSTM-MSE's
$0.789$~m. The pattern per subset is the LSTM's: it halves the error on turns
($0.504$ against the tuned CV's $1.125$~m), takes a third off the visible
evasive maneuvers ($1.594$ against $2.351$~m), loses to constant velocity on
straight flight ($0.470$ against $0.335$~m) and is level with everything else
on the hidden maneuvers ($1.639$~m). Per horizon it tracks the LSTM within
three centimeters, and its miss rate of $0.512$ sits between LSTM-NLL's
$0.520$ and LSTM-MSE's $0.436$.

\paragraph{What this experiment shows, and what it does not.}
It shows that the attention block, the positional encoding and a linear head
are already a complete predictor: an exact hand-written gradient, nine
seconds of training, and a model that beats every physics baseline on the
maneuvers it can see. It does \emph{not} show that Transformers predict
trajectories better than recurrent networks. Three reasons not to read it
that way.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Measure the spread before believing a gap.}
  \code{seed\_study()} retrains all three learned models with three
  initializations on the same split: LSTM-NLL $0.824 \pm 0.004$~m,
  Transformer $0.821 \pm 0.012$~m, LSTM-MSE $0.794 \pm 0.005$~m. The
  Transformer and the Gaussian LSTM are indistinguishable, and the
  squared-error LSTM is ahead of both by more than twice the largest spread.
  The single-seed column of \cref{tab:ch20-results} would have supported
  either conclusion.
  \item \emph{The bottleneck this architecture removes is not binding here.}
  Attention pays when the decoder must reach far back into a long history;
  ours is seven steps, which one LSTM state carries easily.
  \textcite{giuliari2021transformer} report their advantage with longer
  histories, thousands of recorded scenes and a stack of layers --- three
  differences, none of them reproduced here.
  \item \emph{The model is small on purpose}: one layer, no layer
  normalization, a parallel decoder and a squared-error head, so it has no
  uncertainty to report and its calibration column is empty. Each of those is
  a small change to the file and each costs data; \cref{exr:ch20-coding}(d)
  asks you to make them one at a time and to check whether any moves the
  numbers beyond that spread.
\end{itemize}
The general bills are the same. \emph{Data}: more parameters and fewer
built-in assumptions than an LSTM means more trajectories are needed to fit
them, so on a few hundred recorded maneuvers a small LSTM, or constant
velocity, remains the better choice. \emph{Compute}: the quadratic term of
\cref{thm:ch20-complexity} is harmless for the $7 \times 7$ and $12 \times 7$
score matrices here and bites for a planner that attends over all agents and
all time steps every $0.1$~s. \emph{Engineering}: one layer fits in a
listing, a stack wants a framework (\cref{sec:ch20-implementation}). What
attention buys even here is readable weights: averaged over the test set the
decoder queries put $85\,\%$ of theirs on the first four of the seven
observed displacements. The last displacements are already in the
constant-velocity term the head adds, so what the attention has to supply is
how the heading has \emph{changed} across the window.

\section{Multimodality and uncertainty}
\label{sec:ch20-multimodal}

\Cref{thm:ch20-conditional-mean} states the problem precisely: a predictor
trained with the squared error converges to the conditional mean of the
future, so when the future has two distinct modes, left and right around an
obstacle, the prediction runs through the obstacle. The Gaussian output of
\cref{eq:ch20-nll} reports the disagreement as a large $\vect{\sigma}$, which
is honest but still unimodal. Three families of remedies exist.

\paragraph{Mixture density outputs.}
\index{mixture density network}%
The read-out can produce, per step, the parameters of a \textbf{mixture of
Gaussians}~\cite{bishop1994mixture}: $C$ weights $\pi_c$ (a softmax over $C$
logits), means $\vect{\mu}_c$ and covariances $\mat{\Sigma}_c$, with the loss
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MDN}} = -\log \sum_{c=1}^{C} \pi_c\, \Normal\bigl(\vect{y}; \vect{\mu}_c, \mat{\Sigma}_c\bigr),
  \label{eq:ch20-mdn}
\end{equation}
computed with the log-sum-exp trick. At a fork the network learns two
components with weights $\approx \tfrac12$; on straight flight one takes all
the weight. Mixtures are the cheapest form of multimodality and are used by
Trajectron++ and many Transformer predictors.

\paragraph{Sampling-based models.}
\index{generative model!for prediction}%
A second approach feeds the decoder a random latent vector $\vect{z}$ and
trains the network so that different $\vect{z}$ give different plausible
futures. Social GAN~\cite{gupta2018social} does this adversarially, with a
discriminator that tells generated futures from real ones and a \emph{variety
loss} that back-propagates only the best of $K$ samples;
Trajectron++~\cite{salzmann2020trajectron} uses a conditional variational
autoencoder with a discrete latent variable, so that each value of $\vect{z}$
is an interpretable mode, and integrates sampled controls through a dynamics
model so that every sample is feasible. Both are scored with
$\mathrm{minADE}_K$ and need a planner that can consume a set of samples. The
sampling mode of \cref{alg:ch20-seq2seq} is the poor man's version: diverse
rollouts from a unimodal per-step Gaussian.

\paragraph{Ensembles.}\index{ensemble}%
Training several copies with different seeds and averaging their outputs
improves accuracy, and their spread estimates the \emph{model} uncertainty
that the per-step $\vect{\sigma}$ cannot
see~\cite{lakshminarayanan2017ensembles}.

\paragraph{From a distribution to an occupancy region.}
\index{occupancy region!time-indexed}%
Whatever produces the distribution, the planner needs it as geometry. For a
Gaussian prediction at step $h$ with mean $\hat{\pos}_{T+h}$ and covariance
$\mat{\Sigma}_h$, the set of positions the intruder occupies with probability
$p$ is the covariance ellipse of \cref{def:ch02-covariance-ellipse},
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{E}_h(p) = \set{\vect{x} : (\vect{x} - \hat{\pos}_{T+h})\T\mat{\Sigma}_h\inv(\vect{x} - \hat{\pos}_{T+h}) \le k_p^2},
  \qquad k_p = \sqrt{-2\ln(1 - p)},
  \label{eq:ch20-ellipse}
\end{equation}
where $k_p$ follows from the $\chi^2$ distribution with two degrees of
freedom ($k_{0.95} = 2.448$). The sequence $\mathcal{E}_1(p), \dots,
\mathcal{E}_H(p)$ is the \textbf{time-indexed occupancy region} of the
intruder. For a mixture, take the union of the ellipses of the components
whose weight exceeds a threshold; for $K$ samples, the smallest disks or grid
cells that contain a fraction $p$ of them at each step. Before any of this is
trusted its \textbf{calibration}\index{calibration} must be checked on
held-out data: the fraction of true positions inside $\mathcal{E}_h(p)$ should
be about $p$ at every $h$. Learned predictors are often over-confident, the
experiment below quantifies by how much, and the repair is a per-horizon
inflation factor fitted on the validation set
(\cref{exr:ch20-calibration}).

\section[A worked example]{A worked example: constant velocity against a learned predictor}

\begin{example}[Synthetic drone trajectories]
\label{ex:ch20-dataset}
A drone flies at a constant speed drawn from $[1.5, 4]$~m/s with a random
initial heading; its heading changes at a turn rate that depends on the
maneuver class: \emph{straight flight} ($40\,\%$ of the trajectories, turn

$\abs{\omega} \in [0.8, 1.5]$~rad/s lasting $4$--$6$ steps with an onset

every $\dt = 0.2$~s for $20$ steps; the first $T_{\mathrm{obs}} = 8$ are
observed with Gaussian measurement noise of $\sigma = 0.05$~m, the last

\paragraph{Straight flight: constant velocity wins the long horizons.}
On the $317$ straight trajectories the tuned CV has an ADE of $0.335$~m and
the Kalman extrapolation $0.312$~m, against $0.427$~m for LSTM-NLL and
$0.407$~m for LSTM-MSE. The advantage is a long-horizon one: up to $h = 7$
($1.4$~s) the two are within $3.5$~cm of each other and the LSTM is in fact
slightly ahead, and only from $h = 8$ on does the tuned CV pull away
(\cref{fig:ch20-results}, right). This is the expected order, not an accident
of training: under constant-velocity motion the CV prediction is exact and
its only error is amplified measurement noise (\cref{thm:ch20-cv-noise}),
which the Kalman filter averages almost optimally, while the learned model
can only add noise-driven corrections and the \emph{hedge} it has learned
against maneuvers that begin after the window. The naive $k = 1$ baseline is
twice as bad ($0.632$~m): its velocity uses two noisy points $0.2$~s apart,
and \cref{thm:ch20-cv-noise} predicts a root-mean-square final error of
$1.25$~m, hence a mean error of $\sqrt{\pi}/2 \cdot 1.25 = 1.11$~m for an
isotropic Gaussian error, against the measured $1.125$~m. A paper that
compares against \emph{this} baseline would report a learned model that
``halves the error on straight flight''.

\paragraph{Turns and evasive maneuvers: the history reveals the curvature.}
On the $236$ gentle turns the CV and KF predictions leave along the tangent
and end $2.7$~m from the truth, the CA prediction overshoots to $2.2$~m, and
the LSTMs, which see the steady rotation of the observed displacements, end
at $0.9$--$1.0$~m and halve the ADE ($0.55$ against $1.12$~m). On the $187$
maneuvers that start inside the window the LSTMs cut the ADE from $2.35$~m
(tuned CV) to $1.59$~m; the rest is large because how long the burst
continues is only partly predictable from its onset. Here the naive $k = 1$
CV ($2.14$~m) beats the tuned $k = 2$: during a sharp turn the most recent
velocity is the best one. On the $60$ maneuvers that start \emph{after} the
window every predictor scores $1.6$--$1.8$~m and none is ahead by more than
the noise of $60$ trajectories: the irreducible, multimodal part of the
problem, where the honest report is that the learned model has lost
nothing.

\Cref{tab:ch20-horizon} shows the advantage growing with the horizon: at
$0.8$~s the LSTM's $\mathrm{ADE}_4$ of $0.227$~m is $25\,\%$ below the tuned
CV, at $2.4$~s it is $28\,\%$ below and $0.32$~m in absolute terms; the
$0.82$~m over ``all'' hides a $0.43$~m and a $1.60$~m. LSTM-TF pays the
exposure bias of \cref{sec:ch20-seq2seq} in full: its free-running validation
ADE is best at epoch~$3$ and then drifts upward while its training loss keeps
falling, so early stopping ends the run after $13$ epochs and on the test set
it is barely better than CV (\cref{fig:ch20-training}). LSTM-MSE is slightly
more accurate than LSTM-NLL ($0.789$ against $0.821$~m) but knows nothing
about its own uncertainty. LSTM-NLL's $20$ sampled rollouts reach
$\mathrm{minADE}_{20} = 0.695$~m and $\mathrm{minFDE}_{20} = 1.415$~m, below

poor: the $95\,\%$ ellipse contains $76\,\%$ of the true positions at $h = 4$
and only $54\,\%$ at $h = 12$, while the Kalman ellipse contains
$79$--$85\,\%$. The learned $\vect{\sigma}_h$ has the right shape, growing
with $h$ and larger during maneuvers, but the step errors of a free-running
rollout are strongly correlated, and summing per-step variances as if they
were independent underestimates the spread.

\section[Properties]{Properties: when the baseline is optimal, and what the models cost}
\label{sec:ch20-properties}

\begin{proposition}[Error of constant-velocity extrapolation]
\label{thm:ch20-cv-error}
Let the observations be noise-free. (i) If the agent moves with constant
velocity, the CV prediction of \cref{alg:ch20-baselines} has $e_h = 0$ for
every $h$ and every window $k$. (ii) If the agent moves with constant
acceleration $\acc$, then, for the exact velocity at time $T$,
\[
  e_h = \tfrac{1}{2}\norm{\acc}\,(h\,\dt)^2, \qquad
  \mathrm{FDE} = \tfrac{1}{2}\norm{\acc}\,(H\,\dt)^2, \qquad
  \mathrm{ADE} = \tfrac{1}{2}\norm{\acc}\,\dt^2\,\frac{(H+1)(2H+1)}{6},
\]
while the CA predictor has $e_h = 0$. A circular turn at speed $v$ and rate
$\omega$ has centripetal acceleration $v\omega$, so (ii) describes the CV
error on turns for $\omega h\,\dt \ll 1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(i) With $\pos_t = \pos_0 + t\,\dt\,\vel$, line~\ref{alg:ch20-baselines:vel}
gives $(\pos_T - \pos_{T-k})/(k\,\dt) = \vel$ and $\hat{\pos}_{T+h} = \pos_T +
h\,\dt\,\vel = \pos_{T+h}$. (ii) With $\pos_{T+h} = \pos_T + h\,\dt\,\vel_T +
\tfrac12 (h\,\dt)^2\acc$ and the CV prediction $\pos_T + h\,\dt\,\vel_T$, the
difference is $\tfrac12 (h\,\dt)^2\acc$; summing $h^2$ from $1$ to $H$ gives
$H(H+1)(2H+1)/6$, and dividing by $H$ gives the ADE. The CA predictor of
line~\ref{alg:ch20-baselines:acc} recovers $\acc$ and $\vel_T$ exactly from
three noise-free points of a parabola.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Noise amplification of constant velocity]
\label{thm:ch20-cv-noise}
Let the agent move with constant velocity and let the observations be
$\meas_t = \pos_t + \vect{\eps}_t$ with independent $\vect{\eps}_t \sim
\Normal(\vect{0}, \sigma^2\mat{I})$ in $\R^2$. Then the CV prediction with
window $k$ has
\[
  \E\bigl[e_h^2\bigr] = 2\sigma^2\left[\Bigl(1 + \frac{h}{k}\Bigr)^2 + \Bigl(\frac{h}{k}\Bigr)^2\right].
\]
For $\sigma = 0.05$~m and $h = 12$: $\sqrt{\E[e_h^2]} = 1.25$~m for $k = 1$,
$0.65$~m for $k = 2$ and $0.23$~m for $k = 7$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The prediction is $\hat{\pos}_{T+h} = \bigl(1 + \frac{h}{k}\bigr)\meas_T -
\frac{h}{k}\meas_{T-k}$. Substituting $\meas_t = \pos_t + \vect{\eps}_t$ and
using $\pos_T + \frac{h}{k}(\pos_T - \pos_{T-k}) = \pos_{T+h}$ (case (i) of
\cref{thm:ch20-cv-error}) leaves the error $\bigl(1 +
\frac{h}{k}\bigr)\vect{\eps}_T - \frac{h}{k}\vect{\eps}_{T-k}$, a sum of two
independent Gaussian vectors; its expected squared norm is the sum of the
variances of its two coordinates.
\end{proof}

The proposition explains the first two rows of \cref{tab:ch20-results}: the
noise term falls with $k$, but on a turn a large $k$ averages a rotating
velocity and lags, so the best $k$ depends on the mixture of maneuvers.

\begin{theorem}[The conditional mean minimizes the expected squared error]
\label{thm:ch20-conditional-mean}
Let $\vect{x}$ be the observation and $\vect{y}$ the future, jointly
distributed with $\E\norm{\vect{y}}^2 < \infty$. For every measurable
predictor $g$,
\[
  \E\norm{\vect{y} - g(\vect{x})}^2 = \E\norm{\vect{y} - \E[\vect{y} \mid \vect{x}]}^2 + \E\norm{\E[\vect{y} \mid \vect{x}] - g(\vect{x})}^2,
\]
so the expected squared error is minimized by $g^\star(\vect{x}) =
\E[\vect{y} \mid \vect{x}]$ and, up to modification on a set of measure
zero, by nothing else. A predictor trained with
\cref{eq:ch20-mse} on enough data therefore approximates the conditional
mean of the future, and a Gaussian read-out trained with \cref{eq:ch20-nll}
approximates the conditional mean and the conditional
variance.\index{conditional mean}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Write $\vect{m} = \E[\vect{y} \mid \vect{x}]$ and expand $\norm{\vect{y} -
g(\vect{x})}^2 = \norm{\vect{y} - \vect{m}}^2 + 2(\vect{y} - \vect{m})\T(\vect{m} -
g(\vect{x})) + \norm{\vect{m} - g(\vect{x})}^2$. Conditional on $\vect{x}$ the
cross term has expectation zero, because $\E[\vect{y} - \vect{m} \mid \vect{x}]
= \vect{0}$ while $\vect{m} - g(\vect{x})$ is fixed. Taking the expectation over
$\vect{x}$ gives the identity; the first term does not depend on $g$ and the
second is non-negative and zero exactly when $g = \vect{m}$ almost surely.
For the Gaussian read-out, $\E[\log\sigma + (y - \mu)^2/(2\sigma^2) \mid
\vect{x}]$ is minimized over $\mu$ by the conditional mean and then over
$\sigma$ by $\sigma^2 = \Var(y \mid \vect{x})$, by setting the derivatives to
zero.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Mode averaging]
\label{thm:ch20-mode-averaging}
If, given the observation, the future is $\vect{y}^{(1)}$ with probability
$\pi$ or $\vect{y}^{(2)}$ with probability $1 - \pi$, the squared-error-optimal
prediction is $\pi\vect{y}^{(1)} + (1 - \pi)\vect{y}^{(2)}$, which for $\pi =
\tfrac12$ is the midpoint between the two, a trajectory that never occurs. A
two-sample predictor that outputs both has $\mathrm{minADE}_2 = 0$.
\end{corollary}

This is the whole case for multimodal outputs, and why the learned model of
\cref{sec:ch20-example} hedges on straight flight: some straight-looking
histories are followed by a maneuver, and the conditional mean shortens the
predicted progress accordingly.

\begin{proposition}[The constant error carousel]
\label{thm:ch20-carousel}
In the LSTM cell of \cref{def:ch20-lstm}, the Jacobian of $\vect{c}_t$ with
respect to $\vect{c}_{t-1}$ along the direct path (holding $\vect{h}_{t-1}$
fixed) is $\diag(\vect{f}_t)$, and over $\tau$ steps the direct-path product is
$\prod_{s=1}^{\tau}\diag(\vect{f}_{t-s+1})$, whose entries stay close to $1$ as
long as the forget gates do. For the simple recurrence $\vect{h}_t =
\tanh(\mat{W}\vect{z}_t + \vect{b})$ the corresponding product is $\prod_s
\diag(1 - \vect{h}_{t-s+1}^{2})\,\mat{W}_h$, with $\mat{W}_h$ the recurrent block
of $\mat{W}$, which shrinks or grows geometrically unless $\mat{W}_h$ has unit
gain.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$\vect{c}_{t-1}$ enters \cref{eq:ch20-cell} only through the product
$\vect{f}_t \odot \vect{c}_{t-1}$, so with the gates held fixed
$\partial\vect{c}_t/\partial\vect{c}_{t-1} = \diag(\vect{f}_t)$, and the chain
rule multiplies these diagonal matrices along the path. For the simple
recurrence the chain rule gives a product of $\diag(\tanh'(\cdot))\mat{W}_h$
factors, whose norm is bounded by $\norm{\mat{W}_h}^\tau$ and, since
$\tanh' \le 1$ with equality only at $0$, is typically much smaller.
\end{proof}

The full Jacobian also contains the indirect paths through $\vect{h}_{t-1}$,
which behave like the simple recurrence, so the LSTM does not
\emph{guarantee} stable gradients; it guarantees a path along which the error
signal survives when the forget gates are near $1$. The initialization
$\vect{b}_f = \vect{1}$ starts there, and clipping handles the explosions the
indirect paths can still cause.

\begin{proposition}[Cost of the sequence models]
\label{thm:ch20-complexity}
(i) One LSTM step with $M$ inputs and $D$ hidden units costs
$\Theta(D(M + D))$ multiply-adds; encoding $T_{\mathrm{obs}}$ steps and
decoding $H$ steps costs $\Theta((T_{\mathrm{obs}} + H)\,D(M + D))$ and is
inherently sequential in time. (ii) One scaled dot-product attention over
$n$ tokens of dimension $d$ costs $\Theta(n d^2)$ for the query, key and
value projections and $\Theta(n^2 d)$ for the scores and the weighted sum,
with all $n$ outputs computable in parallel; multi-head attention with $n_h$
heads of dimension $d/n_h$ has the same total. (iii) Attention over all $N$
agents and $T$ time steps of a scene as one token set has $n = NT$ and costs
$\Theta(N^2 T^2 d + NT d^2)$ per layer, against $\Theta(NT\,D(M + D))$ for one
LSTM per agent.
\index{attention!complexity}\index{LSTM (long short-term memory)!complexity}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(i) One step multiplies a $4D \times (M + D)$ matrix by a vector; the rest is
element-wise on vectors of length $D$, and each step needs the states of the
previous one. (ii) Each of $\mat{Q}, \mat{K}, \mat{V}$ is an $(n \times d)(d
\times d)$ product; $\mat{Q}\mat{K}\T$ is $(n \times d)(d \times n)$, the
softmax is $\Theta(n^2)$ and the product with $\mat{V}$ is $(n \times n)(n
\times d)$. With $n_h$ heads each projection costs $n d^2/n_h$ and each score
matrix $n^2 d/n_h$; summing over heads restores the totals. (iii) Substitute
$n = NT$ in (ii); for (i) run $N$ independent encoders and decoders.
\end{proof}

\section{Variants and extensions}
\label{sec:ch20-variants}

\Cref{tab:ch20-methods} places the predictors of this chapter side by side.
\emph{Goal-conditioned} predictors first predict where the agent is heading
and then how it gets there, which turns multimodality into a small
classification problem. \emph{Map-aware} predictors add a rasterized map or a
graph of corridors as an input; in a warehouse the aisles decide the modes.
\emph{Joint} prediction outputs the futures of all agents together, so that
the samples are consistent. \emph{Dynamics integration}, as in Trajectron++,
predicts controls and integrates them through the drone model of
\cref{def:ch02-double-integrator}, which makes every sample feasible.
Everything here carries over to three dimensions: the agent frame rotates
about the vertical axis, displacements have three coordinates and ellipses
become ellipsoids. Rudenko et al.~\cite{rudenko2020human} survey the field
along these axes.

\section{Implementation notes}
\label{sec:ch20-implementation}

The reference implementation \code{ch20\_prediction.py} is about $1\,200$
lines of NumPy: the generator of \cref{ex:ch20-dataset}, the baselines, the
metrics, the LSTM cell with its backward pass (\cref{lst:ch20-cell} is the
forward step), the sequence-to-sequence model with BPTT, Adam, the training
loop, attention, the Transformer of \cref{sec:ch20-transformer-model} with
its own backward pass, the ellipse helpers and the experiment. Its self-test checks that the metrics vanish for a perfect
predictor, that CV is exact on straight flight and CA on a parabola, that
the Kalman covariance grows with the horizon, that all gate outputs lie in
$(0, 1)$, that the BPTT gradients agree with finite differences to $10^{-6}$
in all three decoder modes and the Transformer's to $10^{-5}$, that training
lowers the loss and beats the constant-velocity start for both models, and
that attention weights are probability vectors;
it then runs the experiment and asserts the claims of \cref{sec:ch20-example}.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={One step of the LSTM cell (from \code{code/ch20\_prediction.py}); the backward pass follows it in the file.},label={lst:ch20-cell}]
def forward(self, x, h_prev, c_prev):
    """One step.  x (B, n_in), h_prev and c_prev (B, n_hidden)."""
    H = self.n_hidden
    z = np.concatenate([x, h_prev], axis=1)          # [x_t ; h_{t-1}]
    a = z @ self.W.T + self.b                        # pre-activations
    f = sigmoid(a[:, :H])                            # forget gate
    i = sigmoid(a[:, H:2 * H])                       # input gate
    o = sigmoid(a[:, 2 * H:3 * H])                   # output gate
    g = np.tanh(a[:, 3 * H:])                        # candidate values
    c = f * c_prev + i * g                           # new cell state
    tc = np.tanh(c)
    h = o * tc                                       # new hidden state
    cache = (z, f, i, o, g, c_prev, tc)
    return h, c, cache
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Normalization, sequence lengths, batches and seeds.}
The model stores the observed displacements of a batch as an array of shape
$(B, T_{\mathrm{obs}} - 1, 2)$ and the targets as $(B, T_{\mathrm{pred}}, 2)$,
both in the agent frame divided by $s = 0.5$~m. Fixed window lengths keep the
batches rectangular; variable-length histories need padding and a mask.
Shuffle windows, not scenes, keep whole scenes in one split, and use one
seeded generator for the data and another for the training, so that a change
to the model does not change the data. Evaluate on held-out maneuver types
at least once: a test set whose maneuvers all occur in training measures
interpolation, not prediction. Initialize the forget-gate biases to $1$ and
the read-out to small values, so that the untrained network starts as the
constant-velocity model, and clamp the log standard deviations: a network
that drives $\sigma \to 0$ on a few easy examples produces huge gradients on
the next hard one. The finite-difference check in the self-test is the only
way to be sure a hand-written backward pass is right.

\paragraph{The PyTorch version.}
For real data, and for a stack of Transformer layers, use a framework. The
same sequence-to-sequence model is written in PyTorch in
\code{ch20\_prediction\_torch.py} (not run by the self-test): an
\code{nn.LSTM} encodes the batch in one call, an \code{nn.LSTMCell} is
stepped by hand so that the mean can be fed back, an \code{nn.Linear}
read-out produces $(\vect{\mu}_k, \log\vect{\sigma}_k)$ and autograd replaces
the hand-written BPTT --- a dozen lines of forward pass against the two
hundred of the NumPy model, at the price of not seeing the gradient.
Replacing the encoder and decoder by \code{nn.TransformerEncoder} and
\code{nn.TransformerDecoder} with the positional encoding of
\cref{eq:ch20-pe} gives the model of Giuliari et
al.~\cite{giuliari2021transformer}.

\begin{pitfall}[Predicting in absolute coordinates]
A network trained on world positions has to learn that the physics is the
same everywhere and in every direction. With $1600$ trajectories it did not
(\cref{tab:ch20-results}: $1.315$ against the tuned CV's $1.141$~m overall,
and worse on every subset except the hidden maneuvers, where no predictor is
separated from the others). Predict
displacements in the agent-centered frame of \cref{eq:ch20-frame}, and let the
network predict the correction to constant velocity rather than the motion
itself.
\end{pitfall}

\begin{pitfall}[Ignoring multimodality at intersections]
At a junction the conditional mean runs through the pillar
(\cref{thm:ch20-mode-averaging}). A deterministic predictor scored by ADE is
rewarded for exactly this, and a planner that inflates the mean by a
Gaussian ellipse blocks the pillar and nothing else. Where forks occur,
predict a mixture or a set of samples, and hand the planner every mode whose
weight is not negligible.
\end{pitfall}

\section[Where this fits in the drone system]{Where this fits in the drone system: predictions as constraints}
\label{sec:ch20-drone}

The three consumers of a prediction use the same quantity, derived from the
time-indexed occupancy region of \cref{eq:ch20-ellipse}: at step $h$, a disk
of radius
\begin{equation}
  d^{\mathrm{safe}}_h = r_A + r_B + k_p\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(\mat{\Sigma}_h)}
  \label{eq:ch20-inflated}
\end{equation}
around the predicted mean, which contains the intruder (radius $r_B$) with
probability at least $p$ when the ellipse is calibrated, because the disk of
radius $k_p\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}}$ contains the $p$-ellipse, and which adds the
own drone's radius $r_A$ as in the Minkowski sum of
\cref{def:ch02-minkowski-sum}. \code{inflated\_radius} and
\code{occupancy\_cells} compute the radius and the cells inside the ellipse;
\cref{fig:ch20-integration} shows the three interfaces side by side.

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \inputfigure{ch20/integration}
  \caption{One prediction, three consumers. (a) \orca inflates the intruder's
  disk to the radius $d^{\mathrm{safe}}_h$ of \cref{eq:ch20-inflated} at the
  step $h$ that matches the time to collision. (b) \dstarlite raises the cost
  of every cell inside $\mathcal{E}_h(p)$ in time layer $h$ of the space-time
  graph. (c) The MPC keeps its planned position $\pos_{A,h}$ at least
  $d^{\mathrm{safe}}_h$ from the predicted mean. The same sequence of ellipses
  is read three ways --- one radius, a set of cells, a per-step margin --- so
  a predictor that reports a covariance serves all three layers unchanged.}
  \label{fig:ch20-integration}
\end{figure}

\begin{dronebox}
\textbf{Receives.} From the tracker of \cref{ch:ch18} (or the nonlinear
filters of \cref{ch:ch19}) the filtered history of every unknown drone; the
planned positions of the swarm members come from the global planner, are
known exactly and need no prediction.

\textbf{Delivers.} For every unknown drone and every step $h$ of the
horizon, a mean $\hat{\pos}_{T+h}$ and a covariance $\mat{\Sigma}_h$, or a set
of samples, calibrated on validation data and recomputed at every cycle from
the newest observation.

\textbf{Local safety layer} (\cref{ch:ch13}, \cref{ch:ch14}). \orca and DWA
work with disks. The intruder enters as a moving disk whose radius is inflated
by \cref{eq:ch20-inflated} at the step $h$ that matches the time-to-collision
horizon of \cref{ch:ch12}, and whose velocity is the predicted displacement
per $\dt$ rather than the last measured one, so that a turn already in
progress is taken into account. The prediction also decides \emph{whether} the
local layer acts: the trigger of \cref{ch:ch24} fires when the predicted
region intersects the own path within the safety horizon.

\textbf{Replanning layer} (\cref{ch:ch05}). Every cell inside
$\mathcal{E}_h(p)$ gets a high traversal cost at time layer $h$ of the
space-time graph of \cref{ch:ch04}, or, in a static graph, the union over the
horizon does. The prediction is recomputed every cycle, so the costs change
every cycle: exactly the situation \dstarlite is built for.

\textbf{Control layer} (\cref{ch:ch21}). The MPC plans positions $\pos_{A,h}$
over its own horizon; the constraint $\norm{\pos_{A,h} - \hat{\pos}_{T+h}} \ge
d^{\mathrm{safe}}_h$ with $p = 1 - \delta$ in \cref{eq:ch20-inflated} enforces a collision
probability of at most $\delta$ per step under a Gaussian prediction, the
chance constraint of Zhu and Alonso-Mora~\cite{zhu2019chance}.

\textbf{Study plan.} Week~10 of \cref{ch:appA}. Prediction-aware constraints that carry the uncertainty rather than the mean
alone are one of the open research directions of \cref{ch:ch24}.
\end{dronebox}

\section{Summary}
\begin{summary}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Trajectory prediction maps the last $T_{\mathrm{obs}}$ positions to the next $T_{\mathrm{pred}}$; ADE averages the displacement error over the horizon, FDE takes the last one, $\mathrm{minADE}_K$ scores the best of $K$ samples and is not comparable to a deterministic ADE. Report curves per horizon and per subset, split by scene, and tune the baselines.
  \item Constant velocity is exact on straight flight and its only error is amplified noise, $\E[e_h^2] = 2\sigma^2[(1 + h/k)^2 + (h/k)^2]$; the Kalman extrapolation is its optimal linear version with a growing ellipse; constant acceleration overshoots on turns.
  \item The LSTM cell keeps a gated memory $\vect{c}_t = \vect{f}_t \odot \vect{c}_{t-1} + \vect{i}_t \odot \tilde{\vect{c}}_t$ whose direct gradient path is $\diag(\vect{f}_t)$; an encoder-decoder pair of cells predicts displacements in the agent frame one step at a time, with a Gaussian read-out trained by the negative log-likelihood, Adam and early stopping, in the mode in which it is used.
  \item Social pooling sums the neighbors' previous hidden states per grid cell; attention $\operatorname{softmax}(\mat{Q}\mat{K}\T/\sqrt{d_k})\mat{V}$ generalizes it to learned relevance over time steps and over agents at $\Theta(n^2 d)$ per layer.
  \item Squared-error training converges to the conditional mean, which averages the modes; mixtures, samplers and ensembles predict several futures, and a calibrated ellipse per step turns any of them into the inflated radius, cost region or chance constraint that the planners consume.
  \item In the experiment the LSTM beats the tuned baselines by $28\,\%$ overall, halves the error on turns and removes a third of it on visible maneuvers, loses on straight flight beyond $1.4$~s, ties on hidden maneuvers, fails on absolute coordinates or under teacher forcing alone, and its ellipses cover $54\,\%$ instead of $95\,\%$ at $2.4$~s. A one-layer Transformer trained on the same data matches it and does not beat it.
\end{itemize}
\end{summary}

\paragraph{Further reading.}
The LSTM cell is due to Hochreiter and Schmidhuber~\cite{hochreiter1997lstm},
the forget gate to Gers, Schmidhuber and Cummins~\cite{gers2000learning} and
backpropagation through time to Werbos~\cite{werbos1990bptt}; Goodfellow,
Bengio and Courville~\cite{goodfellow2016deep} give the textbook treatment of
recurrent networks, attention, mixture density outputs and
Adam~\cite{kingma2015adam}. Social LSTM~\cite{alahi2016social}, Social
GAN~\cite{gupta2018social} and Trajectron++~\cite{salzmann2020trajectron} are
the three papers to read on interaction-aware and multimodal prediction; the
Transformer is \textcite{vaswani2017attention} and its first use for
trajectories \textcite{giuliari2021transformer}. Rudenko et
al.~\cite{rudenko2020human} organize the field, Sch{\"o}ller et
al.~\cite{schoeller2020constant} made the community tune its
constant-velocity baselines, and the ETH~\cite{pellegrini2009you} and
UCY~\cite{lerner2007crowds} recordings are the standard benchmarks; mixture
density networks are from \textcite{bishop1994mixture}, deep ensembles from
\textcite{lakshminarayanan2017ensembles} and scheduled sampling from
\textcite{bengio2015scheduled}.

\section{Exercises}
\label{sec:ch20-exercises}

\begin{exercise}[\difficulty{1}]
\label{exr:ch20-metrics-hand}
A predictor outputs $\hat{\pos}_{T+1} = (1, 0)$, $\hat{\pos}_{T+2} = (2, 0)$,
$\hat{\pos}_{T+3} = (3, 0)$ while the truth is $(1, 0.1)$, $(2, 0.5)$,
$(3, 1.2)$. Compute the ADE and the FDE. A second sample of the same
predictor is $(1, 0.2)$, $(2, 0.4)$, $(3, 0.8)$; compute $\mathrm{minADE}_2$
and $\mathrm{minFDE}_2$ and explain why they cannot exceed the ADE and FDE of
the first sample.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[\difficulty{1}]
\label{exr:ch20-cv-accel}
A drone decelerates at $\norm{\acc} = 2$~m/s$^2$. Using
\cref{thm:ch20-cv-error}, compute the FDE and the ADE of the
constant-velocity prediction for $H = 12$ and $\dt = 0.2$~s. At which horizon
step does the error first exceed the $0.5$~m safety margin of a small drone?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[\difficulty{2}]
\label{exr:ch20-cv-noise}
Prove \cref{thm:ch20-cv-noise} for a velocity estimated by a least-squares
line fit through the last $k + 1$ observations instead of the endpoint
difference, and compare the two at $h = 12$, $k = 7$. Which $k \in \set{1,
\dots, 7}$ minimizes the endpoint-difference error at $h = 12$, and why is
the answer different on a turn?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[\difficulty{2}]
\label{exr:ch20-cell-hand}
Run one more step of the cell of \cref{ex:ch20-cell} with the input
$\vect{x}_{t+1} = (-1.0, 0.5)$ and the states $\vect{h}_t$, $\vect{c}_t$ of
\cref{tab:ch20-cell}. Which unit forgets most, and which gate would have to
change for unit~2 to keep its memory unchanged? Check your numbers with
\code{LSTMCell.forward}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[\difficulty{2}]
\label{exr:ch20-pe}
Show that for every offset $\delta$ there is a matrix $\mat{M}_\delta$,
independent of $t$, with $\mathrm{PE}(t + \delta) = \mat{M}_\delta\,\mathrm{PE}(t)$
for the encoding of \cref{eq:ch20-pe}. (Hint: treat each pair
$(\sin\theta_i t, \cos\theta_i t)$ separately.) Why does this let an
attention head learn ``attend to the step two before me''?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[\difficulty{2}]
\label{exr:ch20-attention-complexity}
Derive the time and memory complexity of scaled dot-product attention over
$n$ tokens of dimension $d$, of multi-head attention with $n_h$ heads, and of
attention over all $N$ agents and $T$ time steps of a scene. Compare with an
LSTM per agent with $D$ hidden units, and find the scene size $(N, T)$ at which
attention with $d = 64$ costs more than LSTMs with $D = 64$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[\difficulty{2}]
\label{exr:ch20-conditional-mean}
At a T-junction a drone turns left with probability $\pi$ and right
otherwise; the two futures end at $(-2, 4)$ and $(2, 4)$ relative to the
junction. Give the prediction that minimizes the expected squared final
error and its expected FDE as functions of $\pi$; compute both for
$\pi = \tfrac12$ and $\pi = 0.9$. What does a Gaussian read-out trained with
\cref{eq:ch20-nll} report as its $\sigma$ in the $x$ direction, and what is
the $\mathrm{minFDE}_2$ of a predictor that outputs both end points?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[\difficulty{1}]
\label{exr:ch20-protocol}
A report states: ``We cut all recordings into windows of $20$ steps with a
stride of $1$, shuffled them, used $80\,\%$ for training and $20\,\%$ for
testing, normalized all positions by the mean and standard deviation of the
data set, and obtained an ADE of $0.31$~m against $0.58$~m for constant
velocity (velocity from the last two positions) and a $\mathrm{minADE}_{20}$
of $0.12$~m.'' List every violation of the protocol of
\cref{sec:ch20-problem} and say, for each, in which direction it biases the
comparison.
\end{exercise}
